\begin{abstract}
\normalsize
Many real-world decisions rely on external data collection and modeling: consultants provide clients scenarios, fund managers present models of asset returns, and AI chatbots offer recommendations based on internal data. I study how model simplification in these settings distorts not just how data are interpreted but also which data are collected. A sender collects data and estimates a model that helps a receiver make a choice. The sender's model is simple in that it artificially restricts the curvature of the relationship between choices and outcomes: a ``naive'' model understates curvature and neglects tradeoffs between costs and benefits, while a ``cautious'' model overstates curvature and exaggerates them. I show that estimating simple models creates strategic incentives to collect data poorly suited to the receiver's decision. Senders who estimate naive models sample data about an excessively large range of choices, while those who estimate cautious models sample locally around a single extreme choice. With multiple variables, senders sample data that maximize collinearity across variables. These distortions make model simplicity strategically valuable: senders who estimate accurate models benefit when receivers misperceive their models as naive or cautious. In equilibrium, accurate senders surround themselves with simple senders who polarize into two groups: those who estimate maximally naive and maximally cautious models. In the limit, these correspond to linear regressions and ``worst-case scenario'' models, respectively.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Consider a policymaker tasked with designing a carbon tax.
She understands the nonlinear tradeoff between benefits and costs: low taxes do not curb emissions, while high taxes generate costs.
The optimal tax lies between these extremes, but she does not know where.
So, she hires a consultant, who collects data and estimates a \emph{model} that predicts the effects of different taxes.
But while his model is valuable, it presents three frictions.
First, it may be too simple.
Suppose the consultant estimates a linear regression of welfare on taxes.
This implies the optimal tax is either very low or high, \emph{neglecting} the tradeoff between benefits and costs.
Or, he might estimate a ``worst-case scenario'' that \emph{exaggerates} the tradeoff by always assuming high costs.
In either case, the policymaker believes the model is simplistic, and thus tries to infer the underlying data.
This presents a second friction: she cannot observe that data, which may be proprietary or reflect expertise, but which also may be more informative about some taxes than others.
Third, the consultant may financially benefit from making her optimistic about how much she can improve welfare.

This example reflects a broader phenomenon: decisionmakers rely on others to model the world for them.
CEOs hire consultants to guide firm restructuring, investors hire fund managers to model  returns, voters use political narratives to understand laws, and, increasingly, any of these decisionmakers may utilize an AI chatbot.
A growing literature documents that these models are often \emph{simple} by design or only accurate in some cases.
Financial professionals' ``rules of thumb'' \citep{10.1257/aer.107.4.967, bastianello2024mental} and managers' simplified models of price-setting leave profits on the table \citep{dellavigna2019uniform, han2024minds}.
Simultaneously, their data may be opaque: experts choose which data to collect \citep{di2021strategic}, and AI chatbots may excessively lean on unobservable training data \citep{jin2024can}.
Yet these simple models and narratives drive real choices: they may generate optimistic hiring \citep{flynn2025contagious}, political polarization \citep{levy2022misspecified, herrera2026alternative}, or media engagement \citep{eliaz2026news}.
How do \emph{model simplicity and strategic data collection} shape what decisionmakers learn?

This paper studies a theoretical setting where a receiver (she) hires a sender (he) to help her make a choice.
The sender gathers data and estimates a \emph{model} mapping choices to outcomes.
His models are \emph{simple} in that they restrict the curvature of the relationship between choices and outcomes to a narrow range of values, rather than letting the data fully determine it.
Simple models either \emph{neglect} tradeoffs and understate curvature (such as linear regressions) or \emph{exaggerate} them and overstate curvature (such as worst-case scenarios).
I show that a sender who only estimates simple models can strategically sample the data to make the receiver optimistic, since the receiver entertains other models that could have produced those data.
If he neglects tradeoffs, he samples data about an excessively wide range of choices.
If he exaggerates them, he samples locally around an extreme choice.
Then, I show that a sender who estimates an accurate model gains when the receiver mistakes it for a simple one.
Accurate senders thus benefit from surrounding themselves with simple ones.
These simple senders \emph{polarize} into two groups: ones who fix their models' curvature near the lowest value the receiver finds plausible, and ones who fix it near the highest.
In the limit, these correspond to linear regressions and worst-case scenarios.

\paragraph{Model Overview}
A receiver --- such as a policymaker, CEO, investor, or voter --- must choose an action $x$ to maximize an outcome $y$ --- such as profits or the value of a public good.
She believes that the relationship between $y$ and $x$ is given by a set of data-generating-processes (DGPs) that capture a tradeoff between a linear benefit and a convex cost.
The magnitude of the convex cost is given by a parameter $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$, which represents the \emph{curvature} of tradeoffs.

The receiver hires a sender to acquire information for her.
The sender chooses a distribution of $x$ values to sample from.
He then observes the corresponding $y$ values, producing data $(y,x)$.
His sampling choice is unobserved by the receiver.
The sender fits a model to the data.
He considers similar models to the receiver, but may be misspecified and treat tradeoffs in a simplified way.
I consider two types of senders.
The first is a sender who estimates a ``naive'' model.
These models \emph{neglect} tradeoffs the receiver considers by only fitting models that assume low values of curvature $\gamma$; when $\gamma=0$, the model is a linear regression of $y$ on $x$.
The second type of sender estimates a ``cautious'' model that \emph{exaggerates} tradeoffs by only assuming high values of $\gamma$.

Accordingly, the receiver is Bayesian: she observes the sender's model, infers how he collected data, and updates over the set of models she considers. 
The sender's objective is to make the receiver optimistic, i.e. to make her believe the maximum value of $y$ she can expect to achieve is as \emph{high as possible} --- because he would like to maximize her engagement with his services or receives a cut of her expected profits.
He knows that the receiver might entertain additional models that will affect her choice, in turn shaping his initial data sampling decision.


\paragraph{Receiver Learning and Sender Sampling}
The first result is that receiver learning is shaped by the interaction of the sender's model simplicity with strategic data collection.
The sender provides a model, shown in purple in the figure below.
However, the receiver may entertain alternative models.
These plausible models have \emph{steeper} or \emph{shallower curvature} than the original model.

I highlight two particular forms of data collection.
The first is when a sender positions himself as a ``specialist'' by estimating models ``locally'' on a small neighborhood of $x$ values.
In this case, the receiver's set of plausible models is tangent to the sender's model at a single point.
This is illustrated in panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:intro}.
At $x^*$ --- where the sender's model is maximized --- steeper models lie \emph{below} the sender's model and shallower models \emph{above}.

As the set of choices over which the sender samples widens, the receiver's additional models shift up or down relative to the sender's model, depending on their curvature.
The second form of data collection is when a sender estimates models across the largest possible range of $x$ values. I refer to this as ``generalist'' sampling.
In this case, the models the receiver entertains are \emph{secants} through the sender's original model. 
Steeper models lie \emph{above} the original model and shallower models lie \emph{below}.
This is shown in panel (b) of Figure \ref{fig:intro}.

This leads to a second set of results: when the sender presents a \emph{naive} model, he benefits from positioning himself as a generalist.
Formally, I show that this sender samples data centered in the middle of the receiver's choice space with an excessively high variance, which delivers the picture in panel (b) of Figure \ref{fig:intro}.
The intuition is that if the sender presents a naive model, he is neglecting tradeoffs the receiver finds important.
This means that the additional models the receiver entertains will be \emph{steeper} than the original model.
Steeper models lie above the sender's original model when the sender positions himself as a generalist.
Thus, the sender convinces the receiver she can achieve an even higher expected utility than his original model.

The opposite happens when the sender presents a \emph{cautious model}, where he benefits the most from positioning himself as a specialist.
In this case, any additional receiver models are shallower than the original model, which lie above it when he samples as a specialist.
Moreover, I show that this sender samples in the smallest possible neighborhood of either the receiver's maximal or minimal choice.
Panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:intro} shows a specialist sender sampling close to $x=0$.
Sampling thus has a ``bang-bang'' property: the sender either samples data precisely about a fringe receiver choice or across the widest possible range of choices, rather than an intermediate amount.


Third, I extend the baseline model to account for multidimensional choices and show that senders collect data that maximizes collinearity between variables.
If a sender neglects a (negative) \emph{interaction} effect between two variables, he samples data so they are as correlated as possible.
If he exaggerates interactions, he samples so that those variables are as anticorrelated as possible.
The intuition is that if the sender's model neglects interactions, the sample mean observed in his data is in part driven by a negative interaction of two variables that are highly correlated.
If the receiver were to unwind this correlation, the sample mean would be higher than what she initially observed, meaning her maximal expected utility would also be higher.
The opposite logic holds when the sender's model exaggerates interactions.
While the paper abstracts from finite samples, this means that senders who estimate simple models collect datasets that are heavily unbalanced, generating imprecise model parameter estimates.

Fourth, I show that a receiver's choices are more accurate under an intermediate degree of sampling, rather than specialist or generalist sampling. 
Thus, conditional on model simplification, senders do not even sample data in a way that is second-best for the receiver, and include either precise data on choices at the fringe of the receiver's choice space (cautious specialists) or thick-tailed information from all across the choice space (naive generalists).
This low quality of data emerges precisely as a consequence of the sender's model simplification.

While most of the paper assumes the receiver's payoff $y$ is quadratic in $x$,  I also show that the results are robust to the receiver's payoffs being given by a linear benefit less a symmetric, convex loss around a point.
Although the model without symmetry can prove to be very intractable, I provide a regularity assumption that delivers an analogue of my result: a sender who estimates naive models gains more from being a generalist than a specialist, and one who estimates cautious models gains more from being a specialist than a generalist.



\paragraph{Gains from Simplification and Market for Models}
Fifth, I show that a sender who provides an accurate model gains from being mistaken as a sender who supplies simple models.
I consider a case where one sender always provides an accurate model of choices, and the other provides a simple model.
I show that the accurate sender gains from the existence of the simple one.
The reason is that, conditional on seeing a model, the receiver believes it is either an accurate model or a simple model.
In the latter case, the receiver considers models that deliver higher expected utility than the original, due to the combination of strategic sampling with tradeoff neglect/exaggeration.
If a sender who provides an accurate model can plausibly masquerade as one who provides a simple model, he thus makes the receiver more optimistic than he would initially; she considers both his original (true) model and ones that deliver higher expected utility.

Using these insights, I then ask what the \emph{market} for senders may look like for a receiver. This delivers a sixth and final result: whenever a receiver contracts the services of a sender, she will receive one of:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an accurate model mapping choices to outcomes;
\item a highly naive model, calibrated to a fixed value of $\gamma$ close to $0$;
\item or a highly cautious model, calibrated to a fixed value of $\gamma$ close to $\overline{\gamma}$.	
\end{enumerate}
To obtain this result, I consider the problem of an accurate sender who always estimates an accurate model of the receiver's choices without simplifying curvature.
This accurate sender can surround himself with other senders who may estimate simpler models.
For example, a consultant might choose to hire other consultants at his organization who think about tradeoffs in simpler ways; a politician may be able to propagate simple narratives in the media; or an AI chatbot may deploy simpler models alongside complex ones.
In this sense, I allow the accurate sender to choose both the \emph{degree} of simplicity that other senders possess (what models they estimate) as well as their \emph{distribution} in the population (how many of these senders there are).
Thus, when the receiver contracts the services of a sender, she must infer whether the model came from the accurate or simple sender.

I show that, in equilibrium, the accurate sender will always choose to surround himself with simple senders who never estimate accurate models, and instead estimate models \emph{pre-calibrated} to a fixed value of $\gamma$.
For each value of $\gamma$, the accurate sender hires a simple sender who can \emph{only} model tradeoffs with that value of curvature.
That simple sender then strategically samples data (as a generalist or specialist) so that additional receiver models always lie above the original model.
A consultant, for instance, may calibrate a model using predetermined elasticities, rather than estimating one organically on new data.


Then, I show that the \emph{distribution} of these simple sender types is associated with \emph{polarization} in how they consider tradeoffs.
I show that the accurate sender always surrounds himself with few (or no) simple senders who model tradeoffs at intermediate values of $\gamma$.
Instead, he mostly hires senders who estimate models that are (calibrated to be) very cautious (with fixed $\gamma$ close to $\overline{\gamma}$) or very naive (fixed $\gamma$ close to $0$).
These senders not only provide pre-calibrated models, but they tend to be either excessively naive or cautious.
%I show that as the receiver believes she is more and more likely to see the true model, this polarization increases.
In limiting cases, simple senders either \emph{neglect all tradeoffs} and estimate \emph{linear regression models}, or \emph{exaggerate all tradeoffs} and estimate a worst-case model, i.e. those that assume the curvature of tradeoffs is fixed at its maximal value $\overline{\gamma}$.

\paragraph{Literature}\label{section:literature}
This paper broadly adds to our understanding of how we learn from externally-provided models and data, and how incentives to make us optimistic distort the design of information through strategic data collection and model simplification.
Its key contribution is to characterize what \emph{sorts} of data model senders may strategically sample, how incentives to strategically sample data are informed by the simplicity of their models' curvature, and how the interaction of both can generate gains from systematically pursuing highly simplistic data-generating-processes.

Many of the examples in the paper concern \emph{delegation} to experts.
There is a long line of literature --- particularly in organizations and finance --- studying this process, including mistrust in experts \citep{cheng2022distrust}, uncertainty about expertise \citep{kishishita2020not}, and its interaction with persuasion \citep{kolotilin2025persuasion}.
The key contribution of this paper is to study how delegation and communication operate when an expert's recommendation comes in the form of a \emph{model} organizing endogenously collected data.
The most closely related paper in this literature is \citet{li2018delegation}, who study delegation when a portfolio manager provides a model of returns to a client.

The present paper investigates a sender who first chooses a data collection rule and then provides the best-fitting model to that data.
While there are many papers that study learning from models, much of this literature takes as given the past history of data that a model is fit on --- whether that data is exogenous or endogenously generated by a principal's or agent's choices \citep{eliaz2020model, schwartzstein2021using, levy2022misspecified}.
In my setting, I show that a sender's desire to make the receiver optimistic coupled with model simplicity creates a tractable characterization of data sampling: the sender either locally samples data in the smallest possible neighborhood of a single receiver choice, or as distantly as possible across a wide range of receiver choices.
My assumption that the sender first chooses a data collection rule contrasts with other papers where data is endogenously generated either through follow-on evidence generation \citep{dai2026bayesian}, selective disclosure \citep{felgenhauer2014strategic}, nonrandom sampling \citep{di2021strategic}, or sequential data acquisition with endogenous stopping \citep{henry2019research}.
 
The use of strategic \emph{sampling} in the present paper in turn interacts with a sender's model simplicity to make the receiver believe her expected utility is \emph{higher} than under the model provided by the sender.
This channel through which a receiver can be made more optimistic is thus related to model simplicity and data sampling, as opposed to departures from a status quo or the inclusion of confounding variables, such as in \citet{eliaz2020model}.
This also distinguishes the paper from work where a receiver selects between different models with different sample sizes and more or less complex models \citep{montiel2022competing}.

Model fit also plays little role in driving the present paper's results on gains from simplicity or strategic sampling; this is in contrast to papers that investigate gains from model-based persuasion that involve trading off model fit and belief manipulability --- such as \citet{schwartzstein2021using}, \citet{aina2023tailored}, \citet{barron2024narrative}, and \citet{aina2025weighting}.
However, the present paper does show that senders who make receivers more optimistic often create greater \emph{forecast} error for a receiver, suggesting that receiver optimism may come at the cost of uncertainty over models.
The particular angle the present paper takes is closely related to omitted variable bias --- in that a sender cannot (or refuses to) fully model a tradeoff in the way a receiver would like.
This connects the paper to work like \citet{levy2022misspecified}, \citet{gleyze2022value}, and \citet{eliaz2025false} --- who show that model misspecification may interact with competitive pressures or uncertainty about data estimation to generate a range of outcomes from improved communication and engagement to political cycles and the prevalence of false models. 

A key finding of this paper is that senders may generally benefit from providing \emph{simpler} models to receivers.
The idea that senders can benefit from simple models is also captured by \citet{perez2012complicating}, \citet{bloedel2018persuasion}, \citet{lipnowski2020attention}, \citet{eliaz2021strategic}, and \citet{burkovskaya2026causal}. 
While many of these papers are driven by behavioral assumptions like bounded rationality or rational inattention, the findings in this paper are related to sampling and simplicity: given a sender's model, a receiver will entertain additional models.
The set of these additional models is determined by the \emph{simplicity} of the sender's model, and the expected utility they generate for the receiver increases or decreases with the spread of the sender's data sampling.
In particular, the present paper shows that a sender informed of the true model of the world can benefit by making the receiver think his model was estimated by a sender who had an artificially simple model and strategically sampled.
The most closely related paper is \citet{gleyze2022value}, who show that an informed sender can often gain by delegating model provision to a (potentially misspecified) sender.

The present paper is also related to how gains from simplicity operate when there is competition among multiple senders for a receiver's business, which relates it to work such as \citet{dasaratha2025markets}.
Mathematically, senders in the present paper differ in their ability to \emph{model tradeoffs}, which are related to their ability to estimate model curvature on predetermined \emph{subsets} of a parameter space.
This characterization of commitment --- as well as the problem of the ``sender-type designer'' --- is thus conceptually related to the characterization in \citet{bizzotto2026limits} of the limits of commitment in quantity-price games.

Finally, because of its reliance on models as information structures, the present paper also adds to a longer literature on (Bayesian) persuasion \citep{kamenica2011bayesian}, as well as persuasion and competition \citep{gentzkow2017bayesian,wang2013bayesian}.
In particular, receivers in the present paper are uncertain as to the source of data or the broader class of models a sender's model was obtained from.
This connects the paper to models of persuasion and cheap talk with \emph{uncertainty} about information structures \citep{huang2024learning,min2026information} and ambiguity aversion \citep{colo2024communicating}.
%Conceptually, the insight that some senders' gains from providing simple models can be eroded by sequential information acquisition is similar to that in \citet{matyskova2023bayesian}.



\section{Model Setup}\label{section:setup}
I begin by laying out a game between a single receiver $R$ (she) and a single sender $S$ (he). $R$ decides whether she would like to contract the services of a sender $S$.
$S$ collects data and provides $R$ a model describing that data.
$R$ utilizes the information provided in $S$'s model to make a decision.
%We abstract from prices and competition, but return to study these questions in Section \ref{section:competition}.

To help motivate the model further, we will also continue a running example from the introduction. $R$ is a policymaker tasked with designing a carbon tax, and $S$ is a consultant.


\subsection{Receiver and Models}
$R$ faces a set of choices $X=[\xlow,\overline{x}] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ for $\overline{x}>\xlow$. Each of these choices maps to an outcome $y \in \mathbb{R}$.
Ex-ante, $R$ does not know the relationship between $y$ and $x$. However, she believes that the value of $y$ given $x$ can be described by a data-generating process $y = f_R(x)$ for a function $f_R : X \to \mathbb{R}$.
We refer to $f_R$ as a \textbf{model}.
We denote $F_R$ as the set of \emph{models} $f_R$ that $R$ entertains.
%$F_R$ is closed and convex.
I make the following assumptions on $F_R$.
\begin{assumption}
	For each $f_R \in F_R$, we have
	\begin{align*}
	f_R(x) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x - \gamma x^2,	
	\end{align*}
	where $\alpha_0 \sim P_0^R$ on $\mathbb{R}$, $\alpha_1 \sim P_1^R$ on $\mathbb{R}$, and $\gamma \sim P_\gamma^R$ on $[0,\overline{\gamma}] :=\Gamma$ for continuous priors $p_0^R,p_1^R,p_\gamma^R$.
	$p_0^R$ is flat on $\mathbb{R}$. and $p_1^R$ is uniform on a nondegenerate interval $[\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}]$. 
	\label{assquad}
\end{assumption}
Assumption \ref{assquad} says that each of $R$'s functions is concave, and can in particular be expressed as an affine function less a convex, quadratic loss.
\footnote{I assume a quadratic form for tractability but show that the central intuitions generalize in Section \ref{section:general}.}
The prior over each of the three components is independent.
The prior over the set of constant terms is flat, i.e. $R$'s true uncertainty is over the curvature of $f_R$.
Let $q$ denote $R$'s belief over the set of models, i.e. her (joint) belief over these three parameters.
Given a belief $q$ over models, $R$'s expected utility is given by:
\begin{align*}
U_q(x) = \mathbb{E}_{q}[f_R(x)].
\end{align*}
Her goal is to choose $x$ to maximize $U_q(x)$.

In our running example, $x$ represents a carbon tax rate the policymaker can implement.
$y$ represents the welfare of a representative household or voter, whose interests she represents. 
Higher taxes reduce emissions and improve health outcomes, but may also increase energy prices and reduce private sector activity, which may further affect households differently based on their income.
Thus, the single choice of a tax rate may have different consequences for welfare, depending on the aggregate of these different effects.
The policymaker would like to balance these effects and choose the tax rate that maximizes expected welfare.

However, there are many other interpretations of the receiver and her preferences.
If $R$ is an investor, she cares about how much money she can make by investing in a choice $x$.
If $R$ is a CEO restructuring her firm via layoffs, she cares about her decision's impact on profits.
If $R$ is a voter, she cares about the impact of a policy $x$ on her wellbeing.
I utilize these simple preferences for higher $y$ in the baseline model, but Section \ref{section:forecast} extends them to evaluate settings where $R$ also values \emph{forecast accuracy}, and thus knowing the correct model of the world.


\subsection{Sender and Data}
The receiver $R$ contracts a sender $S$ to provide her with information about the true data-generating process.
This sender should be thought of as a \emph{consultant} or \emph{contractor} who provides domain expertise and access to data.
Of particular interest is also the case where $S$ is an AI program, such as a large language model (LLM) that $R$ can interface with.

The sender may consider different models than the receiver; which models he considers is determined by a private type.
The order of events is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Nature draws $S$'s type and the receiver's true model $f_R$.
\item $S$ collects data.
\item $S$ provides a model to $R$ that fits the data he collected. $R$ updates her beliefs about the true data-generating process based on her beliefs about $S$'s type and the model.
\item $R$ makes a decision $x^*$. $S$ receives utility. Uncertainty resolves, and $R$ receives her utility. 
\end{enumerate}
I now provide more detail about each of these components.

\paragraph{Sender's Type}
A sender's type affects the set of models --- and hence information --- he is capable of providing to $R$.  
The key is that $S$ is limited in how he can model the non-affine component of $f_R$, relative to how $R$ thinks about the problem.

The sender's type space is given by $\Theta \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Gamma)$, and is composed of subsets of $\Gamma = [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
I impose the restriction that each $\theta \in \Theta$ is a closed interval.
A type $\theta$ sender is able to provide $R$ a model in the set $F_{\theta}$, where
\begin{align*}
F_{\theta} := \{f_\theta \in F_R : f_\theta = \beta_0+\beta_1x - \delta x^2, \delta \in \theta, (\beta_0,\beta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \}.	
\end{align*}
Intuitively, $\theta$ represents the \emph{degree} to which a sender can model \emph{tradeoffs}.
The sender's type is drawn from a distribution $T$ over $\Theta$, which is known to $R$.

To start, I assume there are two sender types.
I make this assumption to highlight the key features of the model, but introduce a richer sender type space later on.
\begin{assumption}
$\Theta = \{\theta_1,\theta_2\}$ with $\theta_1 = \{0\}$
and $\theta_2 = \{\overline{\gamma}\}$.
\label{asstwotype}	
\end{assumption}
The two sender types in Assumption \ref{asstwotype} have the following interpretation.
The type $\theta_1$ sender estimates a model $y = \beta_0+\beta_1x$, without allowing for any nonlinear effects of $x$.
In this sense, the sender \textbf{neglects tradeoffs} and estimates a \textbf{naive model}: he runs a linear regression of $y$ on $x$ instead of considering a nonlinear relationship.
In our example, the policymaker may believe small carbon taxes generate meaningful reductions in pollution without adverse effects, but very high taxes lead to higher energy prices or lower business activity.
These nonlinear effects would be neglected if a consultant presented a linear regression of welfare on tax rates. 

The type $\theta_2$ sender is the opposite. This sender estimates models of the form $y = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\overline{\gamma}x^2$, where $\overline{\gamma}$ is fixed at its highest possible value.
We say this sender \textbf{exaggerates tradeoffs} and estimates a \textbf{cautious model} of the impact of $x$ on $y$.
For example, the consultant may always consider the ``worst case scenario'' effect that higher taxes can have on business sector activity, even though the receiver believes that worst case is very unlikely (i.e. $\gamma \leq \overline{\gamma}$).
I assume a type $\theta$ sender has a prior over $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ given by measures $\beta_0 \sim P_0^\theta$ and $\beta_1 \sim P_1^\theta$ with support on $\mathbb{R}$.
We assume that his prior over $\beta_1$ has support on $[\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta}]$ with $\underline{\beta}-\underline{\alpha}>0$ and $\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta}>0$ sufficiently large. \footnote{This is a regularity assumption that shuts down edge-cases of learning in equilibrium.}



\paragraph{Sender's Data}
Prior to choosing a model, $S$ chooses a data sampling strategy $\mu_\theta$, where $\mu_\theta$ is a distribution over $X$.
Intuitively, this means that $S$ is choosing a distribution of choices in $X$ to collect data on. 
$R$ does not observe $S$'s choice of $\mu_\theta$.
\begin{assumption}
$\mu_\theta$ is parametrized by $\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta) = x_\theta + \sigma_\theta\cdot \epsilon$,
where $\epsilon$ is mean-zero, symmetric, and $\Var(\epsilon)=1$.
$x_\theta \in X$ and $\sigma_\theta \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]$ for $\overline{\sigma}>\underline{\sigma}>0$ and $\underline{\sigma}$ arbitrarily small.
\label{assdist}
\end{assumption}
Assumption \ref{assdist} provides a convenient parametrization of $\mu_\theta$ as a distribution centered around a mean $x_\theta$. $\sigma$ controls the standard deviation of the distribution.
\footnote{The constraint that $\sigma>0$ is a normality assumption made to ensure learning is structured in equilibrium.}
$x_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta$ will denote the equilibrium choices of a type $\theta$ sender.

We introduce two definitions here.
We say a type $\theta$ sender is a \textbf{generalist} if $\sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma}$.
Intuitively, a type $\theta$ sender's model is estimated \emph{distantly} on as wide a range of $x$ values as possible.
We say a type $\theta$ sender is a \textbf{specialist} if $\sigma_\theta = \underline{\sigma}$.
That is, this sender estimates a model \emph{locally} on a small set of $x$ values.

In our example, when the consultant is first contracted by the policymaker, he will choose whether to position himself as a \emph{generalist} or \emph{specialist}. If he is a generalist, then the data he uses to fit his models is drawn from a wide range of carbon tax rates from many contexts.
If the consultant is a \emph{specialist}, he will utilize information about a small range of carbon tax rates.
 
Given this sampling strategy, $S$ directly observes the joint distribution of $(y,x)$, subject to observational noise.
Formally, if the true data-generating process is $f_R^*$, he observes a joint distribution $D(y,x)$, where the marginal distribution of $x$ is given by $D_x = \mu_\theta$ and the conditional distribution of $y$ given $x$ is given by $D_y(y|x) = f_R^*(x)+\eta$, where
$\eta$ is a mean-zero noise term with unknown variance.
\footnote{I allow $S$ to observe the distribution of data to shut down the role of idiosyncratic data draws in shaping posterior beliefs. This is equivalent to $S$ observing an arbitrarily large (or infinite) number of i.i.d. draws from the true data-generating process.}

\paragraph{Sender's Information}
With the sender's type and sampling strategy in hand, we define the model $S$ provides to $R$.
%We say that a model $f_S$ \textbf{fits the data} with respect to $\theta$ and a data-sampling strategy $\mu_\theta$ if:
%\begin{align*}
%\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[f_S(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{D(\mu_\theta)}\big[y\big].
%\end{align*}
%Our key assumption is that $S$ must provide a model $f_S$ that fits the data.
%Additionally, 
We say that a model $f_S$ \textbf{best-fits the data} with respect to $\theta$ and a data-sampling strategy $\mu_\theta$ if:
\begin{align*}
f_S= \arg\min_{f \in F_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{D(\mu_\theta)}\big[\big(y-f(x)\big)^2\big].
\end{align*}
For example, when we have $\theta_1 = \{0\}$, $f_S$ represents the \emph{best-fitting linear model} to the data.\footnote{The existence of a best-fitting model is given by the Hilbert Projection Theorem.}

I assume $S$ always provides $R$ the best-fitting model to the data. In our running example, the interpretation is that the consultant makes an honest effort to provide $R$ quality information about tax rates, within the constraints of how he approaches the problem.
$S$ may use a well-known algorithm to fit his model (such as a linear regression).\footnote{The policymaker could at least partially verify this through a ``replication package.''}
This allows me to isolate how model \emph{simplicity} and strategic \emph{data collection} shape learning and sender behavior.


\paragraph{Sender's Preferences}

Suppose $S$ provides a model $f_S \in F_\theta$.
Let $x^*$ be the receiver's choice of $x \in X$ conditional on her posterior beliefs over models $q|f_S$.
The sender's utility conditional on $f_S$ is given by:
\begin{align*}
U_S^\theta(x^*) = \mathbb{E}_{q|f_S}[f_R(x^*)].
\end{align*}
Conditional on providing a model $f_S$, a type $\theta$ $S$ would like to maximize $R$'s posterior expected utility at her chosen action.

The interpretation of these preferences in our example is that the consultant would like to make the policymaker \emph{optimistic}.
He may receive a consulting bonus if she is more optimistic about the potential gains she can reap from the carbon tax policy.
Relatedly, she may be more likely to utilize the consultant's services in the future.
Beyond the current example, however, the preferences lend themselves to several interpretations.
\begin{itemize}
	\item  $S$ is a portfolio manager who receives a fixed share of $R$'s expected earnings. He cares about the expected profits $R$ can realize (and thus his payment).
	\item $S$ is a politician who provides information about $y$ to a median voter $R$. The higher the median voter thinks $y$ is, the more likely she is to turn out in an election. If re-elected, the politician will implement $R$'s preferred policy $x^*$.
	\item $S$ is an AI program that wants to maximize its engagement with its user base. The more optimistic users are, the more likely they will be to purchase a premium subscription.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Additional Comments}

In this framework, the receiver's learning is affected by two key features: the fact that she does not know if the sender is a \emph{generalist} or \emph{specialist}, and the fact that the sender's models may be \emph{simplified}.
Uncertainty about whether the sender is a generalist or specialist ultimately boils down to uncertainty about the sorts of data and information the sender has access to, which is a key friction that emerges when communicating with models.
Consider the three examples below.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The sender may have access to a proprietary dataset, which cannot be directly disclosed to the receiver. Companies like Nielsen, for instance, provide proprietary data on ``audience segments'' to help advertisers run targeted campaigns \citep{nielsen2024audiencesegments}.
\item The sender's model may also embed \emph{expertise}. The strategy literature suggests that firms often develop internal ways of approaching a problem based on analogous situations they have experienced in the past \citep{carroll2024strategy}. Accordingly, a consulting firm's recommendations may be informed by experience working on cases similar to each other (as a ``specialist'') or drawn from many different cases and past contexts (as a ``generalist''). 
\item The sender is an AI program. It is often difficult for users of these programs (or even their developers) to understand the mapping from internal data to recommendations \citep{zuccarelli2024blackbox}. In this sense, the mapping from the data to a model is a \emph{black box}.
\end{enumerate}
What matters is that $R$ knows $S$ has access to useful data, but must infer how that data may be strategically sampled when estimating a model.\footnote{
Accordingly, I will be interested in exactly \emph{what sorts of data} the sender will collect in equilibrium, and \emph{how} that data collection interacts with the simplicity of his models.}

Additionally, $R$ may also be unable (or unwilling) to expend organizational time and resources in collecting, understanding, and synthesizing complex data.
Instead, she delegates the collection of data and its synthesis into a model to the sender, expediting her decision-making process in the interest of convenience, but potentially at the cost of information quality.

This friction is related to model simplicity: $S$ may be unable to think about problems exactly the way $R$ would like.
With two types, $S$ either estimates a \emph{naive} linear model that \emph{totally} neglects tradeoffs, or a \emph{cautious} model that totally exaggerates them and assumes a worst-case scenario.
This assumption can be founded through a few different lenses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lack of sophistication}: the case where $S$ estimates a naive model is equivalent to one where $S$ estimates a \emph{linear regression} of $y$ on $x$. The sender may be able to estimate a ``line of best fit,'' but not understand more complicated model fitting techniques. Thus, he does one of two things. Either he continues to totally neglect nonlinear tradeoffs (because he cannot estimate them), or he overcompensates and assumes a worst-case scenario.
\item \textbf{Calibration}: Instead of estimating all parameters organically on data, $S$ may calibrate some model parameters to a fixed value, such as an elasticity from the literature. I discuss calibration later when discussing richer type spaces.
\item \textbf{Strategic Behavior}: the sender may actually be able to make the receiver \emph{more optimistic} if she thinks his model was a simplified model. While I take this assumption as a given, this will be a central result of the paper, and is developed in Section \ref{section:simple}.
\end{enumerate}
%The case where $S$ provides linear regression models is also of particular interest, given their common usage in the business world, economics, and data-driven social sciences.


Finally, I assume that $S$ must provide the best-fitting model to the data, rather than a model that simply ``fits the data.''
The interpretation is that $S$ makes an honest effort to provide $R$ the best model he can, given the set of data-generating processes he can model.
While it could be that $S$'s honesty emerges due to professional norms or unmodeled career concerns, it may also be that $S$ synthesizes data using methods or algorithms that are well-known in his field of expertise.
\footnote{Relatedly, $R$ could require $S$'s methodology to be partially ``replicable.''}
Nevertheless, even though $S$ provides a model of best-fit, he can still manipulate the set of models $R$ entertains through the way he initially samples data as well as his type $\theta$.

I focus largely on settings where the nonlinear component of $f_R$ is quadratic. While this is done for tractability, Section \ref{section:extensions} discusses the assumptions necessary to generalize the logic, where I also extend the model to account for multidimensional choices.

\section{Analysis}\label{section:learning}
This section first investigates the model with the two simple sender types provided above, where one sender provides the receiver a naive ``linear regression'' model that neglects tradeoffs, and the other a cautious ``worst case'' model that exaggerates tradeoffs.
I characterize the set of models that the receiver entertains in each case.
Using this insight, I show that senders who neglect tradeoffs position themselves as ``generalists,'' while those who exaggerate them position themselves as ``specialists.'' I show how this result emerges from the combination of senders' model simplicity with their ability to strategically sample data.

I then move towards a more complex sender type space to show how the results generalize when senders may only neglect or exaggerate some tradeoffs while correctly estimating others.
However, in either case, the sender's model is estimated on data that will ex-ante likely be \emph{irrelevant} to the receiver's eventual choice.
When a sender samples as a generalist, he samples in the center of the receiver's choice space, but includes lots of data about choices at the \emph{edge} of the receiver's choice space.
If the sender samples as a specialist, he only samples data in a small neighborhood on one of the edges of the receiver's choice space.


I then further generalize the baseline model with \emph{multidimensional choices}.
This delivers an additional prediction: senders may sample data to maimize \emph{collinearity} between different variables.
In particular, if a sender \emph{neglects} interaction effects between two variables, he samples data so that they are as \emph{correlated} as possible.
If he \emph{exaggerates} interaction effects, he samples data so that they are as \emph{anticorrelated} as possible.
Thus, although receivers correctly infer the sort of data that senders collect in equilibrium, that data may be \emph{low quality}, in that it suffers from a high degree of collinearity, and embeds information about choices that will (in expectation) be irrelevant.

\subsection{Two Types: Receiver's Learning and Behavior}
I begin by characterizing receiver learning as a function of data-sampling strategies and $S$'s type.
\paragraph{Receiver's Updating}
Suppose $S$ provided a model of the form $f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1 x-\delta x^2$ which best fits the data.
In our two-type example, we have either $\delta = 0$ (when $\theta = \theta_1$) or $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$ (when $\theta = \theta_2$).
We define $\phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$ as the set of functions that could rationalize the data for $R$, given $f_S$ best-fits with respect to $F_\theta$ and $\mu_\theta$:
\begin{align*}
\phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta) = \bigg\{f_R \in F_R : f_S =\arg\min_{f \in F_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[\big(f_R(x)-f(x)\big)^2\big] \bigg\}.	
\end{align*}
The receiver may entertain additional models beyond $f_S$. $\phi$ describes this set.
We necessarily have that for each $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_i} \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[\big(f_R(x)-f(x)\big)^2\big] = 0, 
\end{align*}
which yields the following moment conditions familiar from ordinary-least-squares (OLS):
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x + \delta x^2\big] =0
\label{eq:intercept}
\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta x^2 \big)\big] =0
\label{eq:slope}
\end{align}
Equation (\ref{eq:intercept}) is the moment condition with respect to the intercept, and states that the difference between $f_R$ and $f_S$ must average to zero.
We note that this is the same as the constraint that a model must ``fit the data.''
Equation (\ref{eq:slope}) can be combined with equation (\ref{eq:intercept}) to read that the covariance between $x$ and $f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x - \delta x^2$ is zero.
Both expectations are taken with respect to $\mu_\theta$, which is $S$'s sampling strategy.
The following proposition characterizes the set of functions that $R$ entertains given $f_S$, $\theta$, and $\mu_\theta$.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{reduction}
\label{prop:reduction}
Suppose $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$ best-fits the data and $\mu_\theta = \mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)$. Then, for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = f_S(x)+(\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big).
\end{align*}
\end{restatable}
The proof of this result is contained in the Appendix, and assumes the more general type space $\Theta$ introduced later in the section.

Consider the policymaker and consultant. The policymaker can see that the consultant's model is potentially simpler than she would like.
Thus, she considers the consultant's original model but also other models that could rationalize the tax-welfare data the consultant saw.
Mathematically, the set of models that $R$ considers, given a best-fitting model $f_S$, takes a simple form: they are all the sender's original model plus a constant, less a quadratic term centered at $x_\theta$.
In the case where $\theta_1 = \{0\}$, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x+\gamma\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)	
\end{align*}
for each $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
In the case where $\theta_2 = \{\overline{\gamma}\}$, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\overline{\gamma}x^2 + (\gamma-\overline{\gamma})\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)	
\end{align*}
for each $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
In both cases, the set of models $R$ considers involves a fixed ``affine component'' plus a constant and quadratic term, which may be positive or negative.
Moreover, all plausible $f_R$s have the same slope at $x_\theta$.

\subsection{Two Types: Sender's Data Sampling and Model Provision}
I use these insights to show how $S$ chooses the \emph{spread} of his data as a function of his type.
I defer the sender's choice of $x_\theta$ to the main statement of the result in Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
%We will use the following terminology to distinguish different types of sampling.
%\begin{itemize}
%\item \textbf{Local sampling}: $\sigma = \underline{\sigma}$. In this case, $\mu_\theta$ samples $x$ values in a small neighborhood of $x_\theta$, i.e. is collecting information ``locally relevant'' to $x_\theta$.
%\item \textbf{Distant sampling}: $\sigma = \overline{\sigma}$. In this case, $\mu_\theta$ samples $x$ values from as wide a range as possible $x_\theta$.

\begin{restatable}{proposition}{prop:sampling}
Fix $x_\theta$.
The type $\theta_1$ sender who estimates a naive model positions himself as a generalist.
The type $\theta_2$ sender who estimates a cautious model positions himself as a specialist.
\label{prop:sampling}
\end{restatable}
The proof of the result is a special case of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}, which is the version of the result for the more general type space.

Proposition \ref{prop:sampling} can be interpreted as follows.
If the sender estimates a naive model, the receiver will consider models with $\gamma \geq 0$, i.e. that have \emph{steeper curvature} than the original model.
This creates an incentive for him to position himself as a \emph{generalist}, i.e. to estimate his model on as wide a set of choices as possible.
However, if the sender estimates a cautious model, the receiver will consider models with $\gamma \leq \overline{\gamma}$, i.e. that are \emph{flatter curvature} than the original model.
This gives $S$ an incentive to position himself as a specialist who estimates the DGP locally in a neighborhood of $x_\theta$.

The intuition is driven by the interaction of the curvature of the receiver's additional models with the sender's method of \emph{data sampling}, and is 
shown visually in Figure \ref{fig:samplingintuition} below. 
The top panels (a) and (b) look at the case where $f_S$ is a naive model linear regression of $y$ on $x$.
The bottom panels (c) and (d) look at the case where $f_S$ is the worst-case model fitting the data.

In panel (a), a naive $S$ has positioned himself as a \emph{specialist}, and samples data locally in a neighborhood of $x_\theta$.
All the additional plausible models $f_R$ are tangent to $f_S$ at $x_\theta$.
In panel (a), all these alternative models have steeper curvature (i.e. a higher $\gamma$ value) than $f_S$, and lie below $f_S$ at the point of tangency.
This logic flips when looking at panel (b), where $S$ has positioned himself as a generalist.
Recall that the distribution of $x$ values when $\sigma = \overline{\sigma}$ is a mean-preserving spread of that when $\sigma=\underline{\sigma}$.
To rationalize the data, functions with higher $\gamma$ values that are ``more concave'' must shift up relative to shallower ones.
In panel (b), this means that the steeper $f_R$ models now lie \emph{above} $f_S$.

When he samples a specialist, all these additional models lie \emph{above} the original model.
When he samples as a generalist, these models lie \emph{below} the original model.

Thus:
\begin{itemize}
\item When $S$ presents naive models, he gains the most by positioning himself as a \emph{generalist}.
\item When $S$ presents cautious models, he gains the most by positioning himself as a \emph{specialist}.
\end{itemize}
The key driver of the result is that strategic sampling creates a \emph{gap} between $f_S$ and the other models the receiver entertains.
We denote this gap $G(f_S,\sigma_\theta)$:
\begin{align*}
G(f_S,\sigma_\theta) := 	\mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S}[y|x^*,\sigma_\theta]-f_S(x^*).
\end{align*}
This gap disproportionately makes the receiver more optimistic from naive models that \emph{neglect} tradeoffs than from cautious models that exaggerate them.
\begin{corollary}
$G(f_S,\sigma_\theta)$ changes as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $f_S$ is a naive model so that $\sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma}$, then as $\overline{\sigma}\to\infty$, $G(f_S,\sigma_\theta) \to \infty$.	
\item If $f_S$ is cautious so that $\sigma_\theta = \underline{\sigma}$, then as $\underline{\sigma}\to0$, $G(f_S,\sigma_\theta) \to(\overline{\gamma}-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S])(x^*-x_\theta)^2<\infty$.
\end{itemize}
\label{corr:gain}
\end{corollary}
The interpretation of this corollary is that a \emph{generalist} sender who presents naive models makes a receiver have ``rosier views'' about the maximal expected value of $y$ than the \emph{specialist} sender who presents cautious models, relative to the provided model $f_S$.

We finish this section with a brief commentary on the naive linear regression model.
The result on strategic sampling says that this sender always estimates models using data sampled from widely across the choice space.
However, regressions are most effective when thought of as \emph{local linear approximations}.
Thus, the result on sampling contrasts a sender's persuasive desire to fit a regression to ``distantly sampled data'' with the common perception that regressions are most effective when utilized as ``local'' linear approximations.

\subsection{Model with More than Two Types}
I now move to generalize the results from the previous section by considering a richer sender type space.
In the model with two types, senders either always assumed a naive linear model that completely neglected all tradeoffs, or a cautious worst-case model that exaggerated all of them.
In reality, senders and receivers alike may consider multiple tradeoffs in different ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item Senders could estimate \emph{some} tradeoffs and assume the worst-case or neglect others. In our example, the consultant may thoroughly estimate how carbon taxes affect energy prices, but assume a worst-case scenario for their effects on political backlash. 
\item Senders could \emph{calibrate} the value of a tradeoff at a fixed parameter, without estimating it on the real data. For example, the consultant may calibrate the effect of carbon taxes on energy prices using an elasticity from the literature, rather than organically estimating it on data.
\end{itemize}
Accordingly, I now place the following structure on type spaces.
\begin{assumption}
For each $\theta \in \Theta$, $\theta \subseteq \Gamma$ is a (potentially degenerate) closed interval. 
For each $\theta \neq \theta' \in \Theta$, $\theta \cap \theta' = \emptyset$.
\label{asstype}
\end{assumption}
Assumption \ref{asstype} states that each type corresponds to an interval within $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$. 
I assume type intervals do not intersect for expositional purposes.
The case where they do is of particular interest, and I study it in Section \ref{section:simple}.

We will denote $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$.
Accordingly, type spaces have the following interpretations.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\overline{\delta}_\theta < \overline{\gamma}$, then a type $\theta$ $S$ is \emph{neglecting at least some tradeoffs}. Intuitively, these senders do not allow for the case where $\gamma$ is close to $\overline{\gamma}$.
\item If $\underline{\delta}_\theta > 0$, then a type $\theta$ $S$ is \emph{exaggerating at least some tradeoffs}. These senders do not allow for the case where $\gamma$ is close to $0$.
\end{itemize}
Finally, if $\theta = \{\delta\}$ (i.e. $\theta$ is a degenerate interval), then a type $\theta$ sender \textbf{calibrates} his model to a fixed parameter.
Intuitively, he assumes $\gamma$ has a fixed, predetermined value, and does not actually estimate it using his data.

In addition to priors over $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, a type $\theta$ sender now has a prior over $\delta$ given by $\delta \sim P_\delta^\theta$ with support on $\theta \subseteq \Gamma$.
I make the following assumption on $P_\delta^\theta$.
\begin{assumption}
Suppose $\theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$.
Let $\partial \theta$ denote the boundary of $\theta$. Then, $P_\delta^\theta(\partial \theta-(\{0\}\cup\{\overline{\gamma}\}))>0$.
%Suppose $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$ is non-degenerate and $\theta \subsetneq \Gamma$.
%Consider the following two conditions:
%\begin{align}
%P_\delta^\theta(\{\underline{\delta}_\theta\})>0
%\tag{$P_\delta$-1}
%\\
%P_\delta^\theta(\{\overline{\delta}_\theta\})>0
%\tag{$P_\delta$-2}
%\end{align}
%If $\theta  \ni \overline{\gamma}$, then ($P_\delta$-1) must hold.
%If $\theta \ni 0$, then ($P_\delta$-2) must hold.
%Otherwise, either one of ($P_\delta$-1) or ($P_\delta$-2) must hold.
\label{assboundary}	
\end{assumption}
I impose Assumption \ref{assboundary} as a regularity condition; it is consistent with either $P_\delta$ being a discrete distribution, or $P_\delta$ being a continuous CDF over  $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$ whose mass is censored.
\footnote{At a mathematical level, a type $\theta$ $S$'s sampling decision will be determined by how he considers tradeoffs on the ``edge'' of the interval $\theta$.
This ``edge'' must occur with non-measure-zero probability to have an effect on $S$'s ex-ante expected utility.}
We denote by $P_{f_S}^\theta$ a type $\theta$ sender's prior over models $f_S$.

\paragraph{Learning with Interval Types}
The characterization of $\phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$ --- the set of models $R$ considers in addition to $f_S$ --- is the same as above.
However, we note that if $\theta$ is non-degenerate, we may also have a moment condition corresponding to $\delta$:
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[x^2\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta x^2 \big)\big]
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{align}
This equation may be zero or nonzero, depending on $S$'s type.
Accordingly, we introduce the following notation:
\begin{align*}
\Delta(f_S) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta]-\delta
%&\overline{\gamma}_\theta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma | \gamma \geq \overline{\delta}_\theta,\alpha_1-2\gamma x_R=\beta_1-2\delta x_R]
%\\
%&\underline{\gamma}_\theta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma | \gamma \leq \underline{\delta}_\theta,\alpha_1-2\gamma x_R=\beta_1-2\delta x_R].
%\\
%&\tilde{\gamma}_\theta
\end{align*}
Fixing $f_S$, $\Delta$ represents the \emph{difference} between $R$'s expectation of the quadratic parameter $\gamma$ and the sender's provided parameter $\delta$.
In particular, if $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, we necessarily have that (\ref{eq:gamma}) is equal to $0$, and that $f_S$ is the true model.
In this case, $\Delta=0$.
However, if $\delta \in \partial \theta$, then the receiver may entertain additional models.

The statement of Proposition \ref{prop:reduction} is identical in the case with interval types, and we note that the formal proof utilizes interval types.
However, it is useful to state the format of models the receiver considers in the following corollary.
\begin{corollary}
Consider a sender model $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$ that comes from a type $\theta$ sender. Then, the receiver's expected utility given $x$ is given as follows.
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S,\theta}[y|x]	 = f_S(x)+\Delta(f_S)\big(\sigma_\theta^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)
\end{align*}

\end{corollary}
This generalizes the intuitions in the two-type model.
If $\delta = \overline{\delta}_\theta<\overline{\gamma}$, then $\Delta(f_S)>0$: $R$ considers models that have steeper curvature than the one $S$ has provided because he has neglected some tradeoffs.
If $\delta = \underline{\delta}_\theta>0$, then $\Delta(f_S)<0$: $R$ considers models that have shallower curvature than the one $S$ has provided because he has exaggerated some tradeoffs.
In the special case where $\theta$ is a singleton, $R$ is completely uncertain as to the value of $\gamma$, and $\Delta(f_S)$ may be positive or negative.

\paragraph{Strategic Sampling with Interval Types}
We now formalize the following definitions.
We say a type $\theta$ sender estimates a \textbf{naive model} if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta(\Delta(f_S)) >0$. 
A type $\theta$ sender estimates a \textbf{cautious model} if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta(\Delta(f_S)) <0$. 
Concretely, if a sender estimates a naive model, he tends to \emph{neglect} tradeoffs on average, i.e. underestimate $\gamma$ relative what the receiver considers.
If he estimates a cautious model, he tends to \emph{exaggerate} tradeoffs on average, i.e. overestimate $\gamma$ relative to what the receiver considers.

This allows me to state my main result on strategic sender sampling, which generalizes the logic of Proposition \ref{prop:sampling}.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{sampling}
If $S$ estimates naive models, he positions himself as a generalist $(\sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma})$
and sets $x_\theta = \max\{\xlow,\min\big\{\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[x^*(f_S)],\overline{x}\big\}\big\}$, where $x^*(f_S)$ is the receiver's choice given $f_S$.
If $S$ estimates cautious models, he positions himself as a specialist $(\sigma_\theta = \underline{\sigma})$ and sets $x_\theta = \xlow$ or $\overline{x}$.
\label{thm:sampling}
\end{restatable}
Theorem \ref{thm:sampling} can be interpreted as follows.
If $\delta \in (\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta)$, then $R$ believes $S$'s model is correct.
However, if $\delta = \overline{\delta}_\theta$, $R$ will consider models with $\gamma \geq \overline{\delta}_\theta$.
Thus, as in the two-type case, this creates an incentive for him to sample from all across the choice space.

When $\delta = \underline{\delta}_\theta$, the opposite happens: $R$ considers models with $\gamma \leq \underline{\delta}_\theta$, i.e. ``flatter'' than those that $S$ provided.
Similarly to the two-type case, this gives him an incentive to only estimate models in a small neighborhood of $x_\theta$, i.e. to position himself as a ``specialist.''

Theorem \ref{thm:sampling} additionally shows what value $x_\theta$ a sender will choose.
The intuition is as follows.
When presenting a naive model, $S$ would like to ensure all the additional models that $R$ entertains are as far above $f_S$ as possible.
This gap is largest when $x_\theta$ is at the maximum of $f_S$, i.e. where $f_S'(x_\theta) = 0$.
Because $S$ does not know ex-ante where this maximum will be, he thus samples where he \emph{expects} $f_S$ to be the shallowest.

When $S$ estimates a cautious model, the logic is the opposite.
If $f_S$ is maximized at a point $x$, so are all other models tangent to $f_S$.
However, if all these other models are tangent at a point farther from $x$, the set of tangent models grows higher and higher above $f_S$. Thus, he samples at either $\xlow$ or $\overline{x}$, where he expects $f_S$ to be steepest.

\subsection{Multidimensional Choices}
While the baseline model considered a single choice variable $x$, I also consider what happens when choices are multidimensional.
For example, imagine that our policymaker can shape consumer welfare not just through a carbon tax on businesses but also a complementary clean energy subsidy.
I briefly show that many of the findings of the baseline model generalize.
However, I also show that when the sender samples data about multiple variables, he always has an incentive to introduce nearly perfect \emph{collinearity} between them.
In particular, if there are two choice variables --- $x_1$ and $x_2$ --- and the sender can choose how \emph{correlated} the two variables are when sampling the data, he will make them either as correlated as possible or as \emph{anticorrelated} as possible.
\paragraph{Multidimensional Models}
Suppose now that the receiver's choices are given by $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in X^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.
The models $f_R$ the receiver entertains are now as follows.
\begin{assumption}
	For each $f_R \in F_R$, we have
	\begin{align*}
	f_R(x) = \alpha_0 + \alpha^T x - x^T\gamma x,	
	\end{align*}
	where $\alpha_0 \sim P_0^R$, $\alpha  \sim P_1^R$ for measures on $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $\gamma \sim P_\gamma^R$ on a set $\Gamma$. $\Gamma$ is a compact, convex set of positive-semidefinite matrices such that $0 \in \Gamma$ and such that there exists $\gamma \in \Gamma$ with $\gamma_{ii}>0$ for all $i$.
	Additionally, the priors $P_0^R$ and $P_1^R$ are continuous and uniform over $\mathbb{R}$ and $[\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}]^n$.
%	Moreover:
%	\begin{itemize}
%	\item Let $\overline{\Gamma}$ be a compact set of $n \times n$ positive-semidefinite matrices such that $\overline{\gamma}_{ii} > 0$ for each $i \in \{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $\overline{\gamma} \in \overline{\Gamma}$.
%	\item Let $\Gamma = \{\gamma : \gamma = t\overline{\gamma}, t \in [0,1], \overline{\gamma} \in \overline{\Gamma}\}$. Then, $\gamma \sim P_\gamma^R$ on $\Gamma$.
%	\end{itemize}	
	\label{assmultimodel}
\end{assumption}
$f_R$ now \emph{generalizes} the idea of a quadratic data-generating process to a multivariate setting using a set of concave \emph{quadratic forms}.
This set of forms is given by a set of positive-semidefinite matrices $\Gamma$.
%generated by taking a set of ``extreme'' positive semidefinite matrices $\overline{\Gamma}$ and, for each $\overline{\gamma} \in \overline{\Gamma}$, scaling it by $t \in [0,1]$. ``Higher $t$'' matrices are associated with ``more convex'' quadratic forms, and ``lower $t$'' matrices are ``less convex.''
%This formalization 
Accordingly, I assume the type space $\Theta$ of senders is given by a set of compact, convex subsets of $\Gamma$.
\begin{assumption}
A type $\theta$ sender provides models of the form:
\begin{align*}
f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta^Tx-x^T\delta x,	
\end{align*}
where $\delta \in \theta$ for $\theta \subseteq \Gamma$ closed and convex.
Moreover,  $\theta \cap \theta' = \emptyset$ for all $\theta \neq \theta' \in \Theta$.
Additionally, a type $\theta$ sender has a prior $P_\delta^\theta$ over $\delta \in \theta$.
Finally, we have $P_\delta^\theta(\partial \theta) > 0$, where $\partial \theta$ is the \emph{boundary} of $\theta$.
\label{assmultitype}	
\end{assumption}

Finally, I describe how the sender samples data in multiple variables.
As before, I allow the sender to choose a ``mean'' $x_\theta$ and a variance-covariance matrix $\Sigma_\theta$.
Formally, let $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a \emph{spherically symmetric} random vector that admits a density with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$ and $\Cov(\epsilon) = I_n$.
Let $\Sigma_\theta$ be a symmetric positive-definite matrix.
Then, if the sender chooses $(x_\theta,\Sigma_\theta)$, his data $x$ are sampled with respect to the distribution $\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\Sigma_\theta)$, such that:
\begin{align*}
\mu_\theta(x_\theta, \Sigma_\theta) = x_\theta + \Sigma_{\theta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon.
\end{align*}
I place the following structure on data sampling.
\begin{assumption}
$x_\theta \in X^n$. Additionally,
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\sigma_i := \sqrt{\Sigma_{\theta,ii}} \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]$ and
\item $\rho_{ij} := \frac{\Sigma_{\theta,ij}}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \in [-\overline{\rho},\overline{\rho}]$
\end{enumerate}
for $\overline{\rho} \in (0,1)$ arbitrarily close to $1$.
\end{assumption}
This assumption is a multidimensional generalization of the idea that the ``variance'' of a variable $x_i$ must be between $\underline{\sigma}^2$ and $\overline{\sigma}^2$.
However, the key addition with $n$ choice variables is that the sender can choose the degree of \emph{correlation} between different variables when he samples data.
I bound the correlation away from $-1$ and $1$ to ensure the well-definedness of a solution.

\paragraph{Learning with Multidimensional Choices}
The following result generalizes receiver learning in multiple variables.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{reductionmulti}
\label{prop:reductionmulti}
Suppose $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1'x-x'\delta x$ best-fits the data and $\mu_\theta = \mu_\theta(x_\theta,\Sigma_\theta)$. Then, for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = f_S(x)+\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\theta\big)-(x-x_\theta)'(\gamma-\delta)(x-x_\theta).
\end{align*}
\end{restatable}
The proposition reads almost identically to Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, and the intuition is similar as well.
However, in place of the original scalar ``variance'' term, we instead have $\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\theta\big)$.
To see the impact that this has on the set of models the receiver will entertain, consider the following example with $x \in \mathbb{R}^2$. 
\begin{align*}
\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\delta = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma}^{\,2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
Here, the receiver's model allows for a ``cross effect'' between $x_1$ and $x_2$, while the sender's model neglects it. In turn, we thus have:
\begin{align*}
\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\theta\big)
=\overline{\sigma}^2\trace\bigg(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\bigg)
=\overline{\sigma}^2\rho.
\end{align*}
This trace term represents, as before, the \emph{gap} between the sender's original model and the receiver's model at $x_\theta$. The sender can \emph{increase} this gap if he makes $\rho$ as large as possible.

More formally, the sender in this case provides models that suffer from \emph{neglecting interactions}.
In our running example, the consultant whom the policymaker hires may effectively evaluate the effects of both a subsidy and tax \emph{separately}, but not take into account the \emph{interaction} of their effects on certain firms. 

As before, the receiver is aware of the sender's misspecification, and she entertains additional models. However, the sender can give the receiver a rosier view of these models if he artificially creates a high degree of correlation between $x_1$ and $x_2$ when sampling data.
In our example, if the consultant cannot figure out how to model these interactive effects, he compensates by skewing his data collection towards settings where both a carbon tax and a clean energy subsidy are active at the same time, rather than towards settings that cleanly isolate the effect of one or the other.


\paragraph{Results with Multidimensional Choices}
As before, given $f_S = \beta_0+ \beta^Tx - x^T \delta x$, we denote $\Delta(f_S)$ as:
%\begin{align*}
%\Delta(\delta|\Sigma_\theta) = 
%\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\theta\big)\big|\delta,\theta\big]
%=\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^n (\gamma_{ii}-\delta_{ii})\Sigma_{ii,\theta} +\sum_{i<j} \big[(\gamma_{ij}+\gamma_{ji})-(\delta_{ij}+\delta_{ji})\big]\Sigma_{ij,\theta}\big|\delta,\theta\bigg].
%\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\Delta(f_S) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta]-\delta.
\end{align*}
%As above, if $\theta \subsetneq \Gamma$, when $R$ sees a model with $\delta$ in the interior of $\theta$, she entertains no additional models, and $\Delta(f_S)=0$.
%However, if she says a model with $\delta$ on the boundary of $\theta$, she entertains additional models.
The intuitive mapping from the one-dimensional problem to the multidimensional one can be seen by looking at the diagonal entries of $\Delta(f_S)$.
If the $i^{th}$ diagonal entry is \emph{positive}, this means that $S$ tends to \emph{neglect tradeoffs} with respect to variable $x_i$.
If it is negative, he tends to \emph{exaggerate} tradeoffs.
Thus, we say that a sender estimates a \textbf{naive model in $x_i$} if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[\Delta_{ii}(f_S)] > 0$ and a \textbf{cautious model in $x_i$} if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[\Delta_{ii}(f_S)] < 0$.

However, what happens on the \emph{non-diagonal} entries of $\Delta$ is also of interest. Suppose $\Delta_{ij}(\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(\delta) > 0$. This means that the receiver expects the coefficients on the interactions of $x_i$ and $x_j$ to be \emph{larger} than what the sender provides.
We say that this sender \textbf{neglects interactions between $x_i$ and $x_j$}.
If $\Delta_{ij}(\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(\delta) < 0$, the sender \textbf{exaggerates interactions between $x_i$ and $x_j$}.
With these new definitions in hand, we can state the key multidimensional result.

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{samplingmulti}
Senders who estimate naive models in $x_i$ position themselves as generalists. Those who estimate cautious models in $x_i$ position themselves as specialists. I.e.,
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\mathbb{E}_\delta^\theta[\Delta_{ii}(\delta)] > 0$, then $\sigma_{i,\theta}=\overline{\sigma}$.
\item If $\mathbb{E}_\delta^\theta[\Delta_{ii}(\delta)] < 0$, then $\sigma_{i,\theta}=\underline{\sigma}$.	
\end{itemize}
Moreover, senders who neglect interactions between $x_i$ and $x_j$ create collinearity between $x_i$ and $x_j$. Senders who exaggerate interactions create anticollinearity between $x_i$ and $x_j$. I.e., 
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\mathbb{E}_\delta^\theta[\Delta_{ij}(\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(\delta)] > 0$, then $\Sigma_{ij,\theta}=\overline{\rho}\sigma_{i,\theta}\sigma_{j,\theta}$.
\item If $\mathbb{E}_\delta^\theta[\Delta_{ij}(\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(\delta)] < 0$, then $\Sigma_{ij,\theta}=-\overline{\rho}\sigma_{i,\theta}\sigma_{j,\theta}$.
\end{itemize}
\label{thm:samplingmulti}
\end{restatable}

First, Theorem \ref{thm:samplingmulti} replicates the intuition of the univariate result: if a sender tends to estimate a naive model in variable $x_i$ (i.e. systematically \emph{underestimate} the value of a tradeoff with respect to $x_i$), he positions himself as a generalist in variable $x_i$.
A cautious sender in variable $x_i$ positions himself as a specialist in that variable.

Theorem \ref{thm:samplingmulti} also shows what happens when a sender neglects or exaggerates \emph{interactions} between variables.
Suppose that the receiver believes that the interactions of variables $x_i$ and $x_j$ have positive, interactive effects.
Our policymaker, for instance, might believe that the interaction of a carbon tax and clean energy subsidy may have particularly deleterious welfare effects at very high values, but the sender might totally neglect this.
The theorem states that this sender will sample data so that $x_i$ and $x_j$ are as correlated as possible.

Intuitively, recall that any additional models the receiver entertains all have the same sample average $\mathbb{E}[y]$ over the data.
Because $x_i$ and $x_j$ are highly correlated in that data, this sample average is depressed in large part by the (negative) interaction that the sender's model neglects.
To rationalize the same sample average, the receiver's additional models must hence assign larger separate effects to $x_i$ and $x_j$ than the sender's model.
But since $x_i$ and $x_j$ are also choice variables, the receiver need not correlate them in her eventual choice, increasing her maximal expected utility.

In this sense, when choices are multivariate, senders have incentives to sample data in an \emph{unbalanced way}: rather than ensuring that the marginal effects of different variables are estimated independently of each other, there may be an arbitrary degree of collinearity between variables in the datasets that senders produce.\footnote{While this paper does not explicitly deal with finite samples, in practice, the introduction of this collinearity --- as well as specialist sampling --- can create large standard errors and biased parameter estimates.}


\section{Extensions: Forecasting, General Functional Forms}\label{section:extensions}
This section provides additional results from the baseline, univariate model.
First, I characterize the \emph{value of information} for the receiver for contracting a sender's services.
I show that although this expression does not depend on whether a sender positions as a generalist or specialist, it potentially \emph{does} depend on \emph{where} the sendeer sampled data.

Second, I augment the receiver's baseline preferences with \emph{forecast error}.
As a result, it captures settings where the receiver cares not just about maximizing $y$ but also about predicting $y$ accurately.
I show that the receiver's choices are more accurate when she receives information from a specialist than from a generalist.
Consequently, the receiver is better off hiring a cautious sender than a naive sender, although I also show that neither is first best.

Third, I study the model with non-quadratic utility and show that the central intuitions on sender sampling generalize.
In particular, under a regularity condition, senders who estimate naive models always prefer to sample data as generalists rather than specialists, and senders who estimate cautious models prefer the opposite.
Under an additional symmetry assumption, I am able to obtain the same result as Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
\footnote{While the proofs of these results are significantly more involved, I also walk through techniques that are utilized in the Appendix to make the problem more tractable.}

\subsection{Receiver's Value of Information}
Characterizing the value of information for the receiver from hiring a sender allows us to determine the receiver's \emph{willingness to pay} to hire the sender, prior to data sampling and model provision.
The value of information from hiring a type $\theta$ sender is given by:
\begin{align}
V(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{f_S|\theta}^R\big[\max_x \mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S,\theta}^R	 [f_R(x)|f_S(x)]\big]
-\max_x \mathbb{E}_{f_R}^R[f_R(x)]
\label{eq:valinfo}
\end{align}
In the absence of the sender, $R$ would solely rely on her prior to choose $x^*$.
In particular,  she would choose $x = \frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}$, which we assume is $\in [0,\overline{x}]$.
This allows me to characterize the \emph{value of information} for the receiver as follows.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{valueinfo}
Fix $\theta$.
The value of information $V(\theta)$ for the receiver from hiring a type $\theta$ sender is given by
\begin{align*}
V(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{f_R}^R\bigg[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\theta),\delta_\theta(\gamma)]\bigg(x^*-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg)^2\bigg],
\end{align*}	
where $\delta_\theta(\gamma):=\min\{\max\{\gamma,\underline{\delta}_\theta\},\overline{\delta}_\theta\}$ and
\begin{align*}
x^* = \max\big\{\underline{x},\min\bigg\{\overline{x},x_\theta + \frac{f_R'(x_\theta)}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\theta),\delta_\theta(\gamma)]}	\bigg\}\big\}
\end{align*}

\label{prop:valueinfo}
\end{restatable}
The expression for $V(\theta)$ is familiar from models of information acquisition with quadratic utility.
Indeed, if the receiver perfectly learns $\gamma$ (i.e. $\delta_\theta(\gamma) = \gamma$), $V(\theta)$ collapses to the difference between $\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{\alpha_1^2}{\gamma}\big]$ and $\frac{\mathbb{E}[\alpha_1]^2}{\mathbb{E}[\gamma]}$.
However, in our setting, learning may be distorted if the sender's model is simple, when the receiver learns $f_R$ up to its first derivative at $x_\theta$ and a constant.

Crucially, we note that $V(\theta)$ does not depend on $\sigma$, but does depend on $x_\theta$.
The intuition for the former is that different sampling strategies cause upwards and downwards shifts in the set of potential models the receiver entertains, which have no bearing on the \emph{choice} the receiver eventually makes.\footnote{Generalist and specialist sampling will, however, affect the \emph{variance} of the receiver's outcome, which I address in the next subsection.}

The variation with respect to $x_\theta$ is of interest, although finding the optimal value of $x_\theta$ proves highly intractable. 
The following proposition characterizes how $V(\theta)$ varies with $x_\theta$.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{samplingspot}
Writing the value of information $V(\theta)$ as a function of the parameter $x_\theta$, $V(\theta,x_\theta)$, we have
\begin{align*}
V_{x_\theta}(\theta,x_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{f_R}^R\Bigg[
\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_\theta}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\theta),\delta_\theta(\gamma)]\bigg)
\bigg(\frac{f_R'(x_\theta)}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\theta),\delta_\theta(\gamma)]}\bigg)^2\Bigg].	
\end{align*}
Moreover if $p_1^R$ is absolutely continuous as defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and $\lim_{|\alpha_1| \to \infty} \alpha_1^2p_1^R(\alpha_1) = \ = 0$,
$\arg\max_{x_\theta} V_{x_\theta}(\theta,x_\theta)>0$.
\label{prop:samplingspot}
\end{restatable}

Intuitively, $x_\theta$ affects the receiver's ability to learn about $\gamma$ through a \emph{signal extraction problem}.
That is, the receiver learns the value of $f_R'(x_\theta) = \alpha_1-2\gamma x_\theta$.
If $x_\theta = \xlow$, then the receiver fully learns $\alpha_1$ and places the same relative weights on different $\gamma$ values as in her prior.
However, if $x_\theta \neq \xlow$, she learns a weighted combination of $\alpha_1$ and $\gamma$, with the size of the weight determined by $x_\theta$.
The nonlinearities introduced by this signal extraction problem make finding an optimal $x_\theta$ highly intractable.


\subsection{Forecast Accuracy}\label{section:forecast}
So far, I have assumed only that the receiver would like to maximize $\mathbb{E}[y|x]$.
However, in many settings, the receiver may also directly (or indirectly) value \emph{forecast accuracy}.
For example, our policymaker may not just value picking the optimal carbon tax, but also being able to \emph{accurately forecast} the effects of her policy choice.
Being able to accurately predict the effects of her policy may allow her to better defend her choice to the press and bureaucracy, as well as to be more confident in her decision.
%In this sense, she intuitively prefers \emph{model certainty}, at least in a neighorhood of her optimal choice, which we have abstracted from so far.

To illustrate the impact of model simplicity and sampling on forecast-minded receivers, I consider a receiver whose expected utility is given by
\begin{align}
(1-\psi)\mathbb{E}^R[y|x]-\psi \Var^R(y|x)	
\label{eq:forecastutility}
\end{align}
for $\psi \in (0,1)$.
Intuitively, these preferences can also be thought of as follows. In a first period, the receiver forms an expectation of $y$.
In a second period, given this expectation, the utility she reaps is based on her ability to forecast.
If $\psi = 0$, she does not value the future or forecasting, and only cares about her expectation of $y$.
As $\psi \to 1$, she places a greater and greater emphasis on forecasting.

\paragraph{Exogeneous Forecast Error}
Before studying what happens when the receiver chooses $x$ to maximize (\ref{eq:forecastutility}), I first characterize how the variance of $y$ behaves with respect to $\sigma_\theta$ when the receiver simply maximizes the expected value off $y$.
The following proposition shows that, fixing $x_\theta$,  the variance of outcomes is minimized at an \emph{intermediate} value of $\sigma_\theta$.
That is, neither specialist nor generalist sampling minimizes forecast error for the receiver.
\footnote{As before, comparative statics with respect to $x_\theta$ are not straightforward, and so we omit them throughout.}
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{forecastsimple}
Fix a sender type $\theta$ and let $x^*(f_S)$ maximize $\mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S,\theta,x_\theta,\sigma_\theta}[y|x]$.
Then, there exists $\sigma_\theta^* \in (\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma})$ that maximizes
\begin{align*}
W(\sigma_\theta) :=\mathbb{E}_{f_S|\theta}^R\big[\Var_{f_R|f_S}^R\big(y|x^*(f_S),x_\theta,\sigma_\theta\big)\big].
\end{align*}
with respect to $\sigma_\theta$.
Moreover, $W$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma$ for $\sigma < \sigma_\theta^*$ and increasing for $\sigma>\sigma_\theta^*$.
\label{prop:forecastsimple}
\end{restatable}
The intuition for the result is as follows.
Writing $x^*$ as the value of $x$ that maximizes the expectated value of $y$, recall our previous formulation that
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}[f_R(x^*)|f_S] =f_S(x^*) + \Delta(f_S)\big(\sigma^2-(x^*(f_S)-x_\theta)^2\big)
\end{align*}
The receiver's forecast error is driven by the latter set of terms multiplied by $\Delta(f_S)$.
In particular, the variance of outcomes can be expressed as the difference between the degree of sampling $\sigma^2$ and the squared distance between $x^*$ and $x_\theta$ (the optimal choice and the point around which data are sampled).
When $\sigma^2$ is small, the dispersion is driven by $(x^*-x_\theta)^2$, which is always nonzero in expectation, meaning that the variance can be reduced by increasing $x_\theta$.
Intuitively, because there is uncertainty about what the ex-ante optimal choice $x^*$ will be, an intermediate degree of sampling allows the receiver to ``hedge'' and gather information in a slightly larger region where she expects the choice to be, rather than precisely at a single point.
When $\sigma^2$ is very large however, there is a great deal of vertical dispersion in the potential values of $y$ that may materialize at $x^*$, and that dispersion can be reduced by decreasing $\sigma^2$.

Thus, the \emph{second-best} degree of sampling $\sigma_\theta^*$ that the receiver would choose to minimize outcome variance is necessarily intermediate: that is, neither a sender who samples as a generalist nor a specialist minimizes the variance of the receiver's outcomes.
However, we note that as $\overline{\sigma}$ grows arbitrarily large, the sender \emph{will} be better off under specialist sampling than generalist sampling.


\paragraph{Endogeneous Forecast Error}
We now study the setting where $R$ jointly trades off her expected gain in $y$ with forecast error. 
Conditional on $f_S$ and fixing $x_\theta$, her expected value is
\begin{align*}
V^F(f_S|\theta,\sigma_\theta)
= \max_{x}	(1-\psi)\mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S,\theta,\sigma_\theta}[y|x]-\psi \Var_{f_R|f_S,\theta,\sigma_\theta}[y|x].
\end{align*}



Now, we describe how the receiver's ex-a

\begin{restatable}{proposition}{forecast}
Suppose $R$ chooses $x^*(f_S)$ to maximize (\ref{eq:forecastutility}).
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A naive sender chooses $\sigma_\theta > \sigma_\theta^*$ and a cautious sender chooses $\sigma_\theta < \sigma_{\theta^*}$
\item As $\psi \to 1$, $x^*(f_S) \to x_\theta \pm \sigma$.
\end{enumerate}
\label{prop:forecast}
\end{restatable}
The first part of the result generalizes the intuition of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
Namely, senders who estimate naive models sample data with a \emph{larger spread} than the what the reciever woudl choose, and those who estimate cautious models sample with \emph{less spread}.
The intuition is that the sender accounts for the fact that the receiver will shift her choices to take into account forecast error.
For very high values of $\sigma^2$, a naive sender trades off between increasing the receiver's expectation of $y$ through generalist sampling and increasing the variance of outcomes she faces.
The opposite logic holds for senders who estimate cautious models.

The second part of the result shows that if the receiver increasingly only values forecast error, her choice approaches $x_\theta \pm \sigma_\theta$.
This is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:forecast}.
Thus, $R$ trades off between a choice $x= x^\dag$ that maximizes her expected utility (at an interior point) and minimizing the variance of outcomes, which happens at $x_\theta \pm \sigma$.
When $\sigma$ is very large, $R$ is forced to choose arbitrarily extreme points $x_\theta \pm \sigma$ to minimize variance.
When $\sigma$ is very small, $R$ chooses points close to $x_\theta$.

\subsection{Non-Quadratic Tradeoffs}\label{section:general}
While the main model utilizes quadratic utility to express tradeoffs, in this section, I probe the robustness of many of the main results to \emph{non-quadratic} utility.
In particular, I show an analogue of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}: if $\overline{\sigma}$ is sufficiently large and $S$ estimates a naive model, sampling data as a generalist dominates sampling as a specialist.
If $S$ estimates a cautious model, being a specialist dominates being a generalist. 

\paragraph{General Loss Function}
The following two assumptions consider two generalizations of functional forms for the receiver.
\begin{assumption}
The receiver's models and a type $\theta$ sender's models are given by:
\begin{align}
&f_R(x) = \alpha_0+\alpha_1 x-\gamma \ell(x-x_\theta) 
\label{eq:generalfunctionrec}	
\\
&f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta \ell(x-x_\theta) \qquad \theta \in \Theta
\label{eq:generalfunctionsend}	
\end{align}
where $\ell: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^+$ is strictly convex and differentiable. Moreover, $\ell(0) = 0$, $\ell'(0) = 0$, and $\ell$ is symmetric around $x=0$.
\label{assfunction1}
\end{assumption}
One way to interpret this functional form is to think of a sender's or receiver's model as a \emph{Taylor expansion} around a point $x_\theta$.
This Taylor expansion includes a constant component, a linear term, and a concave $\mathcal{O}(2)$ term centered at $x_\theta$.
{While the convex loss $\ell(x-x_\theta)$ is symmetric around $x_\theta$, the assumption does allow for $f_R$ or $f_S$ themselves to be asymmetric.
This assumption also subsumes the case where $\ell(x) = x^2$, noting that $(x-x_\theta)^2$ can be broken into a constant and linear term plus an $x^2$ term.}

The case where this convex loss itself may be asymmetric is also of interest.
Thus, I consider the following alternative assumption.
\begin{assumption}
The receiver's models and a type $\theta$ sender's models are given by:
\begin{align}
&f_R(x) = \alpha_0+\alpha_1 x-\gamma m(x)
\label{eq:generalfunctionrec2}	
\\
&f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta m(x) \qquad \theta \in \Theta
\label{eq:generalfunctionsend2}	
\end{align}
where $m: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^+$ is strictly convex and differentiable, and $m'(x)$ is onto $\mathbb{R}$. Moreover, there exists $x$ such that $m(x) = m'(x) = 0$.
\label{assfunction2}	
\end{assumption}
This formulation of models allows for a convex loss function $m$ to take many different shapes.

With these two assumptions in hand, I prove a more general version of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{samplinggeneral}
Suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction1} holds.	
If $S$ estimates naive models, he positions himself as a generalist $(\sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma})$
and sets $x_\theta = \max\{\xlow,\min\big\{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\beta_1}^\theta[\beta_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\delta^\theta[\delta]},\overline{x}\big\}\big\}$.
If $S$ estimates cautious models, he positions himself as a specialist $(\sigma_\theta = \underline{\sigma})$ and sets $x_\theta = \xlow$ or $\overline{x}$.
\\
Alternatively, suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction2} holds, suppose $\overline{\sigma}$ is sufficiently large, and fix $x_\theta$.
If $S$ estimates naive models, he \emph{strictly prefers} $\sigma_\theta = \overline{\sigma}$ to $\underline{\sigma}$.
If $S$ estimates cautious models, he \emph{strictly prefers}  $\sigma_\theta = \underline{\sigma}$ to $\overline{\sigma}$.
\label{thm:samplinggeneral}
\end{restatable}

First, we note that the first part of Theorem \ref{thm:samplinggeneral} is identical to Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}: a sender who estimates naive models positions himself as a generalist, and a sender who estimates cautious models positions himself as a specialist.
The key reason is that the set of plausible $f_R$s must obey the following moment conditions.
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x + \delta \ell(x-x_\theta)\big] =0
\label{eq:interceptgeneral}
\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta \ell(x-x_\theta) \big)\big] =0 \label{eq:slopegeneral}
\end{align}
The proof shows that (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) can be boiled down to the condition that the derivative of all plausible $f_R$s must be the same across the data sample $\mu_\theta$.
Since $\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)$ and $\ell(x-x_\theta)$ are both symmetric around $x_\theta$, we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)}[f_R'(x)]
= \alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)}[\ell'(x-x_\theta)]
=\alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma')}[\ell'(x-x_\theta)] 
\qquad \forall \sigma,\sigma' \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]
\end{align*}
Thus, equation (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) is invariant to $\sigma$.
This means that, as in the quadratic case, as $\sigma$ changes, the set of models that $R$ considers as a function of $\sigma$ shifts up or down, allowing us to back out the same logic as in the original case.

However, if models are not characterized by a symmetric loss $\ell(x-x_\theta)$ around $x_\theta$, it is more difficult to obtain a result.
Theorem \ref{thm:samplinggeneral} simply states that, fixing $x_\theta$, $S$ prefers being a generalist to being a specialist if he estimates naive models (and the opposite holds if he estimates cautious models).
The reason is that, if the convex portion of a receiver's models is \emph{not} symmetric around $x_\theta$, the analogue of (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) no longer holds.
I.e., the expression
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)}[f_R'(x)]
=
\alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)}[m(x)]
\end{align*}
is generally \emph{not invariant} to changes in $\sigma$.
As a result, the proof allows $\alpha_1$ to change to ``absorb'' any changes to $\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta(x_\theta,\sigma_\theta)}[m(x)]$ with respect to $\sigma$.
For each $f_R(x)$ with a given $\gamma$ value, changing $\sigma$ thus not only changes $\alpha_0$ (as in the benchmark and symmetric loss models) but also $\alpha_1$.
It is here where we utilize the condition in Assumption \ref{assfunction2} that assigns the same prior mass to different $\alpha_1$ values in the receiver's prior, which ensures that these models receive the same posterior weight.

Consequently, the value of the receiver's objective at her optimal choice $x^*$ does not have an intuitive expression, and depends on nonintuitive features of the third derivative of $m(\cdot)$.
However, the proof shows that if the sender estimates naive models, the value of each $f_R(x)$ the receiver entertains at $x_\theta$ is \emph{increasing} in $\sigma$.
Hence, the value of each $f_R$ at $x_\theta$ for sufficiently high $\overline{\sigma}$ must necessarily be higher than the receiver's optimum $x^*$ at $\underline{\sigma}$.
The intuition for the case where the sender estimates cautious models, as before, is flipped.


\section{Gains from Simplification}\label{section:simple}
This section returns to the univariate quadratic model to investigate gains from model simplicity.
So far, I have taken model simplicity as exogenous: different sender types present models that are artificially naive or cautious.
This section characterizes when a sender benefits from the presence of other, simpler senders.
To start, I study what happens when senders' types overlap (i.e., multiple senders can provide the receiver a given model), and characterize when senders can \emph{gain} from the presence of other senders.

Using this result, I then ask what kind of \emph{market} for models a receiver may endogenously face: i.e., how likely she may be to receive an \emph{accurate} or \emph{simple} model, whether simple models tend to be naive or cautious, and how frequent each model type is in the population of senders she faces.
In particular, I consider an accurate sender or ``designer,'' who can always provide an accurate model of the world.
However, this sender is able to hire additional senders alongside him who may provide simple models.
Conditional on contracting the services of his organization, a receiver may receive information from either the accurate or one of these simple senders.
Formally: the accurate sender is able to endogenously choose the \emph{type space} of simple senders and their distribution in the population.
For example, the accurate sender could be thought of as:
\begin{itemize}
\item a consultant or portfolio manager who can employ other consultants/managers whose recommendations may take the form of simple rules of thumb;
\item a politician who can recruit co-partisans to propagate simple talking points among voters;
\item an AI company that can deploy simpler or more complex LLMs through its chatbot.
\end{itemize}
The key is that he is able to \emph{shape} the receiver's beliefs about whether she is facing a simple or accurate model.

I show that the accurate sender would organize the field of senders as follows.
First, he would either provide his own services, or those of ``simple'' senders who provide models that are calibrated to arbitrary parameters.
Formally, the type space $\Theta$ of simple senders is given by the collection of singletons $\Theta = \big\{ \{\delta\} : \delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]\big\}$.
Second, the \emph{distribution} of these types is characterized by \emph{polarization} in how senders consider tradeoffs.
Formally, for each $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$, the conditional probability of facing a type $\{\delta\}$ sender is given by a density $t(\delta)$.
This density $t(\delta)$ takes the accurate sender's prior over $\delta$ and pushes mass away from intermediate values of $\delta$ towards the edges of $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
I.e., simple senders either estimate models with low $\delta$ values (close to $0$) or high $\delta$ values (close to $\overline{\gamma}$), and never estimate models with intermediate $\delta$ values.
Thus, the set of simple sender types is fully composed of senders who are (arbitrarily) naive or cautious in the models they estimate.
As the probability of seeing the accurate sender's model approaches $1$, the type space of simple senders converges to a one- or two-point distribution concentrated on either $\delta = 0$, $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, or both points.
These accordingly correspond to a ``linear regression'' model and a ``worst-case'' model, as I studied in Section \ref{section:learning}.



\subsection{Model with Overlapping Types}

Previously, I assumed that senders differed in their ability to model the quadratic component of the receiver's models, that different senders could model this component in distinct ways, and thus that the receiver could fully back out the sender's type from the model she received.
However, I now relax Assumption \ref{asstype} and allow the sender's types to have overlapping supports.
That is, I consider the case where there exist $\theta,\theta' \in \Theta$ such that $\theta' \cap \theta \neq \emptyset$.

Let $\delta \in \theta \cap \theta'$.
Conditional on seeing a model of the form $y = \beta_0+\beta_1x -\delta x^2$, the receiver does not know whether this model came from a type $\theta$ or type $\theta'$ sender.
In particular, I can characterize when uncertainty about the source of the signal can actually work to a sender's benefit, and when it hurts the sender.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{simplegain}
Consider two senders $\theta,\theta'$ such that $\theta \cap \theta' \neq \emptyset$.
Suppose $R$ receives information from either sender with positive probability.
Then, a type $\theta$ sender's utility either strictly increases or decreases only if $\partial \theta' \cap \theta \neq \emptyset$ or $\partial \theta \cap \theta' \neq \emptyset$.
\label{prop:simplegain}
\end{restatable}
There are many permutations to consider, and the proof of the proposition walks exhaustively through all the different cases, but we highlight three here.
Denote $\theta = [\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}]$ and $\theta' = [\underline{\delta}',\overline{\delta}']$.
Given $f_S(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$, a type $\theta$ sender's utility strictly \emph{increases} from the existence of type $\theta'$ in the following cases.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, $\delta = \overline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ samples as a generalist.
\item $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, $\delta = \underline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ samples as a specialist.
\item $\delta = \overline{\delta}$, $\theta' = \{\delta\}$, type $\theta$ samples as a specialist, and $\theta'$ samples as a generalist.
\end{enumerate}

In the first case, if $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, then $f_S$ is the true model if it came from type $\theta$.
However, because $\delta = \overline{\delta}'$, if the model came from $\theta'$, the true model may also have $\gamma \geq \delta$.
Accordingly, the type $\theta'$ sender samples as a generalist, meaning that $R$ entertains models above $f_S$.
Thus, the receiver entertains both the possibility that $f_S$ is the true model, but also models with $\gamma \geq \overline{\delta}'$.
This benefits the type $\theta$ sender, who knows that he has the true model.

The same logic applies to the second case, where $\delta = \underline{\delta}'$ and type $\theta'$ samples as a specialist.
Type $\theta$ provides the true model, but the receiver entertains shallower models that lie above $f_S$ thanks to the type $\theta'$ sender's local sampling.

The third case considers a situation where a type $\theta$ sender is restricted in how he can sample.
In particular, it considers a case where $\delta = \overline{\delta}$ but the type $\theta$ sender is forced to sample as a specialist.
This may occur because $\underline{\delta}$ is more likely for this sender type, and he thus ex-ante chooses specialist sampling, even though he (ex-post) would be better off sampling as a generalist.
However, if $\theta' = \{\delta\}$ and that sender is sampling as a generalist, the type $\theta$ sender can be made better off, because the receiver now entertains some models above $f_S$ rather than only ones below.

A corollary of this result is that interactions are zero-sum: if a type $\theta$ sender is made better off by a type $\theta'$, the type $\theta'$ sender is made worse off.
\begin{corollary}
Conditional on providing a model $f_S(x)$, suppose a type $\theta$ sender's expected utility increases from a type $\theta'$ sender's existence.
Then, the type $\theta'$ sender's expected utility decreases from the existence of a type $\theta$ sender.
\end{corollary}


\subsection{Constructing the Market for Senders}
I now use these intuitions to characterize what sorts of models a receiver may face when she contracts the services of a sender, i.e., what the \emph{market of senders} she experiences looks like.
I formalize this problem as an endogenous choice of both a type space $\Theta$ and a distribution $T$ over that type space by an accurate sender or ``designer.''
This sender always provides an accurate model, but can also surround himself with other senders who provide (potentially simpler) models, thus changing the receiver's belief about his accuracy.

\paragraph{Motivation} To motivate the role of the accurate sender further, we return to the problem of the consultant and the policymaker.
Imagine an ``accurate'' consultant employed by a consulting organization, who can think about tradeoffs from carbon taxation in \emph{exactly} the way the policymaker would like.
However, he can also choose to hire additional colleagues, who might think about tradeoffs in a simpler way than both himself and the policymaker.
He can, in particular, choose how many of these colleagues to hire, as well as how simply they might think about problems like the policymaker's.

The consultant thus hires other consultants. The policymaker, in turn, is aware of the composition of the consultant's organization.
When she contracts the organization's services, she is randomly assigned either the ``accurate'' consultant or one of the ``simple'' consultants.
Relatedly, she may contract a team composed of both the accurate consultant as well as other simple consultants, but not know how much oversight the accurate consultant exercised.
Based on the model she is ultimately provided, she in turn attempts to \emph{infer} the sender's type.
Thus, our problem boils down to: will the accurate consultant hire ``simple'' consultants to join him, what kind of simplicity will they adopt, and how many of those consultants will he hire?

The key interpretation of the design problem is thus that a accurate sender can endogenously shape the set of senders that the receiver considers, such as by actively hiring additional senders who model tradeoffs differently.
Beyond hiring, we also highlight additional interpretations based on our motivating examples.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Portfolio management.} Suppose our sender is a portfolio manager who can estimate a highly accurate model of asset returns. The manager may help train other managers or deploy ``robo-advisors'' that use simple ``rules of thumb'' to shape client recommendations.

\item \textbf{Political narratives.} Suppose our sender is a politician who can accurately provide a ``narrative model'' of a policy's effects on voter welfare. However, the same politician can also propagate ``simple narratives'' of a policy's effects through online media accounts or by aligning himself with other politicians who provide simple narratives.
\item \textbf{AI recommendations.} Suppose our sender is an AI platform that uses a large LLM to collect data and estimate a robust model that provides users accurate recommendations. The platform can also deploy smaller LLMs that apply a ``fixed'' way of thinking about a user's problem, and can determine how often users are shown each model under the hood.
\end{enumerate}
As I will show, the key mechanism through which this type space design problem will operate is through manipulating the receiver's \emph{beliefs} about whether the model she sees came from a accurate or simple sender.


\paragraph{Design Problem Setup}
Formally, consider the problem of a accurate sender or ``designer'' $D$.
$D$ has a prior over $\beta_0,\beta_1,\delta$ as before given by $P_0^D$, $P_1^D$, $P_\delta^D$.
To ensure the results are not driven by belief asymmetry, I assume that $P_\delta^D = P_\gamma^R$.
We write $P_{f_S}^D$ for his prior over models $f_S$.

I suppose that $D$ faces the following problem.
Conditional on a model $f_S$, with probability $q_0$, $R$ believes she has received a model from $D$.
With probability $1-q_0$, she believes the model came from a type space $\Theta$.
$D$ can design the type space $\Theta$. That is, his problem is to choose a set $\Theta \subseteq \mathcal{P}([0,\overline{\gamma}])$ such that for all $\theta \in \Theta$, $\theta$ is a closed interval.
His objective is:
\begin{align*}
\max_{\Theta,T} q_0\int_{f_S \in F_R} \max_{x}\mathbb{E}_{q|f_S,T,\Theta}[f_R(x)]dP_{f_S}^D(f_S).
\end{align*}
That is, conditional on being contracted, the designer wants to, as before, maximize the receiver's perception of the maximal value of $y$ she can achieve.
We note that the impact of $\Theta$ is through the receiver's \emph{posterior}.
I take $q_0$ as fixed; this could represent the designer's capacity to hire others (higher $q_0$ means he has less latitude to hire), the exogenous choice of a platform/organization, or a market belief.\footnote{$q_0$ could also endogenously be chosen by the designer himself to trade off the probability with which his services will be contracted with receivers' perceptions of him.}


The order of events is as follows.
$D$ first chooses a type space $\Theta$.
Then, $D$ chooses a distribution over types $T$.
The receiver contracts the services of a sender, and receives a model from $D$ with probability $q_0$ or from the type space $\Theta$ with probability $1-q_0$.
Conditional on the latter event, a type $\theta$ sender is then drawn according to the distribution $T$.
Finally, the deployed sender strategically samples data as before, supplies the model of best fit, and provides it to the receiver.

\paragraph{Optimal Type Space}
We note that because the designer's models are not misspecified, his data sampling strategy does not affect the alternative models $R$ considers.
However, a ``simple'' sender with type $\theta$ will always strategically sample.\footnote{If the sender is such that the expected value of $\Delta(f_S)=0$, we assume that sender samples at $\xlow$.}
Thus, the assumption that the receiver cannot directly see the sender's data is key.

In turn, the optimal characterization of the type space is as follows.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{optimaltype}
Suppose $P_\delta^D$ is continuous.
Then, the optimal type space is the collection of singletons on $[0,\overline{\gamma}]=\Gamma$: $\Theta = \big\{\{\delta\} : \delta \in \Gamma\big\}$.
Moreover, there exists a function $\pi(\delta)$ such that $T$ is given by a continuous distribution $t$ with
\begin{align}
t(\delta) = p_\delta^D(\delta)\pi(\delta).
\tag{$T^*$}
\label{eq:optimalT}	
\end{align}
In particular, there exist thresholds $0 \leq \underline{\delta}_0<\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]<\overline{\delta}_0 \leq \overline{\gamma}$ such that $\pi(\delta)$ is strictly decreasing below $\underline{\delta}_0$, strictly increasing above $\overline{\delta}_0$, and equal to $0$ on $[\underline{\delta}_0,\overline{\delta}_0]$.
%where $\pi(\delta)$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])$, strictly increasing on $(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma],\overline{\gamma}]$, $\pi(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])=-\lambda$, and $\lambda>0$ is a constant.
%Moreover, there exist $0 \leq \underline{\delta}_0<\overline{\delta}_0 \leq \overline{\gamma}$ (with at least one of the weak inequalities strict) such that $t(\underline{\delta}_0)=t(\overline{\delta}_0)=0$.
\label{thm:optimaltype}
\end{restatable}

I first interpret the result before describing its mathematical intuition.
Theorem \ref{thm:optimaltype} says that the optimal type \emph{space} is given by a collection of singletons.
For each $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$ the designer creates a sender of type $\{\delta\}$.
Recalling a prior definition, this sender has the interpretation that he \emph{calibrates} the value of $\delta$ to a fixed (but arbitrary) value in $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
This means that conditional on seeing a model $f_S$, the receiver believes it is either the true model or was calibrated to an \emph{arbitrary} value.

The second part of the theorem characterizes the \emph{shape} of the distribution of $\{\delta\}$ values the designer utilizes.
The solution is that the designer takes his prior over $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$ and \emph{warps it} in a way that pushes more mass to the \emph{edges} of the interval.
In this sense, the designer \emph{polarizes} simple senders towards models calibrated to be \emph{arbitrarily} naive (with $\delta$ close to $0$) or cautious (with $\delta$ close to $\overline{\gamma}$).
In particular, $t(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]) = 0$, i.e. $t$ puts $0$ mass on types with $\delta$ equal to the receiver's prior expectation of $\gamma$.

The first part of the result relies on many of the intuitions of Proposition \ref{prop:simplegain}.
Consider a model of the form $f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$.
For each $\delta$, we introduce a sender of type $\{\delta\}$.
This sender is simple in the sense that he provides models with an arbitrarily fixed value of $\delta$. 
Conditional on seeing $f_S(x)$, $R$ would believe that it was either a true model from the designer or the same model provided by the simple type $\{\delta\}$ sender.
In the former case, the receiver would believe $f_S$ is the true model.
In the latter case, because the simple sender sampled strategically, $R$'s maximal expected utility from considering alternative models would be higher than from just considering $f_S$.
The designer thus always gains from creating a simple sender for each $\delta \in \Gamma$.

Naturally, $D$ could also introduce a sender of type $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$ with $\theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$.
This sender would exaggerate some tradeoffs and neglect others, but also sometimes provide the correct model. 
In this case, the assumption that $P_\delta^D$ has a continuous density is crucial.
The only time $D$ could gain from this sender's existence is if that sender provided a model with either $\delta = \overline{\delta}$ or $\underline{\delta}$.
However, $D$ would not gain at all if $\delta \in (\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta})$, since this type $\theta$ would always be providing the \emph{true model}.
This erases any potential gains from model simplicity and strategic sampling for $D$.
Since $\overline{\delta}$ and $\underline{\delta}$ are single points, each point would have measure $0$ in $D$'s prior, while $(\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta})$ has measure greater than $0$.
Thus, $D$ would prefer to have many different senders model tradeoffs arbitrarily, rather than having other senders sometimes provide the true model and sometimes not.

The intuition for the second part of the result is as follows.
The designer's problem is to choose a distribution over types which, in this case, boils down to just being a distribution over the set of singletons $\{\delta\}$.
In particular, he designates an amount of probability density to allocate to each $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$, subject to the constraint that these densities must integrate out to $1$.

Suppose $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, which is the \emph{receiver}'s ex-ante expectation of $\gamma$.
Looking back to Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, simple senders gain neither from generalist nor specialist sampling in this case.
Relatedly, this means that the designer has no incentive to place mass on $\delta$ values near $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
On the other hand, as $\delta$ gets farther and farther away from $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, simple senders provide a larger and larger gain from simple models.
In fact, the gains to the sender are conditionally maximal if $\delta = 0$ or $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
This is what drives the ``polarization'' of the type space.

\paragraph{Variation with $q_0$} We note that as $q_0$ increases, the receiver is more likely to think that she saw the designer's model, and that as $q_0$ decreases, this is less likely.
In the example with the consultant and policymaker, our ``designer'' may be required to provide his services $q_0$ of the time, but has latitude to choose the senders who the receiver contracts $1-q_0$ of the time.
As $q_0$ increases, this latitude decreases, and the receiver is conditionally more and more sure that she received the true model. 

The following proposition shows that simple sender ``polarization'' is increasing in $q_0$, in the sense that as $q_0$ increases, $t(\delta)$ places more and more mass on extreme types with very high or low $\delta$ values, and less (or none) on intermediate types.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{polarizationlimit}
The polarization of the type space is increasing in $q_0$. That is, $\underline{\delta}_0$ is decreasing in $q_0$ and $\overline{\delta}_0$ is increasing in $q_0$.
As $q_0 \to 1$, $t(\delta)$ converges to a distribution that either places all mass only on $\delta = 0$, $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, or both.
As $q_0 \to 0$, $t(\delta)$ converges to a distribution that is strictly decreasing on $[0,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])$, equal to $0$ at $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and strictly increasing on $(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma],\overline{\gamma}]$.
\label{prop:polarizationlimit}
\end{restatable}
The proposition is visualized above in the right panel of Figure \ref{fig:typespace}. The dashed purple line graphs $t(\delta)$ for a lower value of $q_0$. The solid line graphs $t(\delta)$ for a higher value of $q_0$, showcasing the ``increased polarization'' generated by an increase in $q_0$.

The intuition is as follows.
The designer's gain from being mistaken as a simple sender is high at $\delta = 0$, decreases to $0$ at $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and then rises again until $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
That is, he conditionally gains the most when $\delta$ is very low or very high.
When $q_0$ is large, the ex-ante probability with which he will be seen as a simple sender is low, and he is thus best off concentrating simple senders close to $\delta=0$ and $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, where he expects the gain to be largest.
However, when $q_0$ is small, he is willing to distribute simple senders more evenly throughout the population although, in either case, he concentrates few senders near $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.

I highlight two additional features of this result.
First, polarization survives even as $q_0 \to 0$ (so the receiver is less likely to think she sees the true model).
Second, as $q_0 \to 1$, the optimal distribution either converges to only deploying senders with type $\{0\}$ or type $\{\overline{\gamma}\}$ and nothing in between.
That is, the model returns precisely to the two-type case introduced at the beginning of Section \ref{section:learning}: in the limit, the only simple senders are those who \emph{only provide} linear regression models or \emph{only provide} ``worst-case'' models.

\section{Conclusion}\label{section:conclusion}
This paper studies how strategic data collection and model simplicity shape communication with models and data.
I study a theoretical model where a receiver $R$ contracts a sender $S$ to help her make a decision.
The sender $S$ first collects data and then estimates a model.
However, $R$ does not necessarily know what kind of data $S$ estimated the model on.
$S$'s model may additionally be \emph{simplified} in that it is \emph{naive} and neglects tradeoffs $R$ cares about --- i.e. always assumes models have lower curvature ---- , or is \emph{cautious} and exaggerates the extent of tradeoffs --- i.e. always assumes models with high curvature.
$S$'s goal is to make $R$ as optimistic as possible.
The setting I study has a wide variety of applications to the economics of organizations, portfolio management, political economy, delegation in policymaking, and the use of AI-based decisionmaking.

I first characterize how $R$ learns based on the model that $S$ provides her.
I show that $R$ may sometimes believe that $S$ provided an accurate model of her decisions, but that more often than not, $R$ must entertain additional models.
If $S$ estimated his model on a small set of choices as a ``specialist,'' I show that the additional models $R$ entertains are all tangent to $S$'s original model at a point.
If $S$ estimated his model on a large range of choices as a ``generalist,'' I show that the additional models $R$ entertains are all secants through $S$'s original model.

I then show a close correspondence between what kinds of data $S$ collects and his model \emph{simplicity}.
If $S$ estimates naive models that \emph{neglect} tradeoffs, then he collects data as a generalist across an arbitrarily wide set of a receiver's choices.
If $S$ estimates cautious models that \emph{exaggerate} tradeoffs, then he collects data as a specialist about a small set of choices near the extremes of the receiver's choice space.
In both cases, the receiver considers not only the sender's original model, but also models that deliver strictly higher expected utility, creating gains from the coupling of model simplicity and strategic data sampling.
Thus, there is a close correspondence between naive models and positioning oneself as a ``generalist''; and between cautious models and positioning oneself as a ``specialist.''
When choices are multivariate, I show that the sender samples data in a way that maximizes collinearity between variables, and makes them as correlated (or anticorrelated) as possible.

Taken together, these points suggest that the quality of \emph{data} may often be \emph{low} when contracting external senders to present model-based recommendations.
Moreover, this low quality of data emerges \emph{precisely} as a result of the fact that senders estimate simple models.
In particular, the data underlying simple models will often be only loosely related to the choices receivers eventually make.
Generalist senders sample data as widely as possible across the choice space and, in particular, place as much mass as possible at its edges.
Specialist data are precise, but only describe the value of outcomes at one edge of the choice space.
In both cases, the data embed information about choices the receiver is unlikely to end up making.
Thus, clients should be wary when interpreting the breadth of a consultant's or portfolio manager's data.

The results on multivariate choices show further that the data senders collect may be particularly unbalanced and highly collinear.
Although the paper abstracts from finite samples, high collinearity degrades the precision of parameter estimates and the balance of data across covariates, and thus the accuracy of decisions.
In our example, a consultant who cannot model how a carbon tax and a clean energy subsidy interact draws evidence from settings where both were adopted together; this leaves the policymaker with little information about what each policy does on its own.
More broadly, policymakers may be provided evidence about the effects of multiple reforms operating at once, or firms may receive recommendations based on situations where both advertising campaigns and price promotions were run at the same time.
This collinearity makes it difficult to separate the levers that these different variables control.


The paper further shows that the degree of sampling that minimizes forecast error for the sender is an \emph{intermediate} degree of data collection between pure generalism and specialization.
Hiring either a specialist or generalist sender is likely to result in imprecise learning, even conditional on a sender's misspecification.
This illustrates a tradeoff between optimism and accuracy: the models that make receivers most optimistic do not always lead to the best choices.
A CEO who hires the consultant who makes her potential decisions look best may also struggle to pin down the precise effects of her choices, affecting her ability to justify that decision to a board of directors or to colleagues.

I show that senders who provide the true model can generally gain from the existence of simple senders.
That is, if a sender provides a true, accurate model of a receiver's choices, he always gains if the receiver thinks his model was (artificially) naive or cautious.
The intuition is that, in the latter cases, the receiver entertains not only a sender's original model but also additional models that lie above it.
Thus, the sender who provides an accurate model can make the receiver have a more optimistic belief about her maximal expected utility than the true model.
Accurate senders may therefore try to masquerade as simple ones: clients of a large consulting firm may not know whether a senior partner or junior analyst built their model, investors may be unable to tell whether an experienced manager or a robo-advisor suggested a rule, and users of an AI platform may not know whether a recommendation came from a small or a large model.

Finally, I use these results on model simplicity to characterize the market for senders.
I consider the problem of an accurate sender who estimates an accurate model of a receiver's tradeoffs who is able to provide the receiver an accurate model of tradeoffs, but can also surround himself with ``simple'' senders who estimate naive or cautious models.
First, I show that whenever the accurate sender wants to hire simpler senders, he always hires senders who \emph{calibrate} their models to arbitrary parameters, and thus never truly estimate the value of tradeoffs on data.
In the real world, these correspond to senders who calibrate their models to pre-defined parameters: economic consultants may calibrate models to predetermined elasticities or labor shares from the literature, robo-advisors may apply fixed investing rules of thumb across clients, and politicians may weave narratives in which a policy's costs are fixed in advance.
The paper suggests that calibration of this kind need not reflect a lack of data or expertise, but may instead emerge as a feature of a market for senders that advantages those able to provide the most accurate models.

Second, I show simple senders polarize into two distinct groups: those who estimate (arbitrarily) naive models that are close to ``linear regression'' models, and those who estimate (arbitrarily) cautious models that are close to ``worst-case'' models.
Thus, the market of senders is composed of three groups: some senders who always provide accurate models, some who provide highly naive models, and some who provide highly cautious models.
In the limit, a receiver sees only linear regressions or worst-case models.
These simple models tend to recommend either very extreme or very conservative actions: naive models suggest making choices as extreme as possible, while cautious models may imply sticking to the status quo.
While this form of polarization is common among political narratives, the paper suggests that it may occur more generally across many situations economists study.
A market for simple models may be saturated with models that recommend either very extreme or very conservative choices.

The combination of simple models, strategic data sampling, and polarization all represent key frictions that may emerge when decisionmakers rely on external expertise and modeling to shape their choices.
Future work will investigate these frictions further in the settings of price-based markets and the polarization of political platforms, as well as through empirical work that investigates the use of AI-based decisionmaking in firms or the role of consultants' narratives in shaping organizational decisionmaking.\pagebreak

We rewrite the sender's model as a local expansion around $x_\theta$: $f_S(x) = \tilde{\beta}_0+\tilde{\beta}_1 (x-x_\theta)-\delta(x-x_\theta)^2$, where $\tilde{\beta_1} = \beta_1-2\delta x_\theta$.
Because sender type intervals $\theta$ are non-overlapping, the value of $\delta$ uniquely pins down the sender's type $\theta$.

Suppose $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta)$.
Similarly expanding $f_R$ around $x_\theta$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \tilde{\alpha}_0 + \tilde{\alpha}_1 (x-x_\theta)-\gamma(x-x_\theta)^2.	
\end{align*}
Because $\mu_\theta$ is symmetric, it is elliptical (in one dimension).
By Stein's Lemma \citep{landsman2008stein}, equation (\ref{eq:slope}) can be written as
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-f_S(x) \big)\big]
&= \Cov_{\mu_\theta}(x,f_R(x)-f_S(x))
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}[f_R'(x)-f_S'(x)]
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}[\tilde{\alpha}_1-2\gamma(x-x_\theta)-\tilde{\beta_1}+2\delta (x-x_\theta)]
= 0
\end{align*}
Because the mean of $\mu_\theta$ is $x_\theta$, this yields the condition $\tilde{\alpha}_1 = \tilde{\beta}_1$ which is \emph{independent} of $\sigma^2$.
This means that $\alpha_1-2\gamma x_\theta = \beta_1-2\delta x_\theta$, i.e. $\alpha_1 = \beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\theta$.
Hence, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \alpha_0+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\theta)x-\gamma x^2,
\end{align*}
with the value of $\gamma$ and $\alpha_0$ potentially indeterminate.
However, fixing a plausible $\gamma$ value, from (\ref{eq:interceptgeneral}) we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}[f_R(x)]
= \alpha_0+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\theta)x_\theta-\gamma (x_\theta^2+\sigma^2)
= \beta_0+\beta_1x_\theta-\delta (x_\theta^2+\sigma^2)
= \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}[f_S(x)]
\\
\implies
\alpha_0-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\theta^2=\beta_0-\delta x_\theta^2-\delta \sigma^2
\\
\implies \alpha_0 = \beta_0+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\theta^2).
\end{align*}
Putting the pieces together, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\theta^2)+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\theta)x-\gamma x^2
\\
= \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2 + \big(-(\gamma-\delta)x^2 	
+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\theta^2)+2(\gamma-\delta)x_\theta
\big)
\\
= \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2 + (\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)
\\
= f_S(x)+(\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big).
\end{align*}

\sampling*
\begin{proof}
Following from Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, let $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$.
Suppose $\theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$ and $\delta \in \theta^\circ$.
Then (\ref{eq:gamma}) is necessarily satisfied, and $\phi(f_S;\theta,\mu_\theta) = \{f_S\}$.
However, if $\delta = \underline{\delta}_\theta$ or $\overline{\delta}_\theta$, then $(\ref{eq:gamma})$ is a corner solution from the receiver's perspective.
If $\delta =  \overline{\delta}_\theta$, then any $\gamma \geq \overline{\delta}_\theta$ is plausible from the receiver's perspective.
If $\delta = \underline{\delta}_\theta$, any $\gamma \leq \underline{\delta}_\theta$ is plausible.
Finally, if $\underline{\delta}_\theta=\overline{\delta}_\theta$, any $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$ value is possible.
This pins down the plausible set of $\gamma$ values from the receiver's perspective.

Next, fix $\sigma$.
The receiver chooses $x=x^*$ to maximize:
\begin{align}
\beta_0+\beta_1 x -\delta x^2 + \big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S]-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)
\notag
\\
=\beta_0+\beta_1 x -\delta x^2 + \Delta(f_S)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2\big)
\label{eq:theorem1obj}
\end{align}
We note that because the receiver has a flat prior over $\alpha_0$, her posterior places the same mass on all models that are the same ``up to a constant''; hence, changing $\sigma^2$ does not change the relative mass she places on each model, fixing a plausible $\gamma$ value.
Thus, by the Envelope Theorem, (\ref{eq:theorem1obj}) is increasing in $\sigma^2$ if $\Delta(f_S)$ is positive and decreasing if $\Delta(f_S)$ is negative.
Working backwards, when the sender takes expectations over (\ref{eq:theorem1obj}), he chooses $\sigma = \overline{\sigma}$ if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}[\Delta(f_S)] \geq 0$ and $\underline{\sigma}$ otherwise.


Finally, we characterize the equilibrium value of $x_\theta$.
Note that because the receiver's prior over $\alpha_1$ is uniform and that $[\underline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha}]$ is sufficiently large compared to $[\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta}]$, $\Delta(f_S)$ does not depend on $x_\theta$ from $S$'s persepective.\footnote{In particular, the receiver entertains all plausible $(\alpha_1,\gamma)$ values such that $\alpha_1-2\gamma x_\theta = \beta_1 - 2\delta x_\theta$. For certain values of $x_\theta$ and $\gamma$ values close to $0$ or $1$, the likelihood of a given $(\alpha,\gamma)$ value is $0$. The fact that the sender's prior is thus ``smaller'' than the receiver's allows us to obviate this concern from his perspective.}
Returning to (\ref{eq:theorem1obj}) and using the envelope theorem, we have that the derivative with respect to $x_\theta$ is:
\begin{align*}
2\Delta(f_S)(x^*-x_\theta)
%\propto (\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S]-\delta)\frac{\beta_1-2\delta x_\theta}{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S]}.
\end{align*}
This is (strictly) concave if $\Delta(f_S)>0$, and achieves a maximum when $x^*= x_\theta$.
If $\Delta(f_S) \leq 0$, the function is convex and achieves a maximum at a corner solution $\xlow$ or $\overline{x}$, giving the potential values for $x_\theta$.
Given $S$ must commit to data collection beforehand and integrating backwards, this gives $x_\theta = \mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[x^*]$ if he expects $\Delta(f_S)>0$ and $x_\theta = \xlow$ or $\overline{x}$ otherwise.

Finally, we note that $x^*(f_S)$ solves
\begin{align*}
	\beta_1-2\delta x^* - 2\Delta(f_S)(x^*-x_\theta)=0
%	\implies
%	\beta_1 +2\Delta(f_S)x_\theta = x^*(2\delta+2\Delta(f_S))
	\implies
	x^* = \frac{\beta_1 +2\Delta(f_S)x_\theta}{2\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta]}
	\\
	\implies \mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[x^*(f_S)]=\mathbb{E}_{1,\delta}^{\theta}\bigg[\frac{\beta_1 +2\Delta(f_S)x_\theta}{2\delta+2\Delta(f_S)}\bigg].
\end{align*}


\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
We show the following cases exhaustively.
Given $f_S(x)$, a type $\theta$ sender's utility strictly increases (decreases) from the existence of type $\theta'$ if one of the following holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\delta \in \theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$, $\delta = \overline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ distantly (locally) samples.
\item $\delta \in \theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$, $\delta = \underline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ locally (distantly) samples.
\item $\delta = \overline{\delta}$, $\theta^\circ = \emptyset$, type $\theta$ locally samples, and $\theta'$ distantly (locally) samples.
\item $\delta = \underline{\delta}$, $\theta^\circ = \emptyset$, type $\theta$ distantly samples, and $\theta'$ locally (distantly) samples.
\item $\theta^\circ = \emptyset$, $\theta'^\circ \neq \emptyset$, $\delta = \overline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ distantly (locally) samples.
\item $\theta^\circ = \emptyset$, $\theta'^\circ \neq \emptyset$, $\delta = \underline{\delta}'$, and $\theta'$ locally (distantly) samples.
\end{enumerate}
First, we show how the receiver's expected value from entertaining models that have steeper or shallower tradeoffs than the sender's affects her maximal expected utility.
Suppose $\gamma > \delta$. 
Given a sampling strategy $\sigma$, we have:
\begin{align*}
\max \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta (x-x_\theta)^2 = \beta_0+\beta_1	x_\theta +\frac{\beta_1^2}{4\delta}
\\
\max \beta_0+\beta_1x-\gamma (x-x_\theta)^2 + (\gamma-\delta)\sigma^2 = \beta_0+\beta_1	x_\theta +\frac{\beta_1^2}{4\gamma}+(\gamma-\delta)\sigma^2.
\end{align*}
The latter is greater than the former when
\begin{align*}
\frac{\beta_1^2}{4\gamma}+(\gamma-\delta)\sigma^2 \geq \frac{\beta_1^2}{4\delta}	
\iff
(\gamma-\delta)\sigma^2 \geq 	\frac{\beta_1^2}{4}\bigg(\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\bigg)
\iff
\sigma^2 \geq \frac{\beta_1^2}{\gamma\delta}.
\end{align*}



Now, suppose $\delta \in \theta_i^\circ, \theta_j^\circ$.
Then, the receiver entertains just the sender's model, which she believes is the sender's (only) model.
Thus, we consider situations where the boundaries of the sets intersect.
	
	
Next, suppose $\underline{\delta}_i \in \theta_j^\circ$.
Conditional on seeing $\underline{\delta}_i$, the receiver entertains models with $\gamma \leq \underline{\delta}_i$.
By Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}, the sender would prefer to locally sample in these cases. 
However, in his initial choice of $\sigma$, the sender is forced to consider both the probability of $\overline{\delta}_i$ and $\underline{\delta}_i$.
If $\sigma_i = \overline{\sigma}$, the sender is being forced to distantly sample, which delivers a gain most of the time but a loss in the case where $\underline{\delta}_i$ materializes.
However, if $\underline{\delta}_i \in \theta_j^\circ$, then with some probability, the receiver believes the $\underline{\delta}_i$ model was the true model and came from the type $j$ sender.
This means the receiver places a higher weight on the sender's model itself, and less on models with lower $\gamma$ values (which, under $\sigma_i=\overline{\sigma}$, deliver lower expected utility).

A similar argument shows that the sender \emph{loses out} when $\delta = \underline{\delta}_i$ and $\sigma_i=\underline{\sigma}$.
In this case, the sender is (optimally) gaining from his own local sampling, meaning the remaining models the receiver entertains deliver higher expected utility.
However, the receiver thinks with positive probability that the sender's model is in fact the \emph{true} model, causing her to relatively downweight these alternative models and focus more weight on the sender's model, which delivers lower expected utility.
Yet another similar argument shows that the sender gains (loses) when $\overline{\delta}_i \in \theta_j^\circ$ and $\sigma_i=\underline{\sigma}$ $(\sigma_i = \overline{\sigma})$.

Next, suppose $\delta \in \theta_i^\circ$ and that $\delta = \overline{\delta}_j$.
If the type $j$ sender is distantly sampling ($\sigma_j = \overline{\sigma}$), the receiver entertains not only type $i$'s model but also models with higher $\gamma$ (since she believes the model could have also come from $\theta_j$).
The set of models $R$ entertains is thus rosier than if she were to just entertain the type $i$ sender's model, meaning the type $i$ sender benefits from the type $j$'s distant sampling.
Local sampling, on the other hand, would hurt him.
A similar argument takes care of the case where $\delta = \underline{\delta}_j$, where the type $\theta_i$ sender is hurt by distant sampling and benefits from local sampling.
 
\end{proof}

t(\delta)d\delta
=\int_0^{\underline{\delta}_0(c)}
\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta	
+\int_{\overline{\delta}_0(c)}^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta	
= 1
\\
\pi(\overline{\delta}_0(c),c)p_\delta^D(\overline{\delta}_0(c))\overline{\delta}_0'(c)
-
\pi(\underline{\delta}_0(c),c)p_\delta^D(\underline{\delta}_0(c))\underline{\delta}_0'(c)
+
\int_0^{\underline{\delta}_0(c)}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)
+
\int_{\overline{\delta}_0(c)}^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)
\\
=
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta= 0,
\end{align*}
where we use the fact that $\pi(\overline{\delta}_0(c),c) = \pi(\underline{\delta}_0(c),c) = 0$.

Next, note that, fixing $\delta$, the first order condition can be rearranged as
\begin{align*}
\Xi(c\pi,\delta)
:= 
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{(1+c\pi)^2}
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^D[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}\big)
= \frac{\lambda(c)}{c}
\end{align*}
The FOC then gives
\begin{align*}
\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi_c +\pi)
= \frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}
\implies
c\pi_c = \frac{\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}}-\pi
\end{align*}
But then, using the boundary condition, we have
\begin{align*}
c\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} \pi_c(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta
=
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} \frac{\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}}p_\delta^D(\delta)-\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta=0
\\
\implies \bigg(\lambda'(c)-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c}\bigg)\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi,\delta)}p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta= c
\\
\implies
\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2} = \frac{1}{\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi,\delta)}p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta} < 0
\end{align*}
since $\Xi_{(c\pi)} < 0$ (i.e. $\Xi$ is decreasing in $c\pi$, from the proof of the previous Theorem).

Now, consider $\Xi(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))$, which implicitly defines $\underline{\delta}_0(c)$ as
\begin{align*}
\Xi(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))
:= 
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\underline{\delta}_0(c)\big)
\big(\sigma_\delta^2
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^D[(\beta_1-2\underline{\delta}_0(c) x_\delta)^2]}{4(\underline{\delta}_0(c) )^2}\big)
= \frac{\lambda(c)}{c}.	
\end{align*}
In this case, we have
\begin{align*}
\Xi_{\delta}(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))\underline{\delta}_0'(c) = \frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}
< 0.
\end{align*}
But recall from the proof of the previous result that $\Xi_\delta < 0$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
This means that $\underline{\delta}_0(c)$ is increasing in $c$, i.e. is increasing in $\frac{1-q_0}{q_0}$. Hence, as $q_0$ increases, $\underline{\delta}_0$ decreases.
An analogous proof shows that as $q_0$ increases, $\overline{\delta}_0$ increases.
That is, the ``polarization'' induced by $\pi$ is increasing in $q_0$.

Finally, we investigate the limiting cases as $q_0 \to 0$ ($c \to \infty$) and $q_0 \to 1$ ($c \to 0)$.
We begin with $c \to \infty$, and claim that $\underline{\delta}_0 \to \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
First, by our work above $\frac{\lambda(c)}{c}$ is decreasing in $c$, meaning it converges to a limit.
We claim $\lim_{c \to \infty} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c} = 0$.
Suppose by contradiction that this did not hold, and instead it converged to some $k > 0$.
This would mean that there exists $\overline{c\pi}>0$ such that $c\pi(\delta)\leq\overline{c\pi}$ for all $c$ and $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]-\lim_{c \to \infty}[\underline{\delta}_0(c),\overline{\delta}_0(c)]$.
But then, for all $c$ we would have
\begin{align*}
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} 	\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta \leq \int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}\overline{c\pi}p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta
= \frac{\overline{c\pi}}{c},
\end{align*}
which approaches $0$ as $c \to \infty$, contradicting that $\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} 	\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta=1$.
Hence, we have that $\lim_{c \to \infty} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c} = 0$, meaning $\lim_{c \to \infty}\underline{\delta}_0(c) = \lim_{c \to \infty}\overline{\delta}_0(c) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
Moreover, we note that even for $c$ arbitrarily large, $\pi(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]) = 0$, with $\pi'(\delta) < 0$ below $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ and $\pi'(\delta)>0$ above $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ since $\Xi(c\pi,\delta)$ is decreasing in $\delta$ and increasing in $\delta$ on each range, respectively. 


Finally, we investigate the case where $c \to 0$.
We claim that as $c\to 0$, $\underline{\delta}_0 \to 0$ and $\overline{\delta}_0 \to \overline{\gamma}$ (and hence $t$ converges to a discrete distribution that either concentrates mass on $0$ or $\overline{\gamma}$ or both).
In particular, note that for all $c$, we have
\begin{align*}
c = \int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}c\pi(\delta,c)p_\delta^D(\delta)d\delta,	
\end{align*}
which means that as $c \to 0$, we necessarily must have $c\pi(\delta,c) \to 0$ as well. This can happen only if 
\begin{align*}
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\sigma_\delta^2
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^D[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta)^2}\big)
\leq \lim_{c\to 0} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c},
\end{align*}
i.e. either the multiplier constraint must hold with equality or fail to hold (generating a corner solution at $\pi(\delta,c)= 0$).
Since this must hold pointwise for each $\delta$ for $c$ sufficiently small, we thus have that $\underline{\delta}_0 \to 0$ and $\overline{\delta}_0 \to \overline{\gamma}$, giving the result.
\end{proof}
