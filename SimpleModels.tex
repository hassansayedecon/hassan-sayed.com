\begin{abstract}
\normalsize
Many real-world decisions rely on models and data supplied by experts, such as consultants, financial advisors, and even AI chatbots. I study how model simplification changes experts' incentives to collect data when their goal is to make decisionmakers optimistic. A receiver decides whether to adopt an initiative and, if so, how to implement it. A sender samples data and reports a model of how the receiver's choices under that initiative map to outcomes. He is either ``accurate,'' and correctly estimates the functional form mapping choices to outcomes, or ``simple,'' and calibrates the \emph{curvature} of this relationship to a predetermined value. I show that simple senders strategically sample data only about extreme choices. Because the receiver knows a sender's model may be simple, she entertains every model consistent with these data. By sampling only extreme choices, the sender leaves her uncertain about the intermediate choices she is likely to make, where these alternative models promise higher expected utility than his original model. This makes her more optimistic about the initiative. Consequently, accurate senders benefit when receivers perceive their models as simple, because the receiver then entertains not just an accurate model but also these more optimistic alternatives, making her more likely to adopt the initiative. When an accurate sender can choose the composition of simple senders in the population, he surrounds himself with a polarized group of simple senders who estimate extremely low or extremely high curvature models. In the limit, these correspond to linear regression and ``worst-case scenario'' models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Consider a policymaker designing a carbon tax reform.
She will launch the reform if she believes she can improve household welfare with the right tax.
She understands the basic nonlinear relationship: low taxes do not curb emissions, while high taxes generate economic costs.
The welfare-maximizing tax lies somewhere in the middle.
To help her figure out where it is, she hires a consultant, who collects data and estimates a \emph{model}: a prediction of how each tax rate maps to welfare.
This interaction is potentially valuable, but it presents several issues.
First, if the policymaker launches the reform, the consultant will receive credit for helping her.
He is thus concerned with making her optimistic enough to launch the reform.
Second, the consultant's model may be too simple. Imagine he runs a linear regression of welfare on taxes, neglecting the nonlinear relationship the policymaker entertains.
The policymaker suspects his model is simplistic, and thus considers other models that could have produced similar data.
This leads to a third issue: the consultant chose which data to collect.
Those data may describe the effects of low or high taxes, and say little about the moderate taxes the policymaker is considering.

This example reflects a broader phenomenon: decisionmakers rely on experts to model the world for them.
Investors hire financial advisors to model stock returns, voters use political narratives to understand laws, and, increasingly, any of these decisionmakers may ask an AI chatbot for a recommendation.
Models provide a convenient informational language for understanding the effects of different choices.
Indeed, they can generate optimistic hiring \citep{flynn2025contagious} and political engagement \citep{levy2022misspecified,eliaz2026news}.
However, models that are \emph{too simple} can reduce the quality of our choices \citep{10.1257/aer.107.4.967,han2024minds}.
At the same time, experts may have their own incentives to shape how we perceive the impacts of those choices.

This paper studies how we learn from models and data provided by experts who want to make us optimistic.
A receiver decides whether to adopt an initiative.
If she adopts the initiative, she must make a choice to maximize an outcome.
She hires an expert sender, who collects data and reports the best-fitting model of how her choices map to outcomes under the initiative.
The sender wants her to adopt the initiative.
He is either ``accurate,'' and flexibly estimates the mapping from choices to outcomes on the data, or ``simple,'' and calibrates the curvature of his model to a fixed value.
Since the receiver knows the model might be simple, she also entertains alternative models that could have produced his data.
The key insight is that by choosing which data to collect, the sender can manipulate what alternative models the receiver considers, making her optimistic about choices his data do not cover and thus more likely to adopt the initiative.

I show two sets of results.
First, I find that a simple sender strategically collects data about extreme choices that the receiver is unlikely to make.
The reason is that the spread of his data determines where alternative models agree with his original model.
Because these alternatives differ from his model in their curvature, the sender can choose the spread of his data to ensure that they bend above his original model at intermediate choices.
This increases the receiver's perceived expected utility under the initiative, making her more likely to adopt it.

Second, I find that simple senders create a positive externality for accurate senders.
An accurate sender benefits if the receiver suspects his model might be simple, since she then entertains both the true model and more optimistic alternatives.
I show that this benefit is increasing in how far a simple sender's curvature is from the receiver's prior expectation, and is maximized when accurate senders are surrounded by simple senders who assume extremely low or high curvature, and none with intermediate curvature.
These include \emph{linear regressions}, which assume no curvature, and \emph{worst-case scenarios}, which assume the costs of actions are as high as possible.


\paragraph{Model Overview}
A receiver --- such as a policymaker, CEO, investor, or voter --- is debating whether to launch an initiative --- such as a tax reform or a round of layoffs --- or to take an outside option.
If she launches the initiative, she must choose an action $x$ --- such as a tax rate or number of workers to lay off --- to maximize an outcome $y$ --- such as the value of a public good or profits.
She believes the true functional form mapping $x$ to $y$ is given by a set of data-generating processes (DGPs) that capture a tradeoff between a linear benefit and a convex cost:
\begin{align*}
y = \alpha_0+\alpha_1x - \gamma x^2.	
\end{align*}
The parameter $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$ represents the magnitude of the cost and, relatedly, the \emph{curvature} of the tradeoff between high and low $x$ values.

The receiver hires a sender to acquire information for her.
This information takes the form of a \emph{model}: a functional form mapping choices $x$ to outcomes $y$, which is in turn estimated on data.
The sender first samples that data.
He chooses a distribution of choices $x$ to collect information about.
He then observes the corresponding $y$ values, producing data $(y,x)$ which are unobservable to the receiver.
The sender then fits a model to the data.
He has a private type that determines the set of models he can fit.
He is either ``accurate'' or ``simple.''
An accurate sender considers the same models as the receiver.
A simple sender considers similar models, but he is \emph{misspecified} and calibrates the curvature parameter $\gamma$ to a predetermined, \emph{fixed value}.
If he assumes $\gamma$ is low, he \emph{neglects} tradeoffs the receiver considers.
For example, if he assumes $\gamma=0$, he runs a linear regression of $y$ on $x$.
If he assumes $\gamma$ is very high, he \emph{exaggerates} tradeoffs between choices and outcomes.
For example, if he assumes $\gamma=\overline{\gamma}$, he assumes the convexity of costs is as large as possible, which I refer to as a ``worst-case scenario'' model.
A simple sender believes that the true data-generating process is given by the value he calibrates to.
He thus evaluates the receiver's response as if his model were correct.

Given the data he collects, the sender provides the receiver with the best-fitting model to those data.
Crucially, the receiver cannot simply take his data and fit her own model to it.
This means that she requires the expertise of someone like the sender to interpret data.
The sender, in turn, is bound by scientific or professional norms --- or can be audited by an external party --- that ensure he provides the best-fitting model.
This still permits the sender scope to manipulate the receiver's beliefs through the initial \emph{choice of data}.

The receiver is Bayesian: she observes the sender's model, updates over the set of models she considers, and, if she launches the initiative, makes the choice that maximizes her expected utility.
She launches the initiative if this expected utility exceeds the value of her outside option.
The sender wants her to launch the initiative.
For instance, a consultant gains credit and reputation for helping launch a reform, and a financial advisor earns fees when a client invests, regardless of the eventual outcome.
The sender thus wants to make her \emph{optimistic} in the sense of maximizing her \emph{perceived} expected utility from launching it.
The sender is Bayesian as well.
The accurate sender has the same prior beliefs as the receiver, while the simple sender believes the value of curvature he assumes is the true value.

\paragraph{Receiver Learning and Sender Sampling}
I show that the receiver's learning depends on the interaction between the sender's model simplicity and how he collects data.
If the receiver knew the model came from an accurate sender, she would take it as the true mapping between $x$ and $y$.
But because it may have come from a simple, misspecified sender, she also entertains alternative models that could have rationalized his data.
Since a simple sender calibrates curvature to a fixed $\gamma$, these alternatives differ in their curvature and, as a result, in their intercept and slope.

The key is that every alternative model the receiver finds plausible satisfies two constraints: its \emph{mean} and its average \emph{first derivative} over the sender's data must match the sender's model.
Suppose the sender's underlying data sampled choices $x$ from a distribution with mean $x_\delta$ and variance $\sigma_\delta^2$.
All plausible models must have the same derivative as the sender's original model at $x_\delta$, and also coincide with the original model at $x_\delta - \sigma_\delta$ and $x_\delta + \sigma_\delta$.
The spread of the underlying data determines where these alternative models lie relative to the original model, shown in Figure \ref{fig:intro}.
The solid curve indicates the sender's original model and the dashed curves alternative models. 

When the sender samples data in a small neighborhood of a single point, every plausible model is \emph{tangent} to the sender's model at $x_\delta$ (left panel).
I refer to this as \emph{local} sampling: the data describe the relationship between $x$ and $y$ only in a narrow range of choices.
When the sender instead samples data with a large variance, the plausible models are \emph{secants} that cross the sender's model at $x_\delta \pm \sigma_\delta$ (right panel).
When data are sampled with the largest possible variance, I refer to this as \emph{dispersed} sampling, in which case the data are bimodal and mostly describe extreme choices.

The receiver's beliefs thus depend on the alternative models she entertains, which the sender can control through how he samples data.
I show that a simple sender whose model has \emph{low curvature}, such as a linear regression, samples \emph{dispersed} data, as in the right panel.
Because his model assumes a low value of $\gamma$, the alternative models the receiver entertains are mostly \emph{steeper} than the sender's, shown by the orange dashed lines.
Under local sampling, these steeper models would lie \emph{below} his model.
Under dispersed sampling (right panel), they lie \emph{above} his model for a large set of interior values.
So, for the same model, the receiver's \emph{perceived} expected utility rises the more dispersed his data are.
This requires placing substantial mass on extreme choices, some of which lie outside the receiver's choice set.

A simple sender who estimates a \emph{high curvature} model, such as a worst-case scenario, samples \emph{locally}.
Upon seeing a high curvature model, the receiver mostly entertains alternative models with \emph{flatter} curvature.
Under local sampling (left panel), these flatter models lie above his model. Under dispersed sampling, they would lie below it.
Local sampling therefore makes the receiver more optimistic.
Moreover, he samples in a small neighborhood of either the receiver's lowest or highest feasible choice, where he expects his model to be steepest.

This combination of model simplicity and strategic sampling benefits the accurate sender.
Because the receiver cannot tell whether a model came from a simple or an accurate sender, she entertains alternative models even when the model is in fact accurate.
Since simple senders sample strategically, these alternatives deliver higher expected utility on average than the original model itself.
The receiver thus weighs not only the true model, which the accurate sender knows to be correct, but also alternatives more optimistic than the truth.
This makes her more likely to adopt the initiative, which benefits the accurate sender.

I then extend the baseline model to multidimensional choices and show that simple senders collect data that maximize collinearity between variables.
If a sender assumes a low \emph{interaction} effect between two variables, he samples data so that they are as correlated as possible; if he assumes a high interaction effect, he makes them as anticorrelated as possible.
The intuition is that if the sender's model assumes away (negative) interactions, the sample mean in the sender's underlying data is in part driven by a negative interaction of two highly correlated variables.
If the receiver were to unwind this correlation, the sample mean would be higher than what she initially observed.
The opposite logic holds when the sender's model assumes a high interaction effect.
This means that senders who estimate simple models collect datasets that are heavily unbalanced.

I additionally study several extensions to the model.
First, I show that the value of information from hiring a simple sender depends on the \emph{location} of his data: it is maximized when he samples at or beyond the receiver's prior optimal choice, whereas in equilibrium he samples closer to an extreme.
Second, I show that the receiver's ex-ante \emph{forecast} error is minimized not at the local or dispersed sampling that simple senders choose, but at an intermediate amount that is never chosen in equilibrium.
Thus, strategic data sampling not only reduces the value of the information the receiver obtains, but also makes her forecasts less accurate.
The Appendix studies further extensions to more general type spaces and functional forms.


\paragraph{The Supply of Simple Senders}
The baseline model shows that the accurate sender gains from being mistaken for a simple sender, in that this increases the receiver's perceived expected utility from making the right choice and thus makes her more likely to adopt the initiative.
However, I show that this gain varies with a simple sender's type, i.e., the value of $\gamma$ the simple sender uses to calibrate his model's curvature.
If the accurate sender could \emph{choose} which simple senders operate alongside him, which would he choose?
Formally, what \emph{distribution} of simple sender types would maximize the accurate sender's utility?
I use the answer to this question to characterize what sorts of models simple senders will supply in equilibrium and how frequently the receiver will see different simple models.

In practice, the closest analogue of this problem is one where an accurate sender is able to hire additional simple senders to work alongside him.
A senior consultant, for instance, may build a team by hiring junior consultants who analyze problems in simple ways. In turn, his clients may be unsure as to how closely he supervised the model the team provides them.
The same logic applies more broadly: a politician may propagate simple narratives in the media through extremist co-partisans, or an AI platform may deploy simpler LLMs alongside more complex ones.

I show that the accurate sender benefits the most when he is surrounded entirely with simple senders who assume very low curvature values (close to $\gamma=0$) or very high curvature values (close to $\overline{\gamma}$), and no senders who assume intermediate curvature values.
This is illustrated graphically in Figure \ref{fig:introtypes}.
The intuition is that the accurate sender's gain from being misperceived as simple is higher the farther his model's curvature departs from the receiver's prior expectation $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
Thus, an accurate sender will prefer to place mass on simple senders who calibrate their curvature to very extreme $\gamma$ values, and no mass on those who calibrate their models to intermediate values.
The simple models a receiver encounters are therefore systematically extreme and \emph{polarized}.
In the limit, as the receiver becomes confident she faces the accurate sender, the only remaining simple models are linear regressions and worst-case scenarios.
The prevalence of these extreme models need not reflect experts' ignorance or lack of data.
Instead, it can arise because extreme models are the ones that make accurate experts look best.

\paragraph{Literature}\label{section:literature}
This paper broadly adds to our understanding of how we learn from externally-provided models and data, and how incentives to make us optimistic distort the design of information through strategic data collection and model simplification.
Its key contribution is to characterize what \emph{sorts} of data model senders may strategically sample, how incentives to strategically sample data are informed by the simplicity of their models' curvature, and how the interaction of both can generate gains from systematically pursuing highly simplistic data-generating processes.

Many of the examples in the paper concern \emph{delegation} to experts.
There is a long line of literature --- particularly in organizations and finance --- studying this process, including mistrust in experts \citep{cheng2022distrust}, uncertainty about expertise \citep{kishishita2020not}, and its interaction with persuasion \citep{kolotilin2025persuasion}.
My contribution to this literature is to study how delegation and communication operate when an expert's recommendation comes in the form of a \emph{model} organizing endogenously collected \emph{data}.
This conceptually contrasts my paper with work like \citet{deimen2019delegated}, who also study delegation to experts, but are concerned with the information that can be gleaned from experts' reports when their preferences may be misaligned with decisionmakers'.
The most closely related paper in the finance literature is \citet{li2018delegation}, who study delegation when an investment advisor provides a model of returns to a client.

While there are many papers that study learning from models, much of this literature takes as given the past history of data that a model is fit on --- whether those data are exogenous or endogenously generated by a principal's or agent's choices \citep{eliaz2020model, schwartzstein2021using, levy2022misspecified}.
My contribution is to study a sender who first chooses a data collection rule and then provides the best-fitting model to those data.
Thus, although data collection is endogenous and unobserved by the receiver, the sender's hands are still tied by his initial data collection.
This assumption contrasts with other papers where data are endogenously generated either through follow-on evidence generation \citep{dai2026bayesian}, selective disclosure \citep{felgenhauer2014strategic}, nonrandom sampling \citep{di2021strategic}, or sequential data acquisition with endogenous stopping \citep{henry2019research}.
These papers show that the ability of a sender to persuade a receiver often depends on the sorts of signals about data a sender can transmit.
In my setting, I show that a sender's desire to make the receiver optimistic coupled with model simplicity creates a tractable characterization of data sampling: the sender either locally samples data in the smallest possible neighborhood of an extreme receiver choice, or as distantly as possible across a wide range of receiver choices.

The use of strategic \emph{sampling} interacts with a sender's model simplicity to make the receiver believe her expected utility is \emph{higher} than under the model provided by the sender.
This channel through which a receiver can be made more optimistic is thus related to model simplicity and data sampling, as opposed to departures from a status quo or the inclusion of confounding variables, such as in \citet{eliaz2020model}.
This also distinguishes the paper from work where a receiver selects between different models with different sample sizes and more or less complex models \citep{montiel2022competing}.

My paper shows that gains from model simplicity or strategic sampling can arise without interpreting the role of noise in driving data-generating processes.
This finding contrasts with papers that investigate gains from model-based persuasion that involve trading off model fit and belief manipulability --- such as \citet{schwartzstein2021using}, \citet{aina2023tailored}, \citet{barron2024narrative}, and \citet{aina2025weighting}.
The present paper does, however, show that senders who make receivers more optimistic often create greater \emph{forecast} error for a receiver, suggesting that receiver optimism may come at the cost of uncertainty over models.

The particular angle the present paper takes is closely related to omitted variable bias --- in that a sender cannot (or refuses to) fully model a tradeoff in the way a receiver would like.
Thus, this paper is related to an older literature on omitted variable bias in econometrics \citep[see][and references therein]{theil1957specification, altonji2005selection, oster2019unobservable}.
It also connects the paper to theoretical work like \citet{levy2022misspecified}, \citet{gleyze2022value}, and \citet{eliaz2025false} --- who show that model misspecification may interact with competitive pressures or uncertainty about data estimation to generate a range of outcomes from improved communication and engagement to political cycles and the prevalence of false models.
More broadly, this paper studies a model where a receiver entertains a well-specified model and a sender estimates a misspecified model.
It thus contributes to a deeper theoretical literature on Berk-Nash equilibrium and learning with misspecified beliefs \citep{alonso2016bayesian, esponda2016berk, spiegler2016bayesian, heidhues2018unrealistic, mailath2020learning, bohren2021learning,fudenberg2021limit,de2022non,levy2022persuasion,bohren2025misspecified}. 

A key finding of this paper is that senders may generally benefit from providing \emph{simpler} models to receivers.
The idea that senders can benefit from simple models is also captured by \citet{perez2012complicating}, \citet{bloedel2018persuasion}, \citet{lipnowski2020attention}, \citet{eliaz2021strategic}, and \citet{burkovskaya2026causal}. 
While many of these papers are driven by behavioral assumptions like bounded rationality or rational inattention, the findings in this paper are related to sampling and simplicity: given a sender's model, a receiver will entertain additional models.
The set of these additional models is determined by the \emph{simplicity} of the sender's model, and the expected utility they generate for the receiver increases or decreases with the spread of the sender's data sampling.
In particular, the present paper shows that a sender informed of the true model of the world can benefit by making the receiver think his model was estimated by a sender who had an artificially simple model \emph{and} strategically sampled.
The most closely related paper is \citet{gleyze2022value}, who show that an informed sender can often gain by delegating model provision to a (potentially misspecified) sender.

Finally, because of its reliance on models as information structures, the present paper also adds to a longer literature on (Bayesian) persuasion \citep{kamenica2011bayesian}, as well as persuasion and competition \citep{gentzkow2017bayesian,wang2013bayesian}.
In particular, receivers in the present paper are uncertain as to the source of data or the broader class of models a sender entertained.
This connects the paper to models of persuasion and cheap talk with \emph{uncertainty} about information structures \citep{huang2024learning,min2026information} and ambiguity aversion \citep{colo2024communicating}.



\section{Model Setup}\label{section:setup}
I begin by laying out a game between a single receiver $R$ (she) and an expert sender $S$ (he). $R$ is debating whether to launch an initiative.
If she adopts the initiative, she must make a choice to maximize an outcome associated with the initiative, and enlists the services of $S$.
$S$ collects data and provides $R$ a (potentially misspecified) model describing those data.
$R$ sees $S$'s model, updates, and then makes the choice that maximizes the expected outcome.
Throughout, I use the running example from the introduction where $R$ is a policymaker tasked with designing a carbon tax, and $S$ is a consultant who models the effects of different taxes.


\subsection{Receiver and Models}
$R$ makes a decision $d$. She either sticks to a status quo $s$, or adopts an initiative.
If she adopts the initiative, she makes a choice from a set $X=[\xlow,\overline{x}] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ for $\overline{x}>\xlow$.
Each choice $x\in X$ maps to an outcome $y \in \mathbb{R}$, while the status quo yields utility $u_R \in \mathbb{R}$, which is private information to the receiver.
Her full set of decisions is given by $X\cup\{s\}$.

Ex-ante, $R$ does not know the relationship between $y$ and $x$. However, she believes that the value of $y$ given $x$ can be described by a data-generating process (DGP) $y = f_R(x)$ for a function $f_R : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.
We refer to $f_R$ as a \textbf{model}.
We denote $F_R$ as the set of \emph{models} $f_R$ that $R$ entertains.
$R$ adopts the initiative if and only if her expected outcome from her best choice in $X$ exceeds $u_R$.

I assume that for each $f_R \in F_R$, we have
	\begin{align*}
	f_R(x) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x - \gamma x^2,	
	\end{align*}
	where $\alpha_0 \sim p_0^R$, $\alpha_1 \sim p_1^R$, and $\gamma \sim p_\gamma^R$ on $[0,\overline{\gamma}] :=\Gamma$ for continuous priors $p_0^R,p_1^R,p_\gamma^R$.
	Her prior over each of the three components is independent.
	$p_0^R$ is uniform on $[\underline{\alpha}_0,\overline{\alpha}_0]$ for $\underline{\alpha}_0$ arbitrarily negative and $\overline{\alpha}_0$ arbitrarily large.
	$p_1^R$ is uniform on $[\underline{\alpha}_1,\overline{\alpha}_1]$ and $p_\gamma^R$ is symmetric.	
	I assume that $\underline{\alpha}_1\leq 0$ and $\overline{\alpha}_1\geq 2 \overline{\gamma}\overline{x}$.
	
Each of $R$'s models is concave, and can be represented as an affine function less a convex, quadratic loss.\footnote{I assume a quadratic form for tractability but show that the central intuitions generalize in Appendix \ref{section:general}.}
The magnitude of this quadratic loss is governed by the parameter $\gamma$.
$\gamma$ represents the \emph{curvature} of the data-generating process, and hence how sharp the receiver's \emph{tradeoffs} are.
%The prior over the set of constant terms is uniform, i.e. $R$'s true uncertainty is over the curvature of $f_R$.

The receiver's goal is to maximize the expected value of $y$.
Let $q$ denote her belief over $F_R$, i.e. her (joint) belief over these three parameters.
Her expected utility from a decision $d$ is given by:
\begin{align*}
U_q(d) = \begin{cases}
	\mathbb{E}_{q}[f_R(d)] \qquad & d \in X,
	\\
	u_R \qquad & d = s,
 \end{cases}
\end{align*}
and she makes a decision $d \in X \cup \{s\}$ to maximize $U_q(d)$.

In the running example, $R$ is a policymaker who is debating a carbon tax reform.
Her outside option $s$ is to do nothing.
If she launches a reform, however, she must also choose a carbon tax $x$.
$y$ represents the welfare of a representative household or voter. 
Higher taxes reduce emissions and improve health outcomes, but may also increase energy prices and reduce private sector activity.
The single choice of a tax rate $x$ may have different consequences for welfare, depending on the \emph{aggregate} of these different effects.
If she launches the initiative, the policymaker would like to balance these effects and choose the tax rate that maximizes expected welfare.

However, there are many other interpretations of the receiver and her preferences.
If $R$ is an investor, she cares about how much money she can make by investing in a choice $x$.
If $R$ is a CEO restructuring her workforce, she cares about the effect of layoffs on profits.
If $R$ is a voter, she cares about the impact of a policy $x$ on her wellbeing.\footnote{I utilize these simple preferences for higher $y$ in the baseline model, but Section \ref{section:forecast} extends them to evaluate settings where $R$ also values \emph{forecast accuracy}, and thus knowing the correct model of the world.}


\subsection{Sender and Data}
The receiver $R$ hires a sender $S$ to provide her with information about the relationship between $x$ and $y$.
This sender is an \emph{expert} --- such as a scientist or experienced consultant --- who provides domain expertise and access to data.
Of particular interest is also the case where $S$ is an AI program, such as a large language model (LLM) that $R$ can interface with.
The key idea is that, as an expert, the sender is able to gather and interpret data for the receiver, which she may find difficult to do on her own.

The sender's preference is to maximize the probability with which the receiver adopts the initiative.
While he does not know the receiver's utility $u_R$ from the status quo, he has a prior that $u_R \sim \mathcal{U}[\underline{u},\overline{u}]$ where $\underline{u}$ is arbitrarily small and $\overline{u}$ arbitrarily large.

The sender has a private type $\theta$ determining the sorts of models he can provide the receiver.
The order of events is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Nature draws $S$'s type, the true DGP $f_R$, and the receiver's outside option $u_R$.
\item $S$ samples data and calculates the model of best fit, given his type.
\item $S$ provides the best-fitting model to $R$. $R$ updates about the true data-generating process.
\item $R$ chooses whether to stick to the status quo $s$ or adopt the initiative.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If she sticks to $s$, she receives utility $u_R$ and the sender receives his utility.
\item If she adopts the initiative, she makes a choice in $X$ and the sender receives his utility. Uncertainty resolves, and $R$ receives her utility based on the realized outcome of her choice.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
I now provide more detail about each of these components.

\paragraph{Sender's Type}
A sender's private type $\theta$ affects the \emph{simplicity} of the models he is capable of providing to $R$.
We denote the type space by $\Theta$.
There are two varieties of sender.
The first is an \textbf{accurate} sender.
We denote his type $a$.
This sender provides a model in the set
\begin{align*}
F_a := \{f_a \in F_R : f_a(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x - \delta x^2, (\beta_0,\beta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \delta \in \Gamma \}.
\end{align*}
We note that $F_a = F_R$, i.e. the accurate sender is estimating \emph{exactly} the same models as $R$.
Additionally, I assume he has the same prior over models as the receiver.
The ex-ante probability of seeing a type $a$ sender is $p_a$.

The second variety of sender is a \textbf{simple} sender.
This sender has a type $\delta$, where $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
A simple sender is limited in how he can estimate the \emph{curvature} of models.
\footnote{Appendix \ref{section:intervals} relaxes this assumption and allows types to be intervals of curvature values, so that a sender may neglect or exaggerate some tradeoffs while correctly estimating others. The main results are robust to this richer type space. However, the design problem studied in Section \ref{section:simple} shows that if a receiver saw a simple sender in equilibrium, she would only see one of these \emph{singleton} types.}
A type $\delta$ sender is able to provide $R$ a model in the set $F_{\delta}$.
\begin{align*}
F_{\delta} := \{f_\delta \in F_R : f_\delta(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x - \delta x^2, (\beta_0,\beta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \}.
\end{align*}
The interpretation is that a simple, type $\delta$ sender \textbf{calibrates} the curvature of the convex loss to $\delta$, and does not estimate it on the data.
 For example, the consultant may calibrate the effect of carbon taxes on energy prices using a fixed elasticity from the literature.
 Or, he may believe that the cost of actions is always given by $\delta x^2$, based on his own idiosyncratic experiences.
 The key is that the simple sender believes the \emph{curvature} of the relationship between $x$ and $y$ takes a fixed value.
 
We denote by $P_{f_S}^\delta$ a type $\delta$ sender's prior over models $f_S \in F_\delta$.
He has a prior over $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$.
We assume his prior over $\beta_1$ is identical to the receiver's prior over $\alpha_1$.
The simple sender thus estimates \emph{misspecified models}; he truthfully believes that his way of modeling the problem (in simple terms) is the right way.

The receiver sees a simple sender with ex-ante probability $1-p_a$.
A simple sender's type is in turn drawn from a distribution $T$ over $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$ known to $R$.
If $T$ is continuous with pdf $t$, the ex-ante density of a type $\delta$ simple sender is thus $(1-p_a)t(\delta)$.
In turn, the full type space $\Theta$ is given by $\Theta = \Gamma \cup \{a\}$.

\paragraph{Two Simple Types}
There are two simple sender types that will be of particular interest to our analysis.
The first is a type $\delta = 0$ sender. He estimates models of the form $y = \beta_0+\beta_1x$.
This sender is effectively running a linear regression of $y$ on $x$, even though the receiver believes the true relationship is given by a nonlinear DGP.
In the example, the policymaker may believe small carbon taxes generate meaningful reductions in pollution without adverse effects, but very high taxes lead to higher energy prices or lower business activity.
These nonlinear effects would be neglected if a consultant presented a linear regression of welfare on tax rates. 

The second is a type $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$ sender.
He estimates models of the form $y = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\overline{\gamma}x^2$, where $\overline{\gamma}$ is fixed at its highest possible value.
This sender estimates a ``worst-case'' model: he always assumes the magnitude of the convex loss is as large as possible and, hence, assumes the \emph{curvature} of the tradeoff between choices and outcomes is as sharp as possible.
In our example, the consultant may assume that higher taxes always have maximally deleterious effects on the business sector, which the policymaker may believe is unlikely.

\paragraph{Sender's Data}
Before providing a model to the receiver, the sender first collects data about certain choices.
He selects a distribution of choices $x$ to collect information about.
He then observes the $y$ value associated with that distribution of choices.
This gives him a dataset he can use to estimate his model.

Prior to estimating a model, $S$ chooses a data sampling strategy $\mu$, which is a distribution over $\mathbb{R}$.
Writing $x_\mu := \mathbb{E}_\mu[x]$, the set of distributions he can sample from is given by the set $M$.
\begin{align*}
M := \Big\{\mu \in \Delta(\mathbb{R}) : \; x_\mu \in X,\;\; x - x_\mu \overset{d}{=} x_\mu - x,\;\; \mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq[x_\mu-\overline{\sigma},x_\mu+\overline{\sigma}],\;\; \Var_\mu(x) \geq \underline{\sigma}^2 \Big\},
\end{align*}
where $\underline{\sigma}>0$ is arbitrarily small and $\overline{\sigma}$ is large.
That is, $\mu$ is symmetric about a mean in the receiver's choice set $X$, lies within $\overline{\sigma}$ of that mean, and has variance bounded away from zero, but can otherwise take any shape.
\footnote{The assumption of symmetry is used for tractability to discipline the set of models the receiver will entertain, but the results generalize. Relatedly, the constraint that $\sigma>0$ is a regularity assumption made to ensure learning is structured in equilibrium.}

We will refer to a type $\theta$ sender's choice of $\mu$ as $\mu_\theta$. We denote its mean $x_\theta := \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}[x]$ and variance $\sigma_\theta^2:=\Var_{\mu_\theta}(x)$.
I note that $\mu$ does allow the sender to sample information about $x$ values outside of the receiver's choice set $X$.
This is consistent with the interpretation that the receiver's choices are ``bounded'' in a broader space of potential choices, and that choices outside of $X$ are concretely ``irrelevant'' to the receiver's eventual choice.


Given this sampling strategy over \emph{choices}, $S$ then directly observes the true joint distribution of $(y|x,x)$, subject to observational noise.
Formally, if the true data-generating process is $f_R^*$, he observes a joint distribution $D(y|x,x)$, where the marginal distribution of $x$ is given by $D_x = \mu_\theta$ and the conditional distribution of $y$ given $x$ is given by $D_y(y|x) = f_R^*(x)+\eta$, where
$\eta$ is a mean-zero noise term with unknown variance.
This is equivalent to $S$ observing an arbitrarily large (or infinite) number of i.i.d. draws from the true data-generating process.
\footnote{I allow $S$ to observe the distribution of data to shut down the role of idiosyncratic data draws in shaping posterior beliefs.}

\paragraph{Sender's Information}
With the sender's type and sampling strategy in hand, we define the model $S$ provides to $R$.
%We say that a model $f_S$ \textbf{fits the data} with respect to $\delta$ and a data-sampling strategy $\mu_\delta$ if:
%\begin{align*}
%\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_S(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{D(\mu_\delta)}\big[y\big].
%\end{align*}
%Our key assumption is that $S$ must provide a model $f_S$ that fits the data.
%Additionally, 
We say that a model $f_S$ \textbf{best fits the data} with respect to $\theta$ and a data-sampling strategy $\mu_\theta$ if:
\begin{align*}
f_S= \arg\min_{f \in F_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{D(\mu_\theta)}\big[\big(y-f(x)\big)^2\big].
\end{align*}
For example, when $\theta = \delta = 0$, $f_S$ represents the \emph{best-fitting linear model} to the data.\footnote{The existence of a best-fitting model is given by the Hilbert Projection Theorem.}

I assume $S$ always provides $R$ the best-fitting model to the data.
There are several interpretations of this assumption. The first is that the sender makes an honest effort to provide the receiver with a best-fitting model, conditional on the data he collected.
The second is that, although the receiver may be unable to observe or interpret the raw data herself, the sender's model will be audited at some point.
That audit could be performed by the receiver's organization, or externally by a broader scientific community.

In the running example, the assumption that the consultant provides the best-fitting model given his type can be justified in three ways.
First, imagine that he is a trained economist who relies on well-known methods such as regressions or structural estimation.
Even if the policymaker cannot interpret the raw data herself, her organization can request a replication package to verify his methodology.
Second, his work is subject to scrutiny from the broader scientific community --- in this case, the economics profession --- which would expose fabricated data or poor model fit.
Third, the policymaker can work back and forth with the consultant until he justifies why his model is the best way to describe the data.
In each case, the receiver learns about the data only through the sender's model, which allows me to isolate how model \emph{simplicity} and strategic \emph{data collection} shape learning and sender behavior.


\paragraph{Sender's Preferences}
The sender's goal is to maximize the probability with which the receiver adopts the initiative.
Formally, he receives a utility normalized to $1$ if she adopts the initiative and $0$ otherwise.
Because $S$ does not know the value of $R$'s status quo outside option, but believes it is distributed uniformly on $[\underline{u},\overline{u}]$, his expected utility is linear in the receiver's perceived expected utility from the initiative.

Suppose $S$ provides a model $f_S \in F_\theta$.
$R$ has a posterior belief over the true DGP $f_R \in F_R$ as a function of $f_S$, which also depends on her beliefs about the sender's type and his choice of data $\mu$.
Let $x^*(f_S)$ be the receiver's choice of $x \in X$ given her posterior beliefs, conditional on adopting the initiative.
Up to a positive affine transformation, a type $\theta$ sender's utility is then
\begin{align*}
U^\theta(f_S) = \mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S}^R[f_R(x^*(f_S))],
\end{align*}
which represents the receiver's \emph{perceived} expected utility at her consequent choice.

The interpretation is that the sender would like to make the receiver \emph{optimistic} about the expected impact of her choices.
In our running example, imagine that the policymaker is deciding whether to launch a new carbon tax program.
If she is sufficiently optimistic about the impact the program will have, she is more likely to launch it.
If the program is launched, the consultant can take credit for helping with it, which can bring him future business, regardless of the specifics of the program's \emph{design}.\footnote{One of the key insights of the paper is that the receiver entertains not only the sender's original model but also alternative models that, on average, deliver strictly higher expected utility. Thus, in equilibrium, the receiver's perceived expected utility tends to exceed that implied by the sender's original model.}

These preferences apply more broadly across a variety of settings.
For example, a management consulting firm may inform a CEO's decision to engage in mass hiring, and can take credit for that decision.
If the sender is a financial advisor, he gains from making his clients think their investment returns will be high, so that they invest using his services.
If the sender is a politician, making voters optimistic about the effects of a new law he would pass if reelected may make them more willing to turn out to vote for him.
Finally, if the sender is an AI chatbot, optimistic users are more likely to engage with the chatbot and, as a result, purchase a premium subscription.

\paragraph{Equilibrium}
Given $p_a$ and $T$, an \textbf{equilibrium} consists of a sampling strategy $\mu_\theta^* \in M$ and a receiver belief $\hat{\mu}_\theta \in M$ about it for each type $\theta \in \Theta$, and, for each model $f_S$, a posterior $q(f_R|f_S)$ over $F_R$ and a decision $d^*(f_S) \in X\cup\{s\}$, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A type $\theta$ sender who samples according to $\mu_\theta^*$ provides the model $f_S \in F_\theta$ that best fits the data.
\item $q(f_R|f_S)$ is derived by Bayes' rule from $R$'s prior, $p_a$, $T$, and $\{\hat{\mu}_\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}$.
\item $d^*(f_S) \in \arg\max_{d \in X\cup\{s\}} U_{q(\cdot|f_S)}(d)$.
\item For each $\theta \in \Theta$,
\begin{align*}
\mu_\theta^* \in \arg\max_{\mu \in M}\ \mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta\Big[\max_{x \in X}\ \mathbb{E}_{\hat{q}(f_R|f_S;\mu)}[f_R(x)]\Big],
\end{align*}
where $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta$ is taken with respect to the type $\theta$ sender's beliefs, and $\hat{q}(f_R|f_S;\mu)$ is his belief about $R$'s posterior when he samples according to $\mu$.
\item $\hat{\mu}_\theta = \mu_\theta^*$ for each $\theta\in\Theta$.
\end{enumerate}
The sampling strategy $\mu$ is observable to the receiver, although the realized data are not, so that the receiver's posterior responds to deviations in $\mu$.
\subsection{Additional Comments}

The receiver's learning is affected by two key frictions. The first is that the sender's models are \emph{simple}: $S$ may be unable to think about problems exactly the way $R$ would like.
In the two-simple-type example, $S$ either estimates a linear model that \emph{totally} neglects tradeoffs, or a worst-case model that totally exaggerates them.
This assumption is consistent with several interpretations.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Tractability}: the sender's model may represent the culmination of a highly complex data synthesis process that makes it difficult to directly and fully estimate more complicated models. In the running example, the consultant may perform complex climate simulations which in turn feed into an intricate structural model before delivering a recommendation. Simple models may emerge as a necessity to make data synthesis tractable.
\item \textbf{Calibration}: instead of estimating parameters organically on data, $S$ may calibrate his models' curvature to a fixed value, such as an elasticity from the literature. Relatedly, the sender may believe that, based on his own experience, the true magnitude of the receiver's cost is always given by a fixed value.
\item \textbf{Strategic behavior}: the sender may be able to make the receiver \emph{more optimistic} if she thinks his model was a simplified model. This will be a central result of the paper (Section \ref{section:simple}).
\end{enumerate}

The second friction is that the receiver does not simply request data from the sender and fit the model herself. The interpretation is that the receiver cannot process raw data on her own, and precisely hires an expert to interpret those data for her. That is, the only way for her to process information is through the lens of an expert's model. Additionally:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The sender may have access to a proprietary dataset, which cannot be directly disclosed. Commonly used datasets like those from Nielsen provide proprietary information on ``audience segments,'' for instance, to help advertisers run targeted campaigns \citep{nielsen2024audiencesegments}.
\item The sender's model may also embed \emph{expertise}. The strategy literature suggests that firms often develop internal ways of approaching a problem based on analogous situations they have experienced in the past \citep{carroll2024strategy}. Accordingly, a consulting firm's recommendations may be informed by experience working on cases similar to each other or drawn from many different cases and past contexts.
\item The sender is an AI program. It is often difficult for users of these programs (or even their developers) to understand the mapping from internal data to recommendations \citep{zuccarelli2024blackbox}. In this sense, the mapping from the data to a model is a \emph{black box}.
\end{enumerate}
What matters is that $R$ knows $S$ has access to useful data, but must infer how those data may have been strategically sampled when estimating a model.\footnote{
Accordingly, I will be interested in exactly \emph{what sorts of data} the sender will collect in equilibrium, and \emph{how} that data collection interacts with the simplicity of his models.}
The case where $S$ provides linear regression models is also of particular interest, given their common usage in the business world, economics, and data-driven social sciences.

Additionally, $R$ may also be unable (or unwilling) to expend organizational time and resources in collecting, understanding, and synthesizing complex data.
Instead, she delegates the collection of data and its synthesis into a model to the sender, expediting her decision-making process in the interest of convenience, but potentially at the cost of information quality.


I focus largely on settings where the nonlinear component of $f_R$ is quadratic. While this is done for tractability, Appendix \ref{section:general} discusses the assumptions necessary to generalize the logic.

\section{Analysis}\label{section:learning}
This section first characterizes the set of models that the receiver entertains, and then shows how senders strategically sample data.
I show that simple senders who assume low-curvature models --- such as linear regressions --- collect \emph{dispersed data} that place mass bimodally on extreme choices, which may lie at or beyond the edges of the receiver's choice set.
Simple senders who assume high-curvature models --- like worst-case scenarios --- collect \emph{local data} in a small neighborhood of either the receiver's lowest choice ($x=0$) or highest choice ($x=\overline{x}$).
In either case, the data underlying these models disproportionately contain information about choices that will be \emph{irrelevant} to the receiver's eventual choice.

Consequently, I show that accurate senders gain from being misperceived as simple senders, in that the existence of simple senders allows their models to make the receiver more \emph{optimistic} than they would otherwise, and thus more likely to adopt the initiative.
The reason is that model simplicity leads the receiver to entertain not just the sender's original model, but also alternative models that could have produced his data.
Strategic sampling ensures that these alternatives deliver higher expected utility.
This means that even when the accurate sender provides the true DGP, the receiver rationally considers these alternative DGPs.
From the perspective of the accurate sender, this makes her more optimistic than the truth.

I then further generalize the baseline model with \emph{multidimensional choices}, and show that simple senders maximize \emph{collinearity} between different variables.
In particular, if a sender \emph{neglects} interaction effects between two variables, he samples data so that they are as \emph{correlated} as possible.
If he \emph{exaggerates} interaction effects, he samples data so that they are as \emph{anticorrelated} as possible.
Thus, although receivers correctly infer the sort of data that senders collect in equilibrium, those data may be \emph{low quality}, in that they suffer from a high degree of collinearity and embed information about choices that will (in expectation) be irrelevant.

\subsection{Receiver's Learning and Behavior}
I begin by characterizing receiver learning as a function of data-sampling strategies and $S$'s type.
\paragraph{Receiver's Updating}
Suppose $S$ provided a model of the form $f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1 x-\delta x^2$ which best fits the data.
If the sender is an accurate sender, this is the true model.
However, if the sender is a simple sender, this may not be the true model.
%In our two-type simple sender example, we have either $\delta = 0$ or $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
We define $\phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a)$ as the set of DGPs that could rationalize the data for $R$, given $f_S$ and the sampling strategies of a type $\delta$ and a type $a$ sender.
\begin{align*}
\phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a) = \bigg\{f_R \in F_R : f_S =\arg\min_{f \in F_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[\big(f_R(x)-f(x)\big)^2\big], \theta \in \{a,\delta\} \bigg\}.	
\end{align*}
%The receiver may entertain additional models beyond $f_S$. $\phi$ describes this set.
If $\theta = a$, then because $F_a = F_R$, the best-fitting model is $f_R$ itself, so that $f_R = f_S$.
If $\theta = \delta$, we necessarily have that for each such $f_R$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_i} \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[\big(f_R(x)-f(x)\big)^2\big] = 0, 
\end{align*}
which yields the following moment conditions familiar from ordinary least squares (OLS):
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x + \delta x^2\big] =0
\label{eq:intercept}
\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta x^2 \big)\big] =0
\label{eq:slope}
\end{align}
Equation (\ref{eq:intercept}) is the moment condition with respect to the intercept, and states that the difference between $f_R$ and $f_S$ must average to zero.
We note that this is the same as the constraint that a model must ``fit the data.''
Equation (\ref{eq:slope}) can be combined with equation (\ref{eq:intercept}) to read that the covariance between $x$ and $f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x + \delta x^2$ is zero.
Both expectations are taken with respect to $\mu_\delta$, which is the type $\delta$ sender's sampling strategy.
The following proposition characterizes the set of DGPs that $R$ entertains given $f_S$, $\delta$, and $\mu_\delta$, i.e., assuming the receiver knows the \emph{simple} type $\delta$ sender's underlying data sampling strategy.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{reduction}
\label{prop:reduction}
Suppose $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$ best fits the data and the simple sender's sampling strategy $\mu_\delta$ has mean and variance $x_\delta$ and $\sigma_\delta^2$. Then, for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a)$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = f_S(x)+(\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big).
\end{align*}
\end{restatable}
The proof of this result is contained in Appendix \ref{appendix:proofs}.
Consider the policymaker and consultant. The consultant's model may be accurate.
In this case, $f_R(x) = f_S(x)$, and the consultant's model is the true model.
However, it is also possible that the sender's model was simple.
Thus, she considers both the consultant's original model and other models that could rationalize the tax-welfare data the consultant saw.

In particular, note that the set of models she considers depends \emph{only} on the sampling strategy of the type $\delta$ sender, not the type $a$ sender.\footnote{The accurate sender's model is simply $f_S$, i.e. the member of this set where $\gamma=\delta$.}
Mathematically, the set of models she considers, given a best-fitting model $f_S$, takes a simple form: they are all the sender's original model plus a constant, less a quadratic term centered at $x_\delta$.
In the case where $\delta = 0$, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x+\gamma\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big)	
\end{align*}
for each $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
In the case where $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\overline{\gamma}x^2 + (\gamma-\overline{\gamma})\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big)	
\end{align*}
for each $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
In both cases, the set of models $R$ considers involves a fixed ``affine component'' plus a constant and quadratic term, which may be positive or negative.
Moreover, all plausible $f_R$s have the same slope at $x_\delta$.

However, across the sampling range, they have the same sample mean and average slope.

\paragraph{Receiver's Decisionmaking}
The receiver's learning takes a relatively simple form: regardless of the limitation on $S$'s ability, $R$ always learns what the data-generating process looks like ``up to the first derivative at $x_\delta$ and a constant.'' 
Moreover, as $\sigma$ changes --- and thus $S$'s sampling strategy changes --- the set of plausible $f_R$s remains identical, except that they shift up or down by a constant.
This means that $R$'s optimal \emph{choice} conditional on adopting the initiative depends only on the probability $\Pr(\theta = a|f_S)$ that the model is accurate, her expectation of $\gamma$ given that the model is simple, and $x_\delta$.
Given $f_S$, this choice has the following expression.
\begin{corollary}
Given $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$ and a type $\delta$ sender's sampling strategy $\mu_\delta$, $R$'s optimal choice, conditional on adopting the initiative, is given by $x^*(f_S)$, where:
\begin{align*}
&x^*(f_S)	= \max\{0,\min\{\tilde{x},\overline{x}\}\}
\\
&\tilde{x} = x_\delta + \frac{f_S'(x_\delta)}{2\big(\Pr(\theta=a|f_S)\,\delta+\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big)}.
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}
We note that $\tilde{x}$ is simply $R$'s optimal interior action, and that $x^*(f_S)$ is this interior choice truncated between $0$ and $\overline{x}$.

In turn, the receiver adopts the initiative and makes a choice $x^*(f_S) \in X$ if $\mathbb{E}^R_{f_R|f_S}[f_R(x^*(f_S))] > u_R$.
Otherwise, she chooses the outside option $s$.

\subsection{Sender's Data Sampling and Model Provision}
I use these insights to show how $S$ chooses the \emph{spread} of his data as a function of his type.
We say that a type $\delta$ sender is a \textbf{low-curvature} sender if $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, i.e. the simple sender's assumed value of curvature is lower than the receiver's prior expectation.
The type $\delta=0$ linear regression sender is low-curvature.
We say that a type $\delta$ sender is a \textbf{high-curvature} sender if $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, i.e. the simple sender's assumed value of curvature is \emph{higher} than the receiver's prior expectation.
The type $\delta=\overline{\gamma}$ worst-case scenario sender is high-curvature.

These definitions allow me to state my main result on strategic sender sampling.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{sampling}
Suppose $S$ is a type $\delta$ simple sender.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $S$ is low-curvature, he collects \emph{dispersed} data about extreme choices. $\mu_\delta^*$ is a bimodal distribution that places mass $\frac{1}{2}$ on each of $x_\delta^*-\overline{\sigma}$ and $x_\delta^*+\overline{\sigma}$, where $x_\delta^* \in \big[0, \mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]\big)$. 
\item If $S$ is high-curvature, he collects \emph{local} data about a single extreme choice. $\mu_\delta^*$ is any distribution in $M$ with variance $\underline{\sigma}^2$, and has mean $x_\delta^*$ equal to either $0$ or $\overline{x}$.
\end{enumerate}
\label{thm:sampling}
\end{restatable}

Theorem \ref{thm:sampling} can be interpreted as follows.
If the sender is low-curvature --- such as when he only provides linear regressions --- he collects data about choices as far away from the center of the receiver's choice space as possible.
In fact, the dataset underlying his model is so dispersed that his data are collected from a two-point distribution, which may even draw only on data outside of the receiver's choice set.

However, if the sender is high-curvature, such as the worst-case scenario sender, the opposite happens.
This sender collects data in a small local neighborhood of a single extreme choice.
In particular, the theorem shows that this sender will sample data locally near either $\overline{x}$ or $0$; his equilibrium choice of $\mu_\delta^*$ is such that $x_\delta^*$ is either $\overline{x}$ or $0$ and $\sigma_\delta^* = \underline{\sigma}$ is as low as possible.

The intuition is driven by the interaction of the curvature of the receiver's additional models with the sender's method of \emph{data sampling}. The logic is shown visually in Figure \ref{fig:samplingintuition} below. 
The top panels (a) and (b) look at the case where $f_S$ is a linear regression of $y$ on $x$, and illustrate the logic for low-curvature senders.
The bottom panels (c) and (d) look at the case where $f_S$ is the worst-case model fitting the data, and illustrate the logic for high-curvature senders.
The smaller graphs below the graphs of $f_S$ and $f_R$ show what the distribution of \emph{data} underlying different choices is.

Recall that when the receiver sees a model $f_S$, she entertains alternative models, which are characterized by the set $\phi$.
The relative location of these alternative models depends on the simple sender's sampling strategy $\mu_\delta$ and, in particular, only on its mean $x_\delta$ and variance $\sigma_\delta^2$.

Suppose a low-curvature $S$ collects data in a small, local neighborhood of a point $x_\delta$.
This is shown in panel (a), where the underlying data of the simple sender $\mu_\delta$ are concentrated near $x_\delta$.
In this case, the variance $\sigma_\delta^2$ of those underlying data is small.
As a result, the alternative plausible models $f_R$ are tangent to $f_S$ at $x_\delta$.
These alternative models have steeper curvature (i.e. a higher $\gamma$ value) than $f_S$, and lie \emph{below} $f_S$: i.e., $f_R(x) \leq f_S(x)$ for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a)$.
Thus, when the variance of the underlying data is \emph{small}, conditional on seeing $f_S$ the receiver's maximal expected utility is \emph{lower} than under $f_S$.

However, in panel (b), the plausible alternative models are the same as in panel (a), except that each is shifted up.
For values of $x$ close to $x_\delta$, they each deliver strictly higher utility than $f_S$: $f_R(x_\delta) > f_S(x_\delta)$ for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a)$.
In fact, as $\sigma_\delta^2$ grows higher and higher, the receiver's expected utility conditional on seeing $f_S$ grows higher and higher.

In this sense, by sampling data with a wider distribution, the sender can make the receiver conditionally more optimistic.
Fixing $x_\delta$, the sender would thus sample from the distribution in $M$ with the highest possible variance.
The distribution in $M$ with the highest variance is the two-point distribution that places equal mass on $x_\delta - \overline{\sigma}$ and $x_\delta + \overline{\sigma}$.
Hence, the low-curvature simple sender samples from this two-point distribution.
I refer to this sort of distribution as corresponding to \emph{dispersed} data, and note that this is a bimodal distribution concentrated on the two most extreme possible points. 
The variance of this distribution is given by $\overline{\sigma}^2$.

The logic is flipped for a high-curvature sender.
Panels (c) and (d) look at what happens for a sender who provides a worst-case model.
On the left in panel (c), when the sender's underlying data are sampled in a tiny neighborhood of $x_\delta$, all the alternative plausible models $R$ considers have shallower curvature (i.e. lower $\gamma$) than the sender's model.
However, they all lie \emph{above} the sender's original model, and thus deliver higher expected utility, conditional on $f_S$.
By contrast, for distributions that are more spread out, as on the right in panel (d), these alternative models deliver lower expected utility, conditional on the same model $f_S$.
Thus, a high-curvature sender has an incentive to collect data \emph{locally} in the small neighborhood of a choice.

Finally, Theorem \ref{thm:sampling} additionally characterizes the \emph{mean} $x_\delta^*$ of the distribution $\mu_\delta^*$ that a type $\delta$ sender samples from in equilibrium.
The mean of a low-curvature sender's data lies between $0$ and the receiver's expected choice, roughly in the center of the choice space $X$; what matters, however, is that the data around this mean are spread out as widely as possible.
The mean of a high-curvature sender's data is at an edge of $X$.
To see why, it helps to write out the receiver's expected utility, conditional on seeing $f_S$.
We define $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ as:
\begin{align*}
\Delta(f_S,x_\delta) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]-\delta.
\end{align*}
Fixing $f_S$, $\Delta$ represents the \emph{difference} between $R$'s expectation of the quadratic parameter $\gamma$, conditional on the model being simple, and the sender's provided parameter $\delta$.
For example, $\Delta$ is always positive for the linear regression sender $\delta = 0$ and always negative for the worst-case scenario sender $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
In turn, $R$'s maximal expected utility can be expressed as:
\begin{align*}
\max_x f_S(x)+\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big),
\end{align*}
which she attains at $x=x^*(f_S)$.
The sender's choice of $x_\delta$ is then shaped by two forces.

The first is a \emph{learning} force: $x_\delta$ affects $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ itself.
When the sender samples at $x_\delta$, the receiver learns $f_R'(x_\delta) = \alpha_1 - 2\gamma x_\delta$.
When $x_\delta=0$, she receives a perfect signal about $\alpha_1$ and no signal about $\gamma$; as $x_\delta$ grows, she learns more about $\gamma$.
Because the sender believes his own curvature $\delta$ is correct, he expects this information to pull the receiver's posterior curvature towards $\delta$.
The assumption of a uniform distribution on $\alpha_1$ and a symmetric distribution on $\gamma$ allows a tractable characterization of this effect: the proof shows that $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big]$ is decreasing in $x_\delta$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ and increasing in $x_\delta$ for $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
A low-curvature sender wants the receiver's posterior curvature to be high, while a high-curvature sender wants it to be low, so for both types this force pushes the sender towards sampling at $0$, where his data are uninformative about curvature.

The second is a \emph{location} force.
When $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)\geq 0$, the receiver's expected utility above is concave in $x_\delta$.
This means that, fixing $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta) \geq 0$, the sender would like to choose $x_\delta = x^*(f_S)$.
$S$ does not know ex-ante where this maximum will be, so this force pushes $x_\delta$ towards where he \emph{expects} the receiver's choice to be, $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$.
Combined with the learning force, a low-curvature sender's choice of $x_\delta$ is thus somewhere between $0$ and $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$.
However, when $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta) < 0$, the receiver's expected utility is convex in $x_\delta$, so the sender would prefer to sample at a corner.
This pushes his preferred sampling mean towards $x_\delta = 0$ or $x_\delta = \overline{x}$, and the learning force tilts this choice towards $0$.

Figure \ref{fig:mainsampling} thus visualizes the sender's equilibrium choices.
The left shows a low-curvature (linear regression) sender collecting dispersed data, with mean $x_\delta$ roughly in the center of the choice space.
The right shows a high-curvature (worst-case) sender collecting local data near $0$.

In this sense, simple senders sample data that are \emph{poorly suited} to the receiver's eventual choice.
Even though the low-curvature sender's data are centered roughly between $0$ and $\overline{x}$, the actual choices he samples about are highly dispersed. In fact, those choices are so dispersed that they may not even lie inside the receiver's choice set.
This is shown in the left panel (a).

The high-curvature sender's data may also be poorly suited to the receiver's eventual choice.
He samples in the smallest possible neighborhood of $0$ or $\overline{x}$.
In this sense, he accurately describes the expected value of $y$ given $x$ at just one of these extreme points, but nowhere else.
In the example of the policymaker and consultant, this means that a consultant would mostly collect data where a carbon tax is close to $0$, or where a carbon tax is excessively high, neither of which is likely to represent the policymaker's optimum.

The coupling of \emph{model simplicity} with \emph{strategic sampling} thus creates incentives to collect data that are poorly suited to assessing the effects of choices on outcomes.
Intuitively, by sampling information on the edges of the receiver's choice space, senders create leeway for receivers to optimistically ``fill in the blanks'' about the values of outcomes at intermediate choices.
Indeed, Section \ref{section:forecast} formally shows that, if the receiver values accuracy, the \emph{second-best} degree of sampling would be an interior amount between $\underline{\sigma}$ and $\overline{\sigma}$.

The linear regression model (type $\delta = 0$ sender) is also of note.
The result on strategic sampling says that this sender always estimates models using data sampled bimodally from the far reaches of the choice space or beyond.
However, regressions are most effective when thought of as \emph{local linear approximations}.
Thus, the result on sampling contrasts a sender's persuasive desire to fit a regression to ``dispersed data'' with the common perception that regressions are most effective when utilized as ``local'' linear approximations.

\paragraph{Accurate Sender's Gain}
Because the accurate sender's models are not misspecified, his data sampling strategy does not affect the alternative models $R$ considers.
However, the accurate sender \emph{gains} from the existence of simple senders: he can make the receiver more optimistic than the truth.
Suppose the accurate sender provides a model $f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$.
Because the receiver believes that, with some probability, this model came from a simple type $\delta$ sender, she also entertains alternative models.
The accurate sender, however, knows that $f_S$ is the true model.
His gain, the gap between the receiver's perceived and true maximal expected utility, is given by:
\begin{align*}
G(f_S) = \max_{x \in X}\ f_S(x)+\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big(\sigma_\delta^{*2}-(x-x_\delta^*)^2\big) - \max_{x\in X} f_S(x).
\end{align*}

The following proposition shows that the accurate sender strictly gains from the existence of simple senders.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{simplegain}
Suppose the accurate sender provides a model $f_S$ with curvature $\delta$, that $\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)>0$, and that the type $\delta$ simple sender samples as in Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
Then $G(f_S)>0$ if and only if $\delta\neq\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
\label{prop:simplegain}
\end{restatable}

Intuitively, the receiver can always choose the value of $x$ that maximizes $f_S(x)$, so the accurate sender gains whenever the alternative models lie above $f_S$ at that point.
If the simple type $\delta$ is low-curvature, the alternative models are steeper, and because he samples dispersed data at $x_\delta^* \pm \overline{\sigma}$, they lie above $f_S$ everywhere between these two points, which includes the entire choice set.
If the simple type $\delta$ is high-curvature, the alternative models are flatter, and because he samples local data at an endpoint of $X$, they lie above $f_S$ everywhere except within $\underline{\sigma}$ of that endpoint.

In either case, the receiver is more optimistic than the truth.
She weighs not only the true model (the sender's original model) but also models that promise higher expected utility at her optimal choice.
It is precisely simple senders' restricted treatment of \emph{curvature}, coupled with their \emph{strategic sampling}, that generates this gain, which is larger the more likely the accurate sender is to be perceived as a simple sender.
I probe this result further in Section \ref{section:simple}, when I characterize which composition of simple senders advantages the accurate sender the most.

\subsection{Multidimensional Choices}
While the baseline model considered a single choice variable $x$, I also consider what happens when choices are multidimensional.
For example, imagine that the policymaker can shape consumer welfare not just through a carbon tax but also through clean energy subsidies.
I briefly show that many of the findings of the baseline model generalize.
However, I also show that when the sender samples data about multiple variables, he always has an incentive to introduce nearly perfect \emph{collinearity} between them.
In particular, if there are two choice variables --- $x_1$ and $x_2$ --- and the sender can choose how \emph{correlated} the two variables are when sampling the data, he will make them either as correlated as possible or as \emph{anticorrelated} as possible.
\paragraph{Multidimensional Models}
Suppose now that the receiver's choices are given by $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in X^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.
The models $f_R$ the receiver entertains are now as follows.
	For each $f_R \in F_R$, we have
	\begin{align*}
	f_R(x) = \alpha_0 + \alpha^T x - x^T\gamma x,	
	\end{align*}
	where $\alpha_0 \sim P_0^R$ and $\alpha  \sim P_1^R$ are continuous and uniform\footnote{Each component of $\alpha$ is distributed uniformly on a bounded interval, as in the univariate case.}, and $\gamma \sim P_\gamma^R$ on a set $\Gamma$. $\Gamma$ is a compact, convex set of positive-semidefinite matrices such that $0 \in \Gamma$ and such that there exists $\gamma \in \Gamma$ with $\gamma_{ii}>0$ for all $i$.

$F_R$ now \emph{generalizes} the idea of a quadratic data-generating process to a multivariate setting using a set of concave \emph{quadratic forms}.
This set of forms is given by a set of positive-semidefinite matrices $\Gamma$.
Accordingly, I assume the type space $\Theta$ of senders is given by $\{a\} \cup \Gamma$.

For simple senders, I assume that a type $\delta$ sender provides models of the form:
\begin{align*}
f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta^Tx-x^T\delta x,	
\end{align*}
where $\delta \in \Gamma$ is the sender's type.

Finally, I describe how the sender samples data in multiple variables.
Formally, let $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a \emph{spherically symmetric} random vector that admits a density with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$ and $\Cov(\epsilon) = I_n$.
Let $\Sigma_\delta$ be a symmetric positive-definite matrix.

Then, the sender's choice of distribution is characterized by $(x_\delta,\Sigma_\delta)$.
In this case, his data $x$ are sampled with respect to the distribution $\mu_\delta(x_\delta,\Sigma_\delta)$, such that:
\begin{align*}
\mu_\delta(x_\delta, \Sigma_\delta) = x_\delta + \Sigma_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon.
\end{align*}
I place the following assumptions on data sampling.
As before, I assume $x_\delta \in X^n$. Additionally,
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sigma_i := \sqrt{\Sigma_{\delta,ii}} \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]$ and
\item $\rho_{ij} := \frac{\Sigma_{\delta,ij}}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \in [-\overline{\rho},\overline{\rho}]$ for $\overline{\rho} \in (0,1)$.
\end{itemize}
This assumption is a multidimensional generalization of the idea that the ``variance'' of a variable $x_i$ must be between $\underline{\sigma}^2$ and $\overline{\sigma}^2$.
However, the key addition with $n$ choice variables is that the sender can choose the degree of \emph{correlation} between different variables when he samples data.\footnote{
I bound the correlation away from $-1$ and $1$ to ensure that learning does not collapse at $\rho=1$.}

\paragraph{Learning with Multidimensional Choices}
The following result generalizes receiver learning in multiple variables.
As before, $\phi$ denotes the set of additional models $R$ considers, given a model $f_S$ and sampling strategies $\mu_\delta$ and $\mu_a$.

\begin{restatable}{proposition}{reductionmulti}
\label{prop:reductionmulti}
Suppose $f_S = \beta_0+\beta^Tx-x^T\delta x$ best fits the data and $\mu_\delta = \mu_\delta(x_\delta,\Sigma_\delta)$. Then, for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\mu_\delta,\mu_a)$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = f_S(x)+\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta\big)-(x-x_\delta)^T(\gamma-\delta)(x-x_\delta).
\end{align*}
\end{restatable}
The proposition reads almost identically to Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, and the intuition is similar as well.
However, in place of the original scalar ``variance'' term, we instead have $\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta\big)$.
To see the impact that this has on the set of models the receiver will entertain, consider the following example with $x \in \mathbb{R}^2$. 
\begin{align*}
\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ \frac{1}{2} & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\delta = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma_\delta = \overline{\sigma}^{\,2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
Here, the receiver's model allows for a ``cross effect'' between $x_1$ and $x_2$, while the sender's model neglects it. In turn, we thus have:
\begin{align*}
\trace\big((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta\big)
=\overline{\sigma}^2\trace\bigg(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\bigg)
=\overline{\sigma}^2\rho.
\end{align*}
This trace term represents, as before, the \emph{gap} between the sender's original model and the receiver's model at $x_\delta$. The sender can \emph{increase} this gap if he makes $\rho$ as large as possible.

More formally, the sender in this case provides models that suffer from \emph{neglecting interactions}.
In the running example, the consultant whom the policymaker hires may effectively evaluate the effects of both a subsidy and tax \emph{separately}, but not take into account the \emph{interaction} of their effects.

The receiver is aware of the sender's misspecification, and she entertains additional models. However, the sender can give the receiver a more optimistic view of these models if he artificially creates a high degree of correlation between $x_1$ and $x_2$ when sampling data.
In the example, if the consultant cannot figure out how to model these interactive effects, he compensates by skewing his data collection towards settings where both a carbon tax and a clean energy subsidy are active at the same time, rather than towards settings that cleanly isolate the effect of one or the other.


\paragraph{Results with Multidimensional Choices}
As before, given $f_S = \beta_0+ \beta^Tx - x^T \delta x$ and fixing $x_\delta$, we denote $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ as:
\begin{align*}
\Delta(f_S,x_\delta) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]-\delta.
\end{align*}
The intuitive mapping from the one-dimensional problem to the multidimensional one can be seen by looking at the diagonal entries of $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$.
If the $i^{th}$ diagonal entry is \emph{positive}, this means that $S$ tends to \emph{neglect tradeoffs} and assume low curvature with respect to variable $x_i$.
If it is negative, he tends to \emph{exaggerate} tradeoffs and assume high curvature with respect to variable $x_i$.

However, what happens on the \emph{non-diagonal} entries of $\Delta$ is also of interest. Suppose $\Delta_{ij}(f_S,x_\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(f_S,x_\delta) > 0$. This means that the receiver expects the coefficients on the interactions of $x_i$ and $x_j$ to be \emph{larger} than what the sender provides, and we say that the sender \emph{neglects} interactions between $x_i$ and $x_j$.
If $\Delta_{ij}(f_S,x_\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(f_S,x_\delta) < 0$, the sender \emph{exaggerates} interactions between $x_i$ and $x_j$.
With these new definitions in hand, I can state the key multidimensional result.

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{samplingmulti}
Simple senders always maximize the collinearity of underlying datasets, i.e. make variables as highly correlated or highly anticorrelated as possible.
In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ij}(f_S,x_\delta^*)+\Delta_{ji}(f_S,x_\delta^*)] > 0$, then $\rho_{ij}^* = \overline{\rho}$.
\item If $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ij}(f_S,x_\delta^*)+\Delta_{ji}(f_S,x_\delta^*)] < 0$, then $\rho_{ij}^* = -\overline{\rho}$.
\end{itemize}
If $\gamma$ is diagonal for all $\gamma \in \Gamma$, the sender additionally sets $\sigma_i^* = \overline{\sigma}$ or $\underline{\sigma}$. In particular:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ii}(f_S,0)] > 0$, then $\sigma_{i}^*=\overline{\sigma}$.
\item If $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ii}(f_S,0)] < 0$, then $\sigma_{i}^*=\underline{\sigma}$.	
\end{itemize}
\label{thm:samplingmulti}
\end{restatable}

First, Theorem \ref{thm:samplingmulti} replicates the intuitions of the univariate result: when there are no interaction effects (i.e. $\gamma$ is always diagonal), the simple sender collects data from the widest variance distribution or smallest variance distribution in variable $i$, as opposed to pursuing an intermediate amount of sampling, exactly as before.
With interaction effects, a variable in which the sender estimates a high-curvature model may instead be sampled with an intermediate variance, since spreading it out also increases the gain from its correlation with other variables.

However, Theorem \ref{thm:samplingmulti} also shows that the sender's dataset \emph{maximizes collinearity} between variables.
If he \emph{neglects} an interaction effect between variables $x_i$ and $x_j$ (e.g., by always assuming it is $0$), he samples data so that they are as \emph{correlated as possible}.
If he \emph{exaggerates} interactions, he samples data so that they are as \emph{anticorrelated as possible.}

Concretely, suppose that the policymaker believes that the interaction of a carbon tax and clean energy subsidy may have particularly deleterious welfare effects at very high values, and that this is totally neglected by the consultant.
The theorem states that this sender will sample data to favor settings where both a tax and subsidy are active simultaneously, rather than separately.

Recall that any additional models the receiver entertains all have the same sample average value of $y$.
Because the tax and subsidy are highly correlated in those data, this sample average is depressed in large part by the (negative) interaction that the sender's model neglects.
To rationalize the same sample average, the policymaker's additional models must hence assign larger separate effects to the tax and subsidy than the sender's model.
But since the tax and subsidy are also choice variables, the receiver need not correlate them in her eventual choice, increasing her maximal expected utility. While this paper does not explicitly deal with finite samples, in practice, the introduction of this collinearity can create large standard errors and biased parameter estimates.

In this sense, when choices are multivariate, simple senders have incentives to sample data in an \emph{unbalanced way}: rather than ensuring that the marginal effects of different variables are estimated independently of each other, there may be an arbitrary degree of collinearity between variables in the datasets that senders produce.
Coupled with the previous results where senders collect data about irrelevant choices --- either dispersed, extremal data or local data about a single extreme choice --- high collinearity between variables only worsens the receiver's ability to learn.

\section{Additional Results: Information and Forecasting}\label{section:extensions}
This section provides additional results from the baseline, univariate model.
First, I characterize the \emph{value of information} for the receiver for contracting a sender's services.
I show that although this expression does not depend on whether a sender samples local or dispersed data, it potentially \emph{does} depend on \emph{where} the sender sampled data.

Second, I augment the receiver's baseline preferences with \emph{forecast error}.
As a result, the model captures settings where the receiver cares not just about maximizing $y$ but also about predicting $y$ accurately.
I show that the receiver's choices are more accurate when she receives information from a sender who samples local data than from a sender who samples dispersed data.
Consequently, the receiver may be better off hiring a high-curvature sender than a low-curvature sender, although I also show that neither is first best.

The Appendix deals with additional extensions.
Appendix \ref{section:intervals} studies the model where simple senders can model an \emph{interval} of $\gamma$ values, rather than a singleton $\gamma$ value.
Appendix \ref{section:general} studies the model with non-quadratic utility and shows that the central intuitions on sender sampling generalize.


\subsection{Receiver's Value of Information}
While it is clear that model simplicity inhibits the receiver's ability to learn the true model of the world and, thus, to make the right choice, there is also a question of \emph{how much} the receiver's ability to learn is distorted, conditional on receiving a simple model.
Characterizing the value of information for the receiver from hiring a sender allows me to determine how decision quality varies with sender behavior.
\footnote{It also represents the receiver's \emph{willingness to pay} to hire the sender, prior to data sampling and model provision.}

The value of information from hiring a simple sender of type $\delta$ is given by:
\begin{align}
V(\delta) :=\mathbb{E}_{f_S \in F_\delta}^R\big[\max_x \mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S}^R	 [f_R(x)|f_S,x_\delta^*]\big]
-\max_x \mathbb{E}_{f_R}^R[f_R(x)]
\label{eq:valinfo}
\end{align}
In the absence of the sender, $R$ would solely rely on her prior to maximize the expected value of $y$.
This allows me to characterize the \emph{value of information} for the receiver as follows.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{valueinfo}
Fix $\delta$.
The value of information $V$ for the receiver from hiring a sender of type $\delta$ is given by:
\begin{align*}
V(\delta) = \mathbb{E}_{f_S \in F_\delta}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta^*]\bigg(x^*(f_S)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg)^2\bigg]
\end{align*}	
and does not depend on $\sigma_\delta^*$.
Moreover, for $x^*(f_S)$ interior, we have
\begin{align*}
V(\delta) = \mathbb{E}_{f_R}^R\Bigg[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\delta^*)]\bigg(x_\delta^*+\frac{f_R'(x_\delta^*)}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_R'(x_\delta^*)]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg)^2\Bigg].
\end{align*}
\label{prop:valueinfo}
\end{restatable}
The key result is that $V$ does not depend on $\sigma_\delta^*$, i.e. the spread of the simple sender's data.
The reason is that the set of alternative models that the receiver entertains is simply vertical shifts of each other.
This means that the receiver is effectively receiving a noisy signal about the value of the constant term.
Furthermore, they do not affect the receiver's eventual choice $x^*(f_S)$.

Because the receiver is an expected utility maximizer, these noisy signals about the constant term have no bearing on her expected utility.
This notion of informational value ignores a key tension, which is that the \emph{forecast accuracy} generated by the sender's model will depend on the vertical spread between the DGPs she places positive probability on in equilibrium.
I study this in the next subsection.

Nevertheless, the receiver's value of learning \emph{does} depend on $x_\delta^*$, which is the \emph{mean} of the simple sender's sampling distribution.
The reason is that, by choosing a value of $x_\delta$, the sender reveals $f_R'(x_\delta) = \alpha_1-2\gamma x_\delta$.
When $x_\delta = 0$, the sender is effectively revealing $\alpha_1$ and no information about $\gamma$.
As $x_\delta$ grows higher, the sender reveals more information about $\gamma$ and less about $\alpha_1$, which in turn affects the receiver's expected decision quality.
We thus parametrize the value of information as:
\begin{align*}
V(\delta,x_\delta) :=\mathbb{E}_{f_S \in F_\delta}^R\big[\max_x \mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S}^R	 [f_R(x)|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta]\big]-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg)^2\bigg].
\end{align*}
The following proposition characterizes how $V(\delta,x_\delta)$ varies with $x_\delta$.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{samplingspot}
Suppose the receiver's choice $x^*(f_S)$ is interior.
Then $x_\delta^R = \arg\max_{x_\delta\in[0,\overline{x}]}V(\delta,x_\delta)$ satisfies $x_\delta^R\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}$.
Moreover, if $S$ is low-curvature and $\overline{\sigma}$ is sufficiently large or if $S$ is high-curvature and $x_\delta^*=0$, $x_\delta^R \geq x_\delta^*$.
\label{prop:samplingspot}
\end{restatable}
In particular, the proposition shows that, conditional on misspecification, the \emph{location} around which the simple sender samples is not first-best for the receiver, and may in fact be slightly to the left.
The formal intuition can be gleaned from the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
Recall that the gain generated by the alternative models the receiver considers is approximately proportional to $|\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta]-\delta|$.
This gain is decreasing in $x_\delta$.
This gives simple senders a preference for $x_\delta$ values that tend to be systematically lower than the sampling location $x_\delta^R$ that maximizes the receiver's value of information.


\subsection{Forecast Accuracy}\label{section:forecast}
So far, I have assumed only that the receiver would like to maximize $\mathbb{E}[y|x]$, conditional on launching the initiative.
However, in many settings, the receiver may also directly (or indirectly) value \emph{forecast accuracy}.
In our running example, a policymaker may value not just picking the optimal carbon tax, but also being able to \emph{accurately forecast} the effects of her policy choice.
Being able to predict the outcome (or set of outcomes) her policy choice could generate may allow her to better defend her choice to the politicians and the press, as well as to be more confident in her decision.
That is, the receiver values \emph{knowing the true DGP}.

To illustrate the impact of model simplicity and sampling on forecast-minded receivers, I consider a receiver whose expected utility is given by
\begin{align}
(1-\psi)\mathbb{E}^R[y|x]-\psi \Var^R(y|x)	
\label{eq:forecastutility}
\end{align}
for $\psi \in (0,1)$.
The first term, as before, corresponds to a receiver's desire to maximize the expected value of $y$.
The second term represents the ex-ante \emph{accuracy} with which she can predict $y$, after making her choice but prior to the resolution of any uncertainty.
If $\psi = 0$, she does not value her ability to forecast at all, and only cares about her expectation of $y$.
As $\psi \to 1$, she places a greater and greater emphasis on forecasting.

\paragraph{Exogenous Forecast Error}
Before studying what happens when the receiver chooses $x$ to maximize (\ref{eq:forecastutility}), I first characterize how the variance of $y$ behaves with respect to $\sigma_\delta$ when the receiver simply maximizes the expected value of $y$.
The following proposition shows that, fixing $x_\delta$, the variance of outcomes is minimized at an \emph{intermediate} value of $\sigma_\delta$.
That is, the way in which the simple type $\delta$ samples data to fit his model --- either using heavily dispersed or highly localized datasets --- never minimizes forecast error for the receiver.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{forecastsimple}
Fix a sender type $\delta$ and $x_\delta$, and let
\begin{align*}
W(\sigma_\delta) := \mathbb{E}_{f_S|\delta}^R\Big[\Var_{f_R|f_S}^R\big(f_R(x^*(f_S))\,\big|\,f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta,\sigma_\delta\big)\Big].
\end{align*}
There exists $\sigma_\delta^R$ such that $W(\sigma_\delta)$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_\delta$ for $\sigma_\delta<\sigma_\delta^R$ and strictly increasing for $\sigma_\delta>\sigma_\delta^R$.
\label{prop:forecastsimple}
\end{restatable}


The intuition for the result is as follows.
Writing $x^*$ as the value of $x$ that maximizes the expected value of $y$, recall the previous formulation of the receiver's conditional expected utility:
\begin{align*}
f_S(x^*) + \Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2\big)
\end{align*}
The receiver's forecast error is driven by the latter set of terms multiplied by $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$.
In particular, the variance of outcomes can be expressed as the difference between the simple sender's spread of sampling $\sigma_\delta^2$ and the squared distance between $x^*(f_S)$ and $x_\delta$ (the optimal choice and the point around which data are sampled).
When $\sigma_\delta^2$ is small, the dispersion is driven by $(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2$, which is always nonzero in expectation. This means the variance can be reduced by increasing $\sigma_\delta^2$.
Intuitively, because there is uncertainty about what the ex-ante optimal choice $x^*$ will be, an intermediate level of $\sigma_\delta^2$ allows the receiver to ``hedge'' and gather information in a slightly larger region where she expects the choice to be, rather than precisely at a single point.
When $\sigma_\delta^2$ is very large, however, there is a great deal of vertical dispersion in the potential values of $y$ that may materialize at $x^*$, and that dispersion can be reduced by decreasing $\sigma_\delta^2$.

Thus, the \emph{second-best} degree of sampling $\sigma_\delta^R$ that the receiver would choose to minimize outcome variance is necessarily intermediate: that is, neither a sender who samples with dispersed data $\sigma_\delta^*=\overline{\sigma}$ nor a sender who samples locally with $\sigma_\delta^*=\underline{\sigma}$ minimizes the variance of the receiver's outcomes.
However, as $\overline{\sigma}$ grows arbitrarily large, the receiver \emph{will} be better off under ``local'' sampling than ``dispersed'' sampling.

\paragraph{Endogenous Forecast Error}
I now study the setting where $R$ jointly trades off her expected gain in $y$ with forecast error. 
Conditional on $f_S$ and fixing $x_\delta$, her expected value is
\begin{align*}
V^F(f_S|\sigma_\delta)
= \max_{x}\ (1-\psi)\,\mathbb{E}_{f_R|f_S}^R\big[f_R(x)\,\big|\,f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta,\sigma_\delta\big]
-\psi\,\Var_{f_R|f_S}^R\big(f_R(x)\,\big|\,f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta,\sigma_\delta\big).
\end{align*}
The sender's preferences are the same, which is to maximize the receiver's perceived expected value of $y$ given her optimal choice.
The key difference is that the receiver's optimal choice itself is affected by how much she values forecast error.
The following proposition shows how the sender's choice of $\sigma_\delta$ is shaped by these considerations.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{forecast}
Suppose $R$ chooses $x^*(f_S)$ to maximize (\ref{eq:forecastutility}), and let $\sigma_\delta^R$ be the choice of $\sigma_\delta$ that minimizes the expected variance of outcomes.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A low-curvature sender chooses $\sigma_\delta^* > \sigma_{\delta}^R$ and a high-curvature sender chooses $\sigma_\delta^* < \sigma_{\delta}^R$.
\item As $\psi \to 1$, if $x_\delta\pm\sigma_\delta\in[0,\overline{x}]$, then $x^*(f_S) \to x_\delta - \sigma_\delta$ or $x_\delta + \sigma_\delta$.
\end{enumerate}
\label{prop:forecast}
\end{restatable}
The first part of the result generalizes the intuition of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
Namely, low-curvature senders sample data with a \emph{larger spread} than what the receiver would choose, and high-curvature senders sample with \emph{less spread}.
The intuition is that the sender accounts for the fact that the receiver will shift her choices to take into account forecast error.
For very high values of $\sigma_\delta^2$, a low-curvature sender trades off between increasing the receiver's expectation of $y$ through more dispersed data sampling and increasing the variance of outcomes she faces.
The opposite logic holds for high-curvature senders.

The second part of the result shows that if the receiver increasingly only values forecast error, her choice approaches $x_\delta \pm \sigma_\delta$.
This is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:forecast}.
Thus, $R$ trades off between a choice that maximizes her expected utility (at an interior point) and minimizing the variance of outcomes, which happens at $x_\delta \pm \sigma_\delta$.
When $\sigma_\delta$ is very large, $R$ is forced to choose arbitrarily extreme points $x_\delta \pm \sigma_\delta$ to minimize variance.
When $\sigma_\delta$ is very small, $R$ chooses points close to $x_\delta$.

\section{The Supply of Simple Senders}\label{section:simple}
This section studies the \emph{supply} of simple senders that the receiver will receive models from in equilibrium.
So far, I have taken the distribution of simple sender types as exogenous.
However, Proposition \ref{prop:simplegain} shows that accurate senders gain from being misperceived as simple senders, in that their existence allows the receiver to entertain more optimistic models than the truth, and thus be more likely to adopt the initiative.
Moreover, this gain is different depending on the type $\delta$ of a simple sender.
Thus, if an accurate sender could endogenously choose the \emph{supply} of simple senders who operated alongside him, what simple senders would he choose?
Formally, I ask what \emph{distribution} of simple senders would maximize the accurate sender's expected gain.

I thus augment the model with an additional stage: prior to being hired by the receiver, the accurate sender $a$ first chooses a \emph{distribution} $T$ of simple sender types.
After he chooses that distribution, nature then either selects the accurate sender or a simple sender and, in the latter case, draws the simple sender from $T$.
The interpretation is that the accurate sender is able to \emph{hire} additional senders who may provide simple models.
This endogenously changes the receiver's beliefs about the composition of the type space.
Conditional on contracting the services of his organization, a receiver may receive information from either the accurate sender or one of these simple senders.
For example, the accurate sender could be thought of as:
\begin{itemize}
\item a consultant or investment advisor who can employ other consultants/advisors, whose recommendations may take the form of simple rules of thumb;
\item a politician who can recruit co-partisans to propagate simple talking points among voters;
\item an AI company that can deploy simpler or more complex LLMs through its chatbot.
\end{itemize}
The key is that he is able to \emph{shape} the receiver's beliefs about whether she is facing a simple or accurate model.

I show that the distribution of simple senders that the accurate sender would choose is characterized by \emph{polarization} in how senders consider tradeoffs.
Formally, for each $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$, the conditional probability of facing a type $\delta$ sender is given by a density $t(\delta)$.\footnote{Appendix \ref{section:intervals} shows that even if the accurate sender can consider ``interval'' types, he would never choose an interval type in equilibrium.}
This density $t(\delta)$ takes the accurate sender's prior over $\delta$ and pushes mass away from intermediate values of $\delta$ towards the extremes of $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
I.e., simple senders either estimate models with low $\delta$ values (close to $0$) or high $\delta$ values (close to $\overline{\gamma}$), and never estimate models with intermediate $\delta$ values.
Thus, the set of simple sender types is fully composed of senders whose models have (arbitrarily) low or high curvature.
As the probability of seeing the accurate sender's model approaches $1$, the supply of simple senders converges to a one- or two-point distribution concentrated on either $\delta = 0$, $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, or both points.
These respectively correspond to senders who only provide ``linear regression'' models or ``worst-case scenario'' models, as I studied in Section \ref{section:learning}.

\subsection{Motivation}

To motivate the role of the accurate sender further, I return to the problem of the consultant and the policymaker.
Imagine an ``accurate'' consultant employed by a consulting organization, who can think about tradeoffs from carbon taxation in \emph{exactly} the way the policymaker would like.
However, he can also choose to hire additional colleagues, who might think about tradeoffs in a simpler way than both himself and the policymaker.
He can choose how many of these colleagues to hire, as well as how simply they might think about problems.

The consultant thus hires other consultants. The policymaker, in turn, is aware of the composition of the consultant's organization.
When she hires the organization's services, she is randomly assigned either the ``accurate'' consultant or one of the ``simple'' consultants.
Relatedly, she may contract a team composed of both the accurate consultant as well as other simple consultants, but not know how much oversight the accurate consultant exercised.
Based on the model she is ultimately provided, she in turn attempts to \emph{infer} the sender's type.
Thus, the problem boils down to: will the accurate consultant hire ``simple'' consultants to join him, what kind of simplicity will they adopt, and how many of those consultants will he hire?

The key interpretation of the design problem is thus that an accurate sender can endogenously shape the set of senders that the receiver considers, such as by actively hiring additional senders who model tradeoffs differently.
Beyond hiring, I also highlight additional interpretations based on the motivating examples.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Portfolio management.} Suppose the sender is a financial advisor who can estimate a highly accurate model of asset returns. The advisor may help train other managers or deploy ``robo-advisors'' that use simple rules of thumb.

\item \textbf{Political narratives.} Suppose the sender is a politician who can accurately provide a ``narrative model'' of a policy's effects on voter welfare. However, he can also use online media accounts to propagate ``simple narratives'' of a policy's effects. He could also align himself with extremist politicians who the public knows provide simple narratives.
\item \textbf{AI recommendations.} Suppose the sender is an AI platform that uses a sophisticated LLM to collect data and estimate a robust model that provides users accurate recommendations. The platform can also deploy simpler LLMs that apply a ``fixed'' way of thinking about a user's problem, and can determine how often users are shown each model under the hood.
\end{enumerate}
The key mechanism through which this design problem will operate is through manipulating the receiver's \emph{beliefs} about whether the model she sees came from an accurate or simple sender.

\subsection{Designing the Supply of Senders}
\paragraph{Design Problem Setup}
Formally, consider the following problem for the accurate (type $a$) sender.
Ex ante, with probability $p_a$, $R$ receives a model from $a$.
With probability $1-p_a$, she receives a model from a simple sender.\footnote{Appendix \ref{section:designintervals} allows $a$ to also choose types that are intervals of curvature values, and shows that he would never do so.}
We write $P_{f_S}^a$ for his prior over models $f_S$.
His objective is:
\begin{align*}
\max_{T} p_a\int_{f_S \in F_R} \max_{x}\mathbb{E}_{q|f_S,T}[f_R(x)]dP_{f_S}^a(f_S).
\end{align*}
As before, the type $a$ sender wants to make the receiver optimistic about the expected value of her choice.
We note that the impact of $T$ is through the receiver's \emph{posterior}.
I take $p_a$ as fixed; this could represent the type $a$ sender's capacity to hire others (higher $p_a$ means he has less latitude to hire), the exogenous choice of a platform/organization, or a market belief.\footnote{$p_a$ could also endogenously be chosen by the type $a$ sender himself to trade off the probability with which his services will be contracted with receivers' perceptions of him.}


The order of events is as follows.
$a$ first selects a distribution of types $T$ over $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$.
We denote his optimal choice by $T^*$.
The receiver hires the services of a sender, and receives a model from $a$ with probability $p_a$ or from the simple sender with probability $1-p_a$.
Conditional on the latter event, a type $\delta$ sender is then drawn according to the distribution $T$.
Finally, the deployed sender strategically samples data as before, supplies the model of best fit, and provides it to the receiver, who makes her decision.

\paragraph{Distribution of Simple Senders}
The distribution of simple senders that maximizes the accurate sender's ability to make the receiver optimistic is as follows.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{optimaltype}
There exists a function $\pi(\delta)$ such that $T^*$ is given by a continuous distribution $t$ with
\begin{align}
t(\delta) = p_\gamma^R(\delta)\pi(\delta).
\tag{$T^*$}
\label{eq:optimalT}	
\end{align}
In particular, there exist thresholds $0 \leq \underline{\delta}_0<\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]<\overline{\delta}_0 \leq \overline{\gamma}$ such that $\pi(\delta)$ is strictly decreasing below $\underline{\delta}_0$, strictly increasing above $\overline{\delta}_0$, and equal to $0$ on $[\underline{\delta}_0,\overline{\delta}_0]$.
%where $\pi(\delta)$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])$, strictly increasing on $(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma],\overline{\gamma}]$, $\pi(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])=-\lambda$, and $\lambda>0$ is a constant.
%Moreover, there exist $0 \leq \underline{\delta}_0<\overline{\delta}_0 \leq \overline{\gamma}$ (with at least one of the weak inequalities strict) such that $t(\underline{\delta}_0)=t(\overline{\delta}_0)=0$.
\label{thm:optimaltype}
\end{restatable}

Theorem \ref{thm:optimaltype} characterizes the \emph{shape} of the distribution of $\delta$ values the type $a$ sender utilizes.
The solution is that the type $a$ sender pushes mass to the \emph{edges} of the interval $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$; formally, the distribution is a \emph{warped} version of his and the receiver's prior belief about curvature values.
In this sense, the type $a$ sender \emph{polarizes} simple senders towards models calibrated to (arbitrarily) low curvature (with $\delta$ close to $0$) or high curvature (with $\delta$ close to $\overline{\gamma}$).
In particular, $t(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]) = 0$, i.e. $t(\delta) = 0$ when $\delta$ is close to the prior expectation of $\gamma$.

First, recall by Proposition \ref{prop:simplegain} that the accurate sender benefits from being misperceived as a simple sender.
In particular, this gain comes from the combination of the simple sender's strategic sampling with his assumption of a fixed curvature value when providing a model.

But while the accurate sender always gains, his gain also depends on the true curvature value $\gamma$ in the data.
Thus, he must consider which $\gamma$ values are ex-ante more plausible.
His problem is thus to choose a distribution over types which, in this case, boils down to just being a distribution over the set of plausible simple types $\delta$.
He designates an amount of probability density to allocate to each $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$, subject to the constraint that these densities must integrate to $1$.

Suppose $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, which is the \emph{receiver}'s ex-ante expectation of $\gamma$.
Looking back to Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, simple senders do not gain from sampling from a higher or lower variance distribution in this case.
Relatedly, this means that the type $a$ sender has no incentive to place mass on $\delta$ values near $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
On the other hand, as $\delta$ gets farther and farther away from $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, simple senders provide a larger and larger gain for the accurate sender.
In fact, the gains to the sender are conditionally maximal if $\delta = 0$ or $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
This is what drives the ``polarization'' of simple senders: the accurate sender, ex-ante, does not know what value of $\gamma$ will materialize, but does know that the gain will be small for intermediate values of $\gamma$, and thus pushes more mass to the edge.

\paragraph{Variation with $p_a$} We note that as $p_a$ increases, the receiver is more likely to think that she saw the accurate sender model, and that as $p_a$ decreases, this is less likely.
In the example with the consultant and policymaker, the type $a$ sender may be required to provide his services $p_a$ of the time, but has latitude to choose the senders who the receiver sees $1-p_a$ of the time.
As $p_a$ increases, this latitude decreases, and the receiver is conditionally more and more sure that she received the true model. 
The following proposition shows that simple sender ``polarization'' is increasing in $p_a$, in the sense that as $p_a$ increases, $t(\delta)$ places more and more mass on extreme types with very high or low $\delta$ values, and less (or none) on intermediate types.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{polarizationlimit}
The polarization of simple senders is increasing in $p_a$. That is, $\underline{\delta}_0$ is decreasing in $p_a$ and $\overline{\delta}_0$ is increasing in $p_a$.
As $p_a \to 1$, $t(\delta)$ converges to a distribution that either places all mass only on $\delta = 0$, $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, or both.
As $p_a \to 0$, $t(\delta)$ converges to a distribution that is strictly decreasing on $[0,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])$, equal to $0$ at $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and strictly increasing on $(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma],\overline{\gamma}]$.
\label{prop:polarizationlimit}
\end{restatable}
The proposition is visualized above in the right panel of Figure \ref{fig:typespace}. The dashed purple line graphs $t(\delta)$ for a lower value of $p_a$. The solid line graphs $t(\delta)$ for a higher value of $p_a$, showcasing the ``increased polarization'' generated by an increase in $p_a$.

The intuition is as follows.
The type $a$ sender's gain from being mistaken for a simple sender is high at $\delta = 0$, decreases to $0$ at $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and then rises again until $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$.
That is, he conditionally gains the most when $\delta$ is very low or very high.
When $p_a$ is large, the ex-ante probability with which he will be seen as a simple sender is low, and he is thus best off concentrating simple senders close to $\delta=0$ and $\delta = \overline{\gamma}$, where he expects the gain to be largest.
However, when $p_a$ is small, he is willing to distribute simple senders more evenly throughout the population although, in either case, he concentrates few senders near $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.

I highlight two additional features of this result.
First, polarization survives even as $p_a \to 0$ (so the receiver is less likely to think she sees the true model).
Second, as $p_a \to 1$, the optimal distribution either converges to only deploying senders with type $0$ or type $\overline{\gamma}$ and nothing in between.
That is, the model returns precisely to the two-type example introduced in Section \ref{section:setup}: in the limit, the only simple senders are those who \emph{only provide} linear regression models or \emph{only provide} ``worst-case'' models.


\section{Conclusion}\label{section:conclusion}
This paper theoretically studies how strategic data collection and model simplicity shape communication with models and data.
A receiver $R$ hires a sender $S$ to help her decide whether to launch an initiative and, if so, what shape that initiative should take.
The sender $S$ first collects data and then estimates a model.
However, the sender may be accurate or simple.
If he is accurate, he correctly estimates the data-generating process mapping choices to outcomes.
If he is simple, he assumes the \emph{curvature} of the relationship between choices and outcomes is calibrated to a fixed value.
In the real world, these correspond to senders who calibrate their models to pre-defined parameters: economic consultants may calibrate models to elasticities or labor shares from the literature rather than organically estimating them in their setting; robo-advisors may apply fixed investing rules of thumb across clients; and politicians may weave narratives in which a policy's costs are fixed in advance.
$S$'s goal is to maximize the probability with which $R$ adopts the initiative so that he can receive credit for helping her.
In this sense, he would like to make $R$ optimistic, in the sense of maximizing her \emph{perceived expected utility}.

I first characterize how $R$ learns based on the model that $S$ provides her.
I show that unless $R$ is certain that $S$ provided an accurate model of her decisions, she must entertain additional models.
If $S$ estimated his model on a small set of ``local'' choices, I show that the additional models $R$ entertains are all tangent to $S$'s original model at a point.
If $S$ estimated his model on a large range of choices, the additional models $R$ entertains are all secants through $S$'s original model.

I then show a close correspondence between what kinds of data $S$ collects and his model \emph{simplicity}.
If $S$'s model assumes low curvature, i.e., \emph{neglects} tradeoffs, the data underlying his model are dispersed bimodally across an arbitrarily wide set of choices, and may draw on information that is irrelevant to the receiver's eventual choice.
Low-curvature models include linear regression models.
If $S$ estimates high-curvature models that \emph{exaggerate} tradeoffs, then he collects data locally about a single extreme receiver choice.
These include ``worst-case scenario'' models that always assume the costs of choices are as high as possible.

In both cases, the receiver considers not only the sender's original model, but also models that deliver strictly higher expected utility.
When the sender presents a low-curvature model, the expected utility delivered by these alternative models is strictly increasing in the spread of data collection.
Thus, conditional on receiving a low-curvature model, the receiver's perceived expected utility is increasing in that spread.
When the sender presents a high-curvature model, the expected utility delivered by these alternative models is strictly decreasing in the spread of data collection, which gives a high-curvature sender an incentive to sample data with minimal spread, i.e. locally in a small neighborhood of a point.
In either case, this strategic data sampling increases the receiver's optimism about the potential impact of her optimal choice, in the sense of maximizing her perceived expected utility.
When choices are multivariate, I show that this mechanism generalizes, and the sender samples data in a way that maximizes collinearity between variables, and makes them as correlated (or anticorrelated) as possible.

I then show that an accurate sender gains from being misperceived as a simple sender, providing a channel for the existence of simple senders in equilibrium.
That is, if a sender provides a true, accurate model of a receiver's choices, he always gains if the receiver thinks his model might have (artificially) low or high curvature.
The intuition is that, in the latter cases, the receiver entertains not only a sender's original model but also additional models that lie above it.
Thus, the sender who provides an accurate model can make the receiver have a more optimistic belief about her maximal expected utility than the true model.
Accurate senders may therefore try to masquerade as simple ones: clients of a large consulting firm may not know whether a senior partner or junior analyst built their model, investors may be unable to tell whether an experienced manager or a robo-advisor suggested a rule, and users of an AI platform may not know whether a recommendation came from a small or a large model.

I use this insight on model simplicity to characterize the supply of senders that a receiver may receive information from in equilibrium.
I study a design problem where an accurate sender can ``hire'' additional simple senders, and characterize what distribution of simple senders provides him the maximal \emph{gain}, in the sense of making the receiver the most optimistic. 
I show that he would polarize simple senders into two distinct groups: those whose models have (arbitrarily) low curvature, close to ``linear regression'' models, and those whose models have (arbitrarily) high curvature, close to ``worst-case'' models.
Thus, the market of senders is composed of three groups: some senders who always provide accurate models, some who provide very low-curvature models, and some who provide very high-curvature models.
In the limit, a receiver who receives a simple model sees only linear regressions or worst-case models.
While this form of polarization is common among political narratives, the paper suggests that it may occur more generally when an expert is hired to provide a model or \emph{narrative} of the effects of different choices.

Simple models, strategic data sampling, and polarization all represent key frictions that may emerge when decisionmakers rely on expertise and modeling to shape their choices.
Future work will investigate these frictions further in the settings of price-based markets and the polarization of political platforms, as well as through empirical work that investigates delegation to agentic AI programs or the role of consultants' narratives in shaping organizational decisionmaking.

\reduction*
\begin{proof}
We rewrite the sender's model as a local expansion around $x_\delta$: $f_S(x) = \tilde{\beta}_0+\tilde{\beta}_1 (x-x_\delta)-\delta(x-x_\delta)^2$, where $\tilde{\beta_1} = \beta_1-2\delta x_\delta$.
Because a sender's type is the curvature $\delta$ of his model, the value of $\delta$ uniquely pins down the sender's type.

Suppose $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\mu_\delta)$.
Similarly expanding $f_R$ around $x_\delta$, we have:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \tilde{\alpha}_0 + \tilde{\alpha}_1 (x-x_\delta)-\gamma(x-x_\delta)^2.	
\end{align*}
Because $\mu_\delta$ is symmetric, it is elliptical (in one dimension).
By Stein's Lemma \citep{landsman2008stein}, equation (\ref{eq:slope}) can be written as
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-f_S(x) \big)\big]
&= \Cov_{\mu_\delta}(x,f_R(x)-f_S(x))
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R'(x)-f_S'(x)]
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[\tilde{\alpha}_1-2\gamma(x-x_\delta)-\tilde{\beta_1}+2\delta (x-x_\delta)]
= 0
\end{align*}
Because the mean of $\mu_\delta$ is $x_\delta$, this yields the condition $\tilde{\alpha}_1 = \tilde{\beta}_1$, which is \emph{independent} of $\sigma^2$.
This means that $\alpha_1-2\gamma x_\delta = \beta_1-2\delta x_\delta$, i.e. $\alpha_1 = \beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\delta$.
Hence, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \alpha_0+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\delta)x-\gamma x^2,
\end{align*}
with the value of $\gamma$ and $\alpha_0$ potentially indeterminate.
However, fixing a plausible $\gamma$ value, from (\ref{eq:intercept}) we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R(x)]
= \alpha_0+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\delta)x_\delta-\gamma (x_\delta^2+\sigma^2)
= \beta_0+\beta_1x_\delta-\delta (x_\delta^2+\sigma^2)
= \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_S(x)]
\\
\implies
\alpha_0-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\delta^2=\beta_0-\delta x_\delta^2-\delta \sigma^2
\\
\implies \alpha_0 = \beta_0+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\delta^2).
\end{align*}
Putting the pieces together, we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \beta_0+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\delta^2)+(\beta_1-2(\delta-\gamma)x_\delta)x-\gamma x^2
\\
= \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2 + \big(-(\gamma-\delta)x^2 	
+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma^2-x_\delta^2)+2(\gamma-\delta)x_\delta
\big)
\\
= \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2 + (\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big)
\\
= f_S(x)+(\gamma-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big).
\end{align*}
Because the receiver's prior over $\alpha_1$ is flat, her expected utility (and objective) is given by:
\begin{align*}
\beta_0+\beta_1 x -\delta x^2 + \Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta)\big(\sigma^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big)	
\end{align*}
which is maximized at an interior value when
\begin{align*}
f_S'(x_\delta)-2\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta)(x-x_\delta)=0\\
x=x_\delta+\frac{f_S'(x_\delta)}{2\big(\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\delta+\Pr(\theta=a|f_S)\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\big)}
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\sampling*
\begin{proof}
Following from Proposition \ref{prop:reduction}, given a type $\delta$ sender's model, any $\gamma \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]$ value is possible from the receiver's perspective.
Fixing $x_\delta,\sigma_\delta$, and a model $f_S$ with curvature $\delta$, the receiver chooses $x=x^*(f_S)$ to maximize:
\begin{align*}
f_S(x) + \Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big),
\end{align*}
where $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta) := \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|\gamma,f_S,x_\delta]-\delta$.
In turn, the type $\delta$ sender chooses $x_\delta,\sigma_\delta$ to maximize his expectation of the receiver's perceived expected utility
\begin{align}
\max_{x_\delta,\sigma_\delta}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta
\bigg[
\max_{x \in [0,\overline{x}]}
f_S(x) + \Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x-x_\delta)^2\big)
\big]\bigg]
\label{eq:theorem1obj}
\end{align}
We note that because the receiver has a uniform prior over $\alpha_0$, her posterior places the same mass on all models that are the same ``up to a constant''; hence, changing $\sigma^2$ does not change the relative mass she places on each model, fixing a plausible $\gamma$ value.
Thus, by the Envelope Theorem, (\ref{eq:theorem1obj}) is increasing in $\sigma^2$ if $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ is positive and decreasing if $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ is negative.
Working backwards, when the sender takes expectations over (\ref{eq:theorem1obj}), he chooses $\sigma$ as large as possible.
Fixing $x_\delta$, the distribution with the largest possible variance corresponds to a bimodal distribution which places equal mass on $x_\delta-\underline{\sigma}$ and $x_\delta+\overline{\sigma}$.
Otherwise, if $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}[\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)] \geq 0$, we have $\sigma_\delta^* = \underline{\sigma}$.

Next,  we characterize the equilibrium value of $x_\delta$ and hence $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}[\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)]$.
Note that $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)$ depends not just on $f_S$ but also $x_\delta$.
In particular:
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]
= \frac{\int_{0}^{\overline{\gamma}}\gamma p_\gamma^R(\gamma)p_1(\beta_1+2(\gamma-\delta)x_\delta) d\gamma}{\int_{0}^{\overline{\gamma}} p_\gamma^R(\gamma)p_1(\beta_1+2(\gamma-\delta)x_\delta) d\gamma}	.
\end{align*}
Note that when $x_\delta = 0$, this expression is the receiver's prior expectation of $\gamma$, i.e. $\mathbb{E}_R^\gamma[\delta]$.

Next, $p_1$ is uniform on $[\underline{\alpha}_1,\overline{\alpha}_1]$, $p_1$ is $\neq 0$ only when $\gamma \in \big(\delta +\frac{\underline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta} ,\delta + \frac{\overline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta}\big)$.
Conditional on $f_S$, the receiver's expectation and the sender's prior expectation of that expectation can then be written as
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]
= \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in \big(\delta +\frac{\underline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta} ,\delta + \frac{\overline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta}\big)\big]
\\
\implies
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big]
= \int_{\underline{\alpha}_1}^{\overline{\alpha}_1}\frac{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in \big(\delta +\frac{\underline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta} ,\delta + \frac{\overline{\alpha}-\beta_1}{2x_\delta}\big)\big]\big]}{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}d\beta_1.
\end{align*}
That is, the simple sender is integrating the expectation out with respect to a (uniform) prior over $\beta_1$, which is identical to the prior over $\alpha_1$.
Substituting $u = \frac{\beta_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}$:
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big]
&= \frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in \big[\delta -u,\delta -u+ \frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\big] \cap [0,\overline{\gamma}]\big]\big]du
\end{align*}
Recall from above that when $x_\delta=0$, the expectation is just the receiver's prior. Thus, we can write
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big] = 	\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]+
(\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])
\end{align*}
Breaking the expectation into three regions gives:
\begin{align*}
&\frac{2x_\delta}{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}\bigg(
\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in [\delta -u,\overline{\gamma}]\big]du
+\int_\delta^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\,du
\\
&+\int_{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)}^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\Big[\gamma\Big| \gamma \in \Big[0,\delta -u+ \frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\Big]\Big]du\bigg)-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
\\
=&\frac{2x_\delta}{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}\bigg(
\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in [\delta -u,\overline{\gamma}]\big]du
+\Big(\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-\overline{\gamma}\Big)\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
\\
&
+\int_{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)}^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\Big[\gamma\Big| \gamma \in \Big[0,\delta -u+ \frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\Big]\Big]du
-\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\bigg)
\\
=&\frac{2x_\delta}{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}\bigg(
\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in [\delta -u,\overline{\gamma}]\big]du-\delta\,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
+\int_{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)}^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\Big[\gamma\Big| \gamma \in \Big[0,\delta -u+ \frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\Big]\Big]du-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)\,\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\bigg)
\\
=&\frac{2x_\delta}{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}\bigg(
\int_0^\delta \Big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in [\delta -u,\overline{\gamma}]\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\Big)du
+\int_{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}-(\overline{\gamma}-\delta)}^{\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}} \Big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\Big[\gamma\Big| \gamma \in \Big[0,\delta -u+ \frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}\Big]\Big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\Big)du\bigg)
\end{align*}
Changing variables again with $v = -u +\frac{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}{2x_\delta}+\delta$ in the last integral term gives
\begin{align*}
\frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}\bigg(
&\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in \big[\delta -u,\overline{\gamma}]\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]du
+\int_{\delta}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \in \big[0,v]\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]dv\bigg)
\\
=\frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}\bigg(
&\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \geq u\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]du
+\int_{\delta}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \leq v\big]-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]dv\bigg)
\\
=\frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}\bigg(
&\int_0^\delta \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \geq u\big]du
+\int_{\delta}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \leq v\big]dv-\overline{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\bigg)
:= \frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}K(\delta),
\end{align*}
where $K(\delta)$ does not depend on $x_\delta$ fixing $\delta$.
Thus, fixing $\delta$, $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]$ is linear in $x_\delta$.
$K(\delta)$ is strictly increasing in $\delta$, since its derivative is 
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|\gamma \geq \delta]-	\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|\gamma \leq \delta].
\end{align*}
Moreover, because $\gamma$ is symmetric, we have that $\overline{\gamma}-\gamma$ and $\gamma$ have the same distribution, i.e.
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \geq u\big]
=
\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\overline{\gamma}-\gamma| \overline{\gamma}-\gamma \geq u\big]
=
\overline{\gamma}-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma|\gamma \leq \overline{\gamma}-u].
\end{align*}
But then we have 
\begin{align*}
K(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]) =&\int_0^{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \geq u\big]du
+\int_{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \leq v\big]dv-\overline{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
\\	
=&\int_0^{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]} \overline{\gamma}-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma|\gamma \leq \overline{\gamma}-u]du
+\int_{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \leq v\big]dv-\overline{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
\\
=&\int_0^{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma|\gamma \leq \overline{\gamma}-u]du
+\int_{\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}^{\overline{\gamma}} \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R\big[\gamma| \gamma \leq v\big]dv+\overline{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
-\overline{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
=0.
\end{align*}
Thus, for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$,  $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]$ is strictly decreasing and linear in $x_\delta$.
For $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$,  $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]$ is strictly increasing and linear in $x_\delta$.
When $\delta = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, it is equal to $0$ for all $x_\delta$.

Thus, we have:
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)(\sigma_\delta^2-(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))^2]	
= \bigg(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
- \delta +
\frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}K(\delta)\bigg)
\big(\sigma_\delta^2-\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))^2]\big).
\end{align*}
Differentiating with respect to $x_\delta$ and using an Envelope Argument ($x^*(f_S)$ maximizes the receiver's objective) yields that the sender's objective is increasing in $x_\delta$ if and only if 
\begin{align*}
\frac{2}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}K(\delta)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))^2]\big)
-2\bigg(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]
- \delta +
\frac{2x_\delta}{{\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1}}K(\delta)\bigg)\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))].
\end{align*}
Because $\sigma_\delta$ can be arbitrarily small or large, we have that $\big(\sigma_\delta^2-\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))^2]\big)>0$.
We thus deal with the following cases.


First, suppose $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$. 
Note that because $\sigma_\delta$ is sufficiently large, setting $x_\delta = 0$ always delivers a strictly positive gain.
Indeed, the first set of terms in the derivative is negative (since $K(\delta)$ is negative) and pushes the optimal choice of $x_\delta^*$ towards $0$ linearly.
The latter set of terms pushes towards $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$: since $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta\big[\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,x_\delta,\theta=\delta]\big]-\delta>0$, it is positive when $x_\delta<\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$ and negative when $x_\delta>\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$.
Thus, we have $x_\delta^* \in [0, \mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[x^*(f_S)]$.

Second, suppose $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$. Then $K(\delta)>0$ and $\sigma_\delta^2-\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[(x_\delta-x^*(f_S))^2] \leq 0$.
The first set of terms thus pushes to $0$ linearly.
The latter set of terms represent a convex function and push to either $0$ or $\overline{x}$.
Thus, we have either $x_\delta^* = 0$ or $\overline{x}$.

Finally, putting the pieces together, whenever $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, a type $\delta$ sender always has a positive gain from setting $x_\delta^*\geq 0$.
Thus, these senders set $\sigma_\delta^*=\overline{\sigma}$ via a bimodal distribution with symmetric mass on $x_\delta^* \pm \overline{\sigma}$.
For $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, these senders set $\sigma_\delta^* = \underline{\sigma}$. 


\end{proof}

\reductionmulti*
\begin{proof}
As above, we necessarily have the following moment conditions for all $i$:
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x_i\big(f_R(x)-f_S(x)\big)\big] = 0 \qquad
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_R(x)-f_S(x)\big] = 0
\end{align*}
and we may or may not have the correct moment condition with respect to the entries of $\gamma$. 
Crucially, however, because $\mu_\delta$ is elliptical, by Stein's Lemma for elliptical random vectors \citep{landsman2008stein}, we can simplify the first equation to
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x_i\big(f_R(x)-f_S(x)\big)\big] = 0
&\implies
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}f_R(x)\big]
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}f_S(x)\big] \qquad \forall i
\\
&\implies
\alpha - (\gamma+\gamma')\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[x]
=
\beta - (\delta+\delta')\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[x]
\\
&\implies
\alpha = \beta + \big((\gamma+\gamma')-(\delta+\delta')\big)x_\delta
\\
&\implies
\alpha'x = \beta'x + x_\delta'\big((\gamma+\gamma')-(\delta+\delta')\big)x
\end{align*}
which is invariant to $\Sigma_\delta$.
Next, working with the second equation, we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_R(x)]
= \alpha_0 + \alpha x_\delta -\big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)'\gamma \big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)
= \beta_0 + \beta x_\delta -\big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)'\delta \big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_S(x)].
\end{align*}
Note that
\begin{align*}
\big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)'\gamma \big(x_\delta + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon\big)
= x_\delta'\gamma x_\delta + 2x_\delta'\gamma\Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}} \epsilon + \Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon'\gamma\Sigma_\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon	
\end{align*}
and taking the expectation with respect to $\epsilon$ thus gives
\begin{align*}
x_\delta'\gamma x_\delta + \trace{(\gamma\Sigma_\delta)}	.
\end{align*}
Utilizing the same logic with $\delta$ and plugging in the $\alpha$ equation gives
\begin{align*}
\alpha_0=\beta_0+(\beta-\alpha)x_\delta + 	x_\delta'(\gamma-\delta) x_\delta + \trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)}	
\\
= \beta_0-x_\delta'\big((\gamma+\gamma')-(\delta+\delta')\big)x_\delta + \trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)}	
\\
= \beta_0-x_\delta'(\gamma-\delta)x_\delta + \trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)}
\end{align*}
Hence, we renormalize $f_R$, plug in the values of $\alpha_0,\alpha$, and reorganize the quadratic terms to get:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x)
&= \alpha_0 + \alpha' x - x'\gamma x
\\
&= \beta_0-x_\delta'(\gamma-\delta)x_\delta + \trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)}
+\beta'x + x_\delta'\big((\gamma+\gamma')-(\delta+\delta')\big)x- x'\gamma x
\\
&= \beta_0+\beta'x + \trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)} - (x-x_\delta)'(\gamma-\delta)(x-x_\delta).
\end{align*}

\end{proof}

\samplingmulti*
\begin{proof}
The proof of this result is similar to that of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
The problem for a type $\delta$ sender is given by:
\begin{align*}
\max_{\Sigma_\delta,x_\delta}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta	
\bigg[
\max_{x \in [\xlow,\overline{x}]}
\beta_0+\beta'x +
\Pr(\theta=\delta|f_S)
\bigg(\trace{((\gamma-\delta)\Sigma_\delta)} 
- (x-x_\delta)'(\gamma-\delta)(x-x_\delta)\bigg)\bigg]
\end{align*}
subject to the constraints that $\Sigma_\delta$ is positive definite and variances/correlations are in their bounded intervals:
\begin{align*}
\Big(\prod_{i=1}^j \sigma_i^2\Big)\det\begin{pmatrix}
1 & \rho_{12} & \cdots & \rho_{1j}\\
\rho_{12} & 1 & \cdots & \rho_{2j}\\
\vdots & & \ddots & \vdots\\
\rho_{1j} & \rho_{2j} & \cdots & 1
\end{pmatrix} > 0 \quad \text{for } j = 2,\ldots,n, \quad
\sigma_i \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}],\quad
\rho_{ij} \in [-\overline{\rho},\overline{\rho}].
\end{align*}
We first work with $\Sigma_\delta$, fixing $x_\delta$, and note that the program with respect to $\Sigma_\delta$ depends only on $\gamma-\delta$ and $\Pr(\theta=a|f_S)$.
Recalling the definition of $\Delta(f_s,x_\delta) = \mathbb{E}[\gamma|f_S,\theta \neq a]-\delta$, the maximizer is equivalent to the maximizer of
\begin{align*}
\max_{\Sigma_\delta}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta	
\big[
\trace{(\Delta(f_s,x_\delta)\Sigma_\delta)}
\big]	
=
\max_{\Sigma_\delta}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta	
\big[
\sum_{i=1}^n \Delta_{ii}(f_S,x_\delta)\sigma_i^2
+ \sum_{i<j}
(\Delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ji})\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j
\big]	
\end{align*}
subject to the same constraints.
This program is linear in the objective, meaning the solution is given by an extreme point of the feasible set.
In particular, fixing $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$, the objective is linear in $\rho_{ij}$.
Thus, we have
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ji}]>0 \implies \rho_{ij} = \overline{\rho}
\\
&\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\delta[\Delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ji}]<0 \implies \rho_{ij} = -\overline{\rho},		
\end{align*}
noting that because $|\rho|<1$, we have:
\begin{align*}
\det\begin{pmatrix}
1 & \rho_{12} & \cdots & \rho_{1j}\\
\rho_{12} & 1 & \cdots & \rho_{2j}\\
\vdots & & \ddots & \vdots\\
\rho_{1j} & \rho_{2j} & \cdots & 1
\end{pmatrix} > 0
\end{align*}
We further note that the value of $\rho$ does not depend on $\sigma_i$ or $\sigma_j$, since they are always positive.
With the values of $\rho_{ij}$ in hand, we then note that because $\sigma_{i}>0$ for all $i$, we necessarily have that $\prod_{i=1}^j \sigma_i^2>0$, meaning the positive definiteness constraint is always satisfied strictly.
Finally, the optimal choice of $\sigma_{i}$ is given by taking the program above, plugging in the values of $\rho_{ij}$, and solving the linear program, which yields either $\sigma_i = \overline{\sigma}_i$ or $\underline{\sigma}_i$ as maximizers.

The program is linear in the entries of $\Sigma_\delta$. In particular, the sign of the coefficient on $\Sigma_{ij}$ is given by the sign of $\Delta_{ij}(f_S,x_\delta)+\Delta_{ji}(f_S,x_\delta)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}

Conditional on $f_S$ from a type $\delta$ sender, we have:
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E}^R_{f_R|f_S}[f_R(x^*)] - \mathbb{E}^R_{f_R|f_S}[f_R(x_0)]\\
&= f_S(x^*) + \Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x^*-x_\delta^*)^2\big) - f_S(x_0) - \Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x_0-x_\delta^*)^2\big)\\
&= \beta_1(x^*-x_0) - \delta\big(x^{*2}-x_0^2\big) + \Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big((x_0-x_\delta^*)^2-(x^*-x_\delta^*)^2\big)\\
&= \beta_1(x^*-x_0) - \delta\big(x^{*2}-x_0^2\big) + \Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big(2x_\delta^*(x^*-x_0) - (x^{*2}-x_0^2)\big)\\
&= \big(\beta_1+2\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)x_\delta^*\big)(x^*-x_0) - \big(\delta+\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big)\big(x^{*2}-x_0^2\big)\\

&= (x^*-x_0)\Big[2\big(\delta+\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big)x^* - \big(\delta+\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big)(x^*+x_0)\Big]\\
&= \big(\delta+\Delta(f_S,x_\delta^*)\big)(x^*-x_0)^2\\
&= \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta^*]\,(x^*(f_S)-x_0)^2
\\
&= \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta^*]\bigg(x^*(f_S)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^R[\alpha_1]}{2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg)^2
\\

\end{align*}

\begin{align*}

Integrating over $f_R$ gives:

\end{align*}
\end{proof}

Since $\alpha_1$ is uniform on $[\underline{\alpha}_1,\overline{\alpha}_1]$ with $\overline{\alpha}_1-\underline{\alpha}_1 \ge 2\overline{x}\,\overline{\gamma}$, and $f_R'(x_\delta) = \alpha_1-2\gamma x_\delta$, we have

0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
and in the two edge cases $f_R'(x_\delta) = \underline{\alpha}_1-2x_\delta t$ and $f_R'(x_\delta) = \overline{\alpha}_1-2x_\delta t$, respectively.

\begin{proof}
Given $f_S$ and the other parameters, we have:
\begin{align*}
\Var_{f_R|f_S}^R\big(f_R(x^*(f_S))\big)
&= \Var_{f_R|f_S}^R\big(f_S(x^*(f_S))+(\gamma-\delta)(\sigma_\delta^2-(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2)\big)
\\
&=\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2\big)^2\Var_\gamma^R(\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta),
\end{align*}
where we note that information about $\gamma$ depends on both $f_S$ as well as $x_\delta$.
Because the sender's model fixes curvature at a single value, $R$ remains uncertain about $\gamma$ after seeing $f_S$, and this variance is $>0$.
Hence, our problem is to choose $\sigma_\delta$ to minimize
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{f_S|\delta}^R\big[\big(\sigma_\delta^2-(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2\big)^2\Var_\gamma^R(\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta)\big].
\end{align*}
Since neither $x^*(f_S)$ nor $\Var_\gamma^R(\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta)$ depends on $\sigma_\delta$, this function is quadratic in $\sigma_\delta^2$ and minimized at
\begin{align*}
\sigma_\delta^2 = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{f_S|\delta}^R\big[(x^*(f_S)-x_\delta)^2\Var_\gamma^R(\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta)\big]}{\mathbb{E}_{f_S|\delta}^R\big[\Var_\gamma^R(\gamma|f_S,\theta=\delta,x_\delta)\big]}.
\end{align*}
The terms in the numerator represent the ex-ante expectation of the squared difference between $x_\delta$ and the eventual choice $x^*(f_S)$ multiplied by the variance of $\gamma$. The term in the denominator is the expected variance of $\gamma$.
Because there is uncertainty about $x^*(f_S)$, the numerator is strictly positive, and because $x^*(f_S),x_\delta\in[0,\overline{x}]$, this ratio is at most $\overline{x}^{\,2}<\overline{\sigma}^2$, which gives the result.
\end{proof}

By Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}, these senders sample so that $\sigma_\delta = \overline{\sigma}$ and $x_\delta^*\to 0$ as $\overline{\sigma}$ grows, so that $\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]\to\mathbb{E}_1^a[\beta_1^2]$ and
\begin{align*}
\xi(\delta,\pi) = 	c\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\frac{\overline{\sigma}^2}{(1+c\pi)^2}
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[\beta_1^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}\big).	
\end{align*}
The derivative of this expression with respect to $\delta$ is $< 0$ if and only if
\begin{align*}
\frac{\overline{\sigma}^2}{(1+c\pi)^2}
>
\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[\beta_1^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}
\bigg(1+\frac{2(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta)}{\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]}\bigg),
\end{align*}
which holds for $\overline{\sigma}$ sufficiently large.
Thus, $\xi(\delta,\pi)$ is positive and decreasing in $\delta$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and equals $0$ at $\delta=\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, so $\pi(\delta)$ is decreasing in $\delta$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.

Now, we consider $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
In this case, we have $\sigma_\delta = \underline{\sigma}\to 0$, and by Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}, $x_\delta^*\in\{0,\overline{x}\}$: high-curvature senders sample at whichever edge of $X$ they expect $f_S$ to be steepest, i.e. $x_\delta^* = \arg\max_{x \in \{0,\overline{x}\}} \mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x)^2]$.
In this case, we have
\begin{align*}
\xi(\delta,\pi) \approx 	c\big(\delta-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\big)
\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta^*)^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}.
\end{align*}
By the Envelope Theorem, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta^*)^2] = -4x_\delta^*\,\mathbb{E}_1^a[\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta^*]$, which is zero at $x_\delta^*=0$ and nonnegative at $x_\delta^*=\overline{x}$, since $\overline{x}$ is chosen over $0$ only if $\mathbb{E}_1^a[\beta_1]\le\delta\overline{x}$.
Hence, the derivative of $\xi$ with respect to $\delta$ has the sign of at least
\begin{align*}
\big(\delta+c\pi\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\big)-2\big(\delta-\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]\big)
= (2+c\pi)\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta,
\end{align*}
which is positive for all $\delta<\overline{\gamma}$, since symmetry of $p_\gamma^R$ gives $\overline{\gamma}=2\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
Thus, $\xi(\delta,\pi)$ is positive and increasing in $\delta$ for $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, so $\pi(\delta)$ is increasing in $\delta$ for $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
In sum, the marginal gain $\xi(\delta,\pi)$ from a type $\delta$ sender is decreasing to $0$ at $\delta=\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ and increasing thereafter.

Thus, whenever $\pi(\delta)>0$, it is strictly decreasing for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ and strictly increasing for $\delta >  \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
To see that $\pi(\delta) = 0$ on an interior interval, we simply note that for every $\lambda > 0$, there exists an interval of $\delta$ values containing $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ such that
\begin{align*}
c\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{(1+c\pi(\delta))^2}
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi(\delta)\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}\big)
< \lambda
\end{align*}
at $\pi = 0$ (and, by concavity, for all $\pi \geq 0$).
\end{proof}



\polarizationlimit*
\begin{proof}
We recall our initial first-order condition implicitly defining $\pi(\delta)$
\begin{align*}
c\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{(1+c\pi(\delta))^2}
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi(\delta) \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}\big) = \lambda	,
\end{align*}
where the left-hand side is decreasing in $\pi$, decreasing in $\delta$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$, and increasing in $\delta$ for $\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
We also recall that $c := \frac{1-p_a}{p_a}$, and that as $c \to 0$, $p_a \to 1$, and that as $c \to \infty$, $p_a \to 0$.

Recall also that $\overline{\delta}_0$ and $\underline{\delta}_0$ are the points at which $\pi(\delta)=0$ and are, in turn, functions of $c$.
By the constraint that $t(\delta)$ is a density and by writing $\pi(\delta,c)$ as a function of $c$, we have
\begin{align*}
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
t(\delta)d\delta
=\int_0^{\underline{\delta}_0(c)}
\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta	
+\int_{\overline{\delta}_0(c)}^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta	
= 1
\\
\pi(\overline{\delta}_0(c),c)p_\gamma^R(\overline{\delta}_0(c))\overline{\delta}_0'(c)
-
\pi(\underline{\delta}_0(c),c)p_\gamma^R(\underline{\delta}_0(c))\underline{\delta}_0'(c)
+
\int_0^{\underline{\delta}_0(c)}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)
+
\int_{\overline{\delta}_0(c)}^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)
\\
=
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\pi_c(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta= 0,
\end{align*}
where we use the fact that $\pi(\overline{\delta}_0(c),c) = \pi(\underline{\delta}_0(c),c) = 0$.

Next, note that, fixing $\delta$, the first-order condition can be rearranged as
\begin{align*}
\Xi(c\pi,\delta)
:= 
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\frac{\sigma_\delta^2}{(1+c\pi)^2}
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta + c\pi \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma])^2}\big)
= \frac{\lambda(c)}{c}
\end{align*}
The FOC then gives
\begin{align*}
\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi_c +\pi)
= \frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}
\implies
c\pi_c = \frac{\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}}-\pi
\end{align*}
But then, using the boundary condition, we have
\begin{align*}
c\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} \pi_c(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta
=
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} \frac{\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}}p_\gamma^R(\delta)-\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta=0
\\
\implies \bigg(\lambda'(c)-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c}\bigg)\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi,\delta)}p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta= c
\\
\implies
\frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2} = \frac{1}{\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{\Xi_{(c\pi)}(c\pi,\delta)}p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta} < 0
\end{align*}
since $\Xi_{(c\pi)} < 0$ (i.e. $\Xi$ is decreasing in $c\pi$, from the proof of the previous Theorem).

Now, consider $\Xi(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))$, which implicitly defines $\underline{\delta}_0(c)$ as
\begin{align*}
\Xi(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))
:= 
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\underline{\delta}_0(c)\big)
\big(\sigma_\delta^2
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\underline{\delta}_0(c) x_\delta)^2]}{4(\underline{\delta}_0(c) )^2}\big)
= \frac{\lambda(c)}{c}.	
\end{align*}
In this case, we have
\begin{align*}
\Xi_{\delta}(0,\underline{\delta}_0(c))\underline{\delta}_0'(c) = \frac{\lambda'(c)}{c}-\frac{\lambda(c)}{c^2}
< 0.
\end{align*}
But recall from the proof of the previous result that $\Xi_\delta < 0$ for $\delta < \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
This means that $\underline{\delta}_0(c)$ is increasing in $c$, i.e. is increasing in $\frac{1-p_a}{p_a}$. Hence, as $p_a$ increases, $\underline{\delta}_0$ decreases.
An analogous proof shows that as $p_a$ increases, $\overline{\delta}_0$ increases.
That is, the ``polarization'' induced by $\pi$ is increasing in $p_a$.

Finally, we investigate the limiting cases as $p_a \to 0$ ($c \to \infty$) and $p_a \to 1$ ($c \to 0$).
We begin with $c \to \infty$, and claim that $\underline{\delta}_0 \to \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
First, by our work above $\frac{\lambda(c)}{c}$ is decreasing in $c$, meaning it converges to a limit.
We claim $\lim_{c \to \infty} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c} = 0$.
Suppose by contradiction that this did not hold, and instead it converged to some $k > 0$.
This would mean that there exists $\overline{c\pi}>0$ such that $c\pi(\delta)\leq\overline{c\pi}$ for all $c$ and $\delta \in [0,\overline{\gamma}]-\lim_{c \to \infty}[\underline{\delta}_0(c),\overline{\delta}_0(c)]$.
But then, for all $c$ we would have
\begin{align*}
\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} 	\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta \leq \int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}\overline{c\pi}p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta
= \frac{\overline{c\pi}}{c},
\end{align*}
which approaches $0$ as $c \to \infty$, contradicting that $\int_0^{\overline{\gamma}} 	\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta=1$.
Hence, we have that $\lim_{c \to \infty} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c} = 0$, meaning $\lim_{c \to \infty}\underline{\delta}_0(c) = \lim_{c \to \infty}\overline{\delta}_0(c) = \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$.
Moreover, we note that even for $c$ arbitrarily large, $\pi(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]) = 0$, with $\pi'(\delta) < 0$ below $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ and $\pi'(\delta)>0$ above $\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]$ since $\Xi(c\pi,\delta)$ is decreasing in $\delta$ and increasing in $\delta$ on each range, respectively. 


Finally, we investigate the case where $c \to 0$.
We claim that as $c\to 0$, $\underline{\delta}_0 \to 0$ and $\overline{\delta}_0 \to \overline{\gamma}$ (and hence $t$ converges to a discrete distribution that either concentrates mass on $0$ or $\overline{\gamma}$ or both).
In particular, note that for all $c$, we have
\begin{align*}
c = \int_0^{\overline{\gamma}}c\pi(\delta,c)p_\gamma^R(\delta)d\delta,	
\end{align*}
which means that as $c \to 0$, we necessarily must have $c\pi(\delta,c) \to 0$ as well. This can happen only if 
\begin{align*}
\big(\mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]-\delta\big)
\big(\sigma_\delta^2
-\frac{\mathbb{E}_1^a[(\beta_1-2\delta x_\delta)^2]}{4(\delta)^2}\big)
\leq \lim_{c\to 0} \frac{\lambda(c)}{c},
\end{align*}
i.e. either the multiplier constraint must hold with equality or fail to hold (generating a corner solution at $\pi(\delta,c)= 0$).
Since this must hold pointwise for each $\delta$ for $c$ sufficiently small, we thus have that $\underline{\delta}_0 \to 0$ and $\overline{\delta}_0 \to \overline{\gamma}$, giving the result.
\end{proof}

\section{Extensions}\label{appendix:extensions}
This appendix relaxes two assumptions from the main text.
Sections \ref{section:intervals} allow sender types to be an \emph{interval}.
Appendix \ref{section:general} allows tradeoffs to be non-quadratic.

\subsection{Interval Type Spaces}\label{section:intervals}
In the main text, a type $\delta$ sender's model fixes curvature at a single value $\delta$.
However, in reality, senders and receivers alike may consider multiple tradeoffs in different ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item Senders could estimate \emph{some} tradeoffs and assume the worst-case or neglect others. In the example, the consultant may thoroughly estimate how carbon taxes affect energy prices, but assume a worst-case scenario for their effects on political backlash. 
\item Senders could \emph{calibrate} the value of a tradeoff at a fixed parameter, without estimating it on the real data. For example, the consultant may calibrate the effect of carbon taxes on energy prices using an elasticity from the literature, rather than organically estimating it on data.
\end{itemize}
Accordingly, I now assume that a sender's type $\theta$ is a \emph{subset} of $\Gamma$, with the following structure.

For each $\theta \in \Theta$, $\theta \subseteq \Gamma$ is a closed, nondegenerate interval. 
For each $\theta \neq \theta' \in \Theta$, $\theta \cap \theta' = \emptyset$.
In turn, I now assume a type $\theta$ sender provides models in the set
\begin{align*}
F_\theta := \{\beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2 : (\beta_0,\beta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \delta \in \theta\}.
\end{align*}
Thus, each simple type corresponds to an interval within $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$. 
I initially assume type intervals do not intersect for expositional purposes.\footnote{The case where they do is of particular interest, and I study it in Appendix \ref{section:overlap}.}
We will denote $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$.
Accordingly, type spaces have the following interpretations.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\overline{\delta}_\theta < \overline{\gamma}$, then a type $\theta$ $S$ is \emph{neglecting at least some tradeoffs}. Intuitively, these senders do not allow for the case where $\gamma$ is close to $\overline{\gamma}$.
\item If $\underline{\delta}_\theta > 0$, then a type $\theta$ $S$ is \emph{exaggerating at least some tradeoffs}. These senders do not allow for the case where $\gamma$ is close to $0$.
\end{itemize}

In addition to priors over $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, a type $\theta$ sender now has a prior over $\delta$ given by $\delta \sim P_\delta^\theta$ with support on $\theta \subseteq \Gamma$.
%I assume that if $\theta \neq \{0\}$ and $\theta \neq \{\overline{\gamma}\}$, then $P_\delta^\theta(\partial \theta \cap (0,\overline{\gamma}))>0$.
%This assumption is a regularity condition; it is consistent with either $P_\delta$ being a discrete distribution, or $P_\delta$ being a continuous CDF over $[0,\overline{\gamma}]$ whose mass is censored.
%At a mathematical level, a type $\theta$ $S$'s sampling decision will be determined by how he considers tradeoffs on the ``edge'' of the interval $\theta$.
%This ``edge'' must occur with non-measure-zero probability to have an effect on $S$'s ex-ante expected utility.
We denote by $P_{f_S}^\theta$ a type $\theta$ sender's prior over models $f_S$.

\paragraph{Learning with Interval Types}
The characterization of $\phi(f_S;\mu_\theta)$ --- the set of models $R$ considers in addition to $f_S$ --- is the same as above.
However, we note that if $\theta$ is non-degenerate, we may also have a moment condition corresponding to $\delta$:
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\theta}\big[x^2\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta x^2 \big)\big]
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{align}
This equation may be zero or nonzero, depending on $S$'s type.
With interval types, $\Delta(f_s,x_\delta)$ is defined as in the main text.
In particular, if $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, we necessarily have that (\ref{eq:gamma}) is equal to $0$, and that $f_S$ is the true model.
In this case, $\Delta(f_S,x_\delta)=0$.
However, if $\delta \in \partial \theta$ (the boundary of $\theta$), then the receiver may entertain additional models.

The statement of Proposition \ref{prop:reduction} is identical in the case with interval types, and the expression for $R$'s expected utility in Section \ref{section:learning} continues to hold.
This generalizes the intuitions in the singleton type model.
If $\delta = \overline{\delta}_\theta<\overline{\gamma}$, then $\Delta(f_s,x_\delta)>0$: $R$ considers models that \emph{only} have higher curvature than the one $S$ has provided because he has neglected some tradeoffs.
If $\delta = \underline{\delta}_\theta>0$, then $\Delta(f_s,x_\delta)<0$: $R$ considers models that \emph{only} have lower curvature than the one $S$ has provided because he has exaggerated some tradeoffs.

\paragraph{Strategic Sampling with Interval Types}
Let $x_\theta^*$ denote the mean of the underlying data that a type $\theta$ sender samples and $\sigma_\theta^{*2}$ its variance.
\begin{proposition}
Suppose $S$ is a type $\theta$ simple sender.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[\Delta(f_S,x_\theta^*)]>0$, he collects \emph{dispersed} data about extreme choices. $\mu_\theta^*$ is a bimodal distribution that places mass $\frac{1}{2}$ on each of $x_\theta^*-\overline{\sigma}$ and $x_\theta^*+\overline{\sigma}$. 
\item If $\mathbb{E}_{f_S}^\theta[\Delta(f_S,x_\theta^*)]<0$, $\mu_\theta^*$ is a distribution with variance $\underline{\sigma}^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\label{prop:samplingintervals}
\end{proposition}
The proposition characterizes the spread of the data underlying a simple sender's models and the logic and proof are identical to the singleton case.

The multidimensional results of Section \ref{section:learning} extend in the same way when each type $\theta$ is a closed and convex subset of $\Gamma$, distinct types are disjoint, a type $\theta$ sender has a prior $P_\delta^\theta$ over $\delta \in \theta$, and, if $\gamma$ is not an extreme point of $\Gamma$, $P_\delta^\theta(\partial \theta \cap \Gamma^\circ) > 0$.

\subsection{Designing Interval Type Spaces}\label{section:designintervals}
In the design problem of Section \ref{section:simple}, suppose that $a$ can choose any type space $\Theta \subseteq \mathcal{P}([0,\overline{\gamma}])$ such that for all $\theta \in \Theta$, $\theta$ is a closed interval.
\begin{proposition}
The optimal type space contains no non-degenerate intervals, and is the collection of singletons on $[0,\overline{\gamma}]=\Gamma$: $\Theta = \big\{\{\delta\} : \delta \in \Gamma\big\}$.
\label{prop:optimaltypeintervals}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose by contradiction that there existed a nondegenerate interval of type $\theta = [\underline{\delta}_\theta,\overline{\delta}_\theta]$.
Suppose $\theta^\circ \neq \emptyset$ and consider $\delta \in \theta^\circ$.
Conditional on seeing this model, the receiver believes the model $f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$ is the true model.

Now, for each $\delta \in \theta^\circ$, as before, $a$ can create a type $\{\delta\}$.
This type strategically samples by setting $\sigma$ such that $\max_x f_S(x) \leq \max_x f_S(x) + (\mathbb{E}[\gamma|\delta])(\sigma^2-(x-x_\theta)^2)$.
Thus, conditional on seeing a model of the form $\beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta x^2$, $R$ believes that $f_S(x)$ is either the true model or the model from the $\{\delta\}$ type.
Let $q(\delta|\beta_0,\beta_1,T)$ be the conditional probability of the type $\{\delta\}$ sender relative to the original type $\theta$ sender.
Let $\overline{\gamma}_{\{\delta\}}$ be the receiver's expectation of $\gamma$ if $\delta \geq \gamma$ given $f_S$ and $\underline{\gamma}_{\{\delta\}}$ likewise if $\delta < \gamma$.
Then, this generates a difference on the order of
\begin{align*}
\int_{\underline{\delta}_\theta}^{\overline{\delta}_\theta}
\max_x f_S(x) + q(\delta|\beta_0,\beta_1,T)\big(\overline{\gamma}_{\{\delta\}}-\delta\big)(\overline{\sigma}^2-(x-x_\theta)^2)-
\max_x f_S(x)
dP_\delta^a(\delta \leq \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]| \delta \in \theta^\circ)
\\
+
\int_{\underline{\delta}_\theta}^{\overline{\delta}_\theta}
\max_x f_S(x) + q(\delta|\beta_0,\beta_1,T)\big(\underline{\gamma}_{\{\delta\}}-\delta\big)(\underline{\sigma}^2-(x-x_\theta)^2)-
\max_x f_S(x)
dP_\delta^a(\delta > \mathbb{E}_\gamma^R[\gamma]| \delta \in \theta^\circ)
\end{align*}
The first line considers any simple senders who sample dispersed data.
The second line considers any simple senders who sample local data.
In both cases, the gain is positive and is increasing in $q(\delta|\beta_0,\beta_1,T)$; i.e., the gain is higher the more likely $R$ is to think that $f_S(x)$ came from type $\{\delta\}$.

We are left to consider the boundary types of $\theta$: $\overline{\delta}_\theta$ and $\underline{\delta}_\theta$.
These may generate higher conditional expected utility than a singleton type, but because $p_\gamma^R$ is continuous, they occur with measure $0$.
By contrast, replacing the non-degenerate interval with a continuum of types generates a non-measure-zero positive gain.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Non-Quadratic Tradeoffs}\label{section:general}
While the main model utilizes quadratic utility to express tradeoffs, in this subsection, I probe the robustness of many of the main results to \emph{non-quadratic} utility.
In particular, I show an analogue of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}: if $\overline{\sigma}$ is sufficiently large and $S$ is low-curvature, sampling dispersed data dominates sampling local data.
If $S$ is high-curvature, sampling local data dominates dispersed data. 

\paragraph{General Loss Function}
The following two assumptions consider two generalizations of functional forms for the receiver.
\begin{assumption}
The receiver's models and a type $\theta$ sender's models are given by:
\begin{align}
&f_R(x) = \alpha_0+\alpha_1 x-\gamma \ell(x-x_\theta) 
\label{eq:generalfunctionrec}	
\\
&f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta \ell(x-x_\theta)
\label{eq:generalfunctionsend}	
\end{align}
where $\ell: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^+$ is strictly convex and differentiable. Moreover, $\ell(0) = 0$, $\ell'(0) = 0$, and $\ell$ is symmetric around $x=0$.
\label{assfunction1}
\end{assumption}
One way to interpret this functional form is to think of a sender's or receiver's model as a \emph{Taylor expansion} around a point $x_\theta$.
This Taylor expansion includes a constant component, a linear term, and a concave $\mathcal{O}(2)$ term centered at $x_\theta$.
While the convex loss $\ell(x-x_\theta)$ is symmetric around $x_\theta$, the assumption does allow for $f_R$ or $f_S$ themselves to be asymmetric.
This assumption subsumes the case where $\ell(x) = x^2$.

The case where this convex loss itself may be locally asymmetric is also of interest.
Thus, I also consider the following alternative assumption.
\begin{assumption}
The receiver's models and a type $\theta$ sender's models are given by:
\begin{align}
&f_R(x) = \alpha_0+\alpha_1 x-\gamma m(x)
\label{eq:generalfunctionrec2}	
\\
&f_S(x) = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta m(x)
\label{eq:generalfunctionsend2}	
\end{align}
where $m: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^+$ is strictly convex and differentiable, and $m'(x)$ is onto $\mathbb{R}$. Moreover, there exists $x$ such that $m(x) = m'(x) = 0$.
\label{assfunction2}	
\end{assumption}
This formulation of models allows for a convex loss function $m$ to take many different shapes.
With these two assumptions in hand, I prove a more general version of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{samplinggeneral}
Suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction1} holds.
If a type $\delta$ simple sender is low-curvature, he collects dispersed data with $\sigma_\delta^{*2}=\overline{\sigma}_\delta^2$.
If a type $\delta$ simple sender is high-curvature, he collects local data with $\sigma_\delta^{*2}=\underline{\sigma}_\delta^2$.
\\
Alternatively, suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction2} holds and $\overline{\sigma}$ is sufficiently large.
If a type $\delta$ simple sender is low-curvature, he prefers to collect dispersed data with $\sigma_\delta^{2}=\overline{\sigma}_\delta^2$ to local data with $\sigma_\delta^{2}=\underline{\sigma}_\delta^2$.
If a type $\delta$ simple sender is high-curvature, he prefers to collect local data with $\sigma_\delta^{2}=\underline{\sigma}_\delta^2$ to dispersed data with $\sigma_\delta^{2}=\overline{\sigma}_\delta^2$.
\label{thm:samplinggeneral}
\end{restatable}

First, we note that the first part of Theorem \ref{thm:samplinggeneral} is identical to Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}.
The key reason is that the set of plausible $f_R$s must obey the following moment conditions.
\begin{align}
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x + \delta \ell(x-x_\delta)\big] =0
\label{eq:interceptgeneral}
\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-\beta_0-\beta_1 x+\delta \ell(x-x_\delta) \big)\big] =0 \label{eq:slopegeneral}
\end{align}
The proof shows that (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) can be boiled down to the condition that the derivative of all plausible $f_R$s must be the same across the data sample $\mu_\delta$.
Since $\mu_\delta$ and $\ell(x-x_\delta)$ are both symmetric around $x_\delta$, we have that for $\mu_\delta '$ with $x_\delta = x_\delta'$ but $\sigma_\delta' \neq\sigma_\delta$
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R'(x)]
= \alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[\ell'(x-x_\delta)]
=\alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta'}[\ell'(x-x_\delta)] 
\qquad \forall \sigma,\sigma' \in [\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]
\end{align*}
Thus, equation (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) is invariant to $\sigma$.
This means that, as in the quadratic case, as $\sigma$ changes, the set of models that $R$ considers as a function of $\sigma$ shifts up or down, allowing us to back out the same logic as in the original case.

However, if models are not characterized by a symmetric loss $\ell(x-x_\delta)$ around $x_\delta$, it is more difficult to obtain a result.
Theorem \ref{thm:samplinggeneral} simply states that, fixing $x_\delta$, $S$ prefers sampling dispersed data to local data if he is low-curvature (and the opposite holds if he is high-curvature).
The reason is that, if the convex portion of a receiver's models is \emph{not} symmetric around $x_\delta$, the analogue of (\ref{eq:slopegeneral}) no longer holds.
I.e., the expression
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R'(x)]
=
\alpha_1-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[m(x)]
\end{align*}
generally changes as the variance of a distribution $\mu_\delta$ increases, fixing its mean.
As a result, the proof allows $\alpha_1$ to change to ``absorb'' any changes to $\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[m(x)]$ with respect to $\sigma$.
For each $f_R(x)$ with a given $\gamma$ value, changing $\sigma$ thus not only changes $\alpha_0$ (as in the benchmark and symmetric loss models) but also $\alpha_1$.
It is here where we utilize the condition in Assumption \ref{assfunction2} that assigns the same prior mass to different $\alpha_1$ values in the receiver's prior, which ensures that these models receive the same posterior weight.

Consequently, the value of the receiver's objective at her optimal choice $x^*$ does not have an intuitive expression, and depends on nonintuitive features of the third derivative of $m(\cdot)$.
However, the proof shows that if the sender is low-curvature, the value of each $f_R(x)$ the receiver entertains at $x_\delta$ is \emph{increasing} in $\sigma$.
Hence, the value of each $f_R$ at $x_\delta$ for sufficiently high $\overline{\sigma}$ must necessarily be higher than the receiver's optimum $x^*$ at $\underline{\sigma}$.
The intuition for a high-curvature sender, as before, is flipped.



\paragraph{Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:samplinggeneral}}
We prove this result via the following lemmata. The first part of the theorem (assuming Assumption \ref{assfunction1}) follows immediately from the first lemma after applying the same reasoning as in Theorem \ref{thm:sampling}. 

%LEMMATA HERE

\begin{lemma}
Suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction1} holds and fix $f_S$.
Then, for all $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\delta,\mu_\delta)$, there exists $L(\sigma)$ strictly increasing in $\sigma$ with $L(0) = 0$ such that:
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = f_S(x)+(\gamma-\delta)\big[L(\sigma)-\ell(x-x_\delta)\big].
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $f_R \in \phi(f_S;\delta,\mu_\delta)$ for $f_S = \beta_0+\beta_1x-\delta\ell(x-x_\delta)$.
This means that we have
\begin{align*}
f_R(x) = \tilde{\alpha}_0 + \tilde{\alpha}_1 x-\gamma\ell(x-x_\delta).
\end{align*}
Applying Stein's Lemma as above, the moment condition with respect to $\tilde{\alpha}_1$ can be written as
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}\big[x\big(f_R(x)-f_S(x) \big)\big]
&= \Cov_{\mu_\delta}(x,f_R(x)-f_S(x))
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R'(x)-f_S'(x)]
\\
&=
\sigma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[\tilde{\alpha}_1-\gamma\ell'(x-x_\delta)-\beta_1+\delta\ell'(x-x_\delta)]
= 0.
\end{align*}
Because $\ell(x-x_\delta)$ is symmetric around $x_\delta$, the terms with $\ell'$ are equal to $0$.
Thus, we have $\tilde{\alpha}_1 = \beta_1$ for all $\sigma$ and are left to solve for $\tilde{\alpha}_0$.
But for each $\gamma$ value, as above, we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R(x)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_S(x)]
\implies
\tilde{\alpha}_0 - \gamma\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[\ell(x-x_\delta)]
= \beta_0 - \delta\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[\ell(x-x_\delta)]
\\
\implies
\tilde{\alpha}_0 = \beta_0 + (\gamma-\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[\ell(x-x_\delta)].
\end{align*}
Defining $L(\sigma) = \mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[\ell(x-x_\delta)]$, we note that because $\ell(x-x_\delta)$ is convex, $L(\sigma)$ is increasing in $\sigma$. As $\sigma \to 0$, $L(\sigma) \to 0$.
This gives the result.	
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
Suppose Assumption \ref{assfunction2} holds and fix $f_S$.
Then, for each $f_R \in \phi(f_S; \delta,\mu_\delta)$, $f_R(x_\delta)$ is increasing (decreasing) in $\sigma$ if $\gamma>\delta$ ($\gamma<\delta$).
\end{lemma}	
\begin{proof}
As above, we write $
f_R(x) = \tilde{\alpha}_0 + \tilde{\alpha}_1 x-\gamma m(x)$.
Applying Stein's Lemma again to the slope moment condition, we have $\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_R'(x)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[f_S'(x)]$.
Using this condition, we implicitly define $\alpha_1(\sigma)$ as
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1(\sigma) = \beta_1 +(\gamma-\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta}[m'(x)] 		
\end{align*}
and note that its sign with respect to $\sigma$ is ambiguous.
Similarly, we define $\alpha_0(\sigma)$ implicitly as
\begin{align*}
\alpha_0(\sigma)
= \beta_0 + \big(\beta_1- \alpha_1(\sigma)\big)x_\delta+(\gamma-\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[m(x)]
\end{align*}
and note in particular that, by definition, we have
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[f_S(x)]=
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[f_R(x)]
=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}\big[\alpha_0(\sigma)x +\alpha_1(\sigma)
-\gamma m(x)
\big]
\end{align*}
But then, by the Envelope Theorem, we have that
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}
f_R(x_\delta|\sigma)
&= \alpha_0'(\sigma)+\alpha_1'(\sigma)x_\delta
- \gamma m(x_\delta)
\\
&=	-\alpha_1'(\sigma)x_\delta+(\gamma-\delta)\frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[m(x)]+\alpha_1'(\sigma)x_\delta
\\
&= (\gamma-\delta)\frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[m(x)]
\end{align*}
Since $m$ is convex, this is weakly positive if and only if $\gamma \geq \delta$.
Moreover, as $\sigma \to \infty$, $\mathbb{E}_{\mu_\delta,\sigma}[m(x)]$ is bounded away from $0$, meaning that $f_R(x_\delta)$ approaches $\infty$ as $\sigma \to \infty$.

We note that because the receiver has a uniform prior over $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$, her posterior places the same mass on all models that are the same ``up to a constant and linear term.''
This means that for $\gamma > \delta$, there exists $\sigma$ such that $f_R(x_\delta|\sigma) > f_R(x^*|\underline{\sigma})$, where $x^*$ maximizes $f_R(x|\underline{\sigma})$.
Hence, $S$ is better sampling dispersed data than local data when $\gamma > \delta$.
The same logic shows that $S$ prefers local data when $\gamma < \delta$.
Applying the same logic as in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:sampling} gives the result.
\end{proof}
